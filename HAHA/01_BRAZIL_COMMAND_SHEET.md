# 1. BRAZIL IN ONE MINUTE

## Brazil's entire position

Memorize this first:

> **Brazil wants a legally binding international framework on LAWS. Brazil supports two things together: clear prohibitions for autonomous systems or uses that are unacceptable, and strict rules for other autonomous systems. Human judgement, human responsibility, IHL compliance, legal review and accountability must remain at the centre.**

That is the whole position.

Everything else in this file explains those sentences.

### The five words

# **PROHIBIT — REGULATE — CONTROL — REVIEW — ACCOUNT**

When you get a difficult POI and your brain goes blank, rebuild your answer around those five words.

# 2. WHAT BRAZIL WANTS

Brazil's position can be broken into **six goals**.

## 1. A legally binding international instrument

Brazil's stated long-term goal is a **legally binding instrument** dealing with the humanitarian and legal consequences of LAWS.

Brazil said this clearly in its 2024 CCW statement and again in its 2025 CCW statement. citeturn392657view1turn392657view0

### Simple meaning

Brazil does **not** want the final outcome to be only:

- a political declaration;
- a voluntary promise;
- a non-binding code;
- general principles with no real rules.

Those tools can still be useful as interim steps, but Brazil's intended destination is a binding international instrument.

### Committee line

> **"Brazil is willing to negotiate the path, but not lose sight of the destination: a meaningful legally binding international instrument."**

---

## 2. Prohibit the unacceptable

Brazil does not support the idea that every autonomous weapon must automatically be banned.

Instead, Brazil supports identifying autonomous systems or uses that cannot meet the required legal and control conditions.

Brazil's 2024 submission says an autonomous weapon would be incompatible with IHL if it cannot be controlled by humans or cannot be used consistently with IHL. It gives examples involving inability to direct force at a specific military objective, inability to limit effects as required by IHL, or inability to sufficiently understand, predict or explain relevant effects. citeturn415268search12

### Simple meaning

Some forms of autonomy are not just "risky".

They may be **unacceptable** because the State cannot make their use lawful or keep real human control over them.

---

## 3. Strictly regulate the rest

Brazil supports a **two-tier approach**.

### Tier 1

**Prohibit the unacceptable.**

### Tier 2

**Strictly regulate what can still be used.**

Brazil's 2025 CCW statement explicitly described the rolling text as following a widely supported two-tier approach: prohibitions for some systems and restrictions on the design and use of others. citeturn392657view0

### Very important

Do not say:

> "Brazil wants to ban all LAWS."

That is too broad.

Say:

> **"Brazil supports prohibitions where autonomy becomes incompatible with IHL or effective human control, and strict regulation for systems that remain permissible."**

---

# 3. HUMAN JUDGEMENT AND HUMAN CONTROL

This is probably the most important concept you need to understand.

## What Brazil means

Brazil's 2024 submission says meaningful human control means that the human operator keeps the **ultimate decision-making power over critical functions**, especially targeting decisions and the use of force. citeturn415268search12

Brazil also says the human operator should be able to:

- set environmental limits;
- set spatial limits;
- set time limits;
- approve the operational context with enough situational awareness;
- rely on sufficient reliability and predictability in target identification, selection and engagement;
- make sure mission parameters cannot change without human validation. citeturn415268search12

## In simple words

Brazil is **not** satisfied with:

> "There is a human somewhere in the chain of command."

Brazil wants:

> **"The human must actually have the information, authority, limits and ability to act that make their judgement real."**

---

# 4. THE HUMAN CONTROL TEST

Whenever you hear "human control", immediately ask these six questions:

### 1. Does the human understand the system?

Can the operator understand:

- what the system does;
- what it cannot do;
- where it may fail?

### 2. Does the human set the limits?

Can the human define:

- target type;
- location;
- time;
- mission;
- scale?

### 3. Does the human know the context?

Does the operator have enough information about the real situation to make a meaningful decision?

### 4. Can the human intervene?

Can the human:

- stop the action;
- change the mission;
- deactivate the system;
- otherwise intervene where technically and operationally feasible?

### 5. Can the system stay within the human's limits?

Does it remain inside the approved boundaries?

### 6. Can responsibility be traced afterwards?

Can the State show:

- who authorized the mission;
- what parameters were used;
- what system version was active;
- what happened?

### Your simple test

> **Understand → Limit → Decide → Monitor → Intervene → Account**

Memorize that.

---

# 5. WHAT BRAZIL DOES NOT MEAN BY HUMAN CONTROL

Do not reduce the concept to:

> "A human presses the button."

Also do not reduce it to:

> "A human is technically present."

Human control can be meaningless if:

- the operator does not understand the system;
- the time available is too short for real judgement;
- the system's limits are too broad;
- the operator cannot realistically intervene;
- the system changes critical mission parameters without validation;
- the interface hides uncertainty;
- the operator is trained to trust the machine automatically.

Brazil's language is therefore much closer to **effective human judgement** than to simple human presence.

# 6. DO NOT OVERCLAIM THE LEGAL STATUS OF "MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL"

This is an extremely important MUN distinction.

Do **not** say:

> "International law already universally defines meaningful human control as a treaty rule."

That is too strong.

Say:

> **"Brazil argues that meaningful human control is necessary to ensure compliance with IHL."**

The 2026 GGE work itself distinguishes the concept of context-appropriate human judgement and control from simply pretending the exact phrase is already a universally codified standalone legal rule. The 2026 rolling-text discussion also treats human judgement/control as part of the framework while States debated how it relates to existing legal obligations. citeturn606922search8

### Easy memory rule

**Brazil can strongly defend the substance without claiming universal agreement on the vocabulary.**

# 7. BRAZIL AND IHL

Brazil's approach is built around existing international humanitarian law.

Key ideas:

- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- avoiding indiscriminate effects;
- human responsibility and accountability.

Brazil's position is **not**:

> "IHL does not apply to autonomous weapons."

The 2025 UNGA resolution reaffirmed that international law, including the UN Charter, IHL and international human rights law, applies in relation to autonomous weapons systems. citeturn643638search26

Brazil's position is more precise:

> **IHL applies, but autonomy creates new practical problems that need specific rules and safeguards.**

### Strong committee line

> **"Brazil is not asking the international community to replace IHL. Brazil is asking us to make IHL workable in a world where systems can select and engage targets with increasing autonomy."**

---

# 8. THE FOUR BIG IHL QUESTIONS FOR LAWS

When you are attacked on legality, think:

## DISTINCTION

Can the system reliably distinguish relevant military targets from protected persons or objects?

## PROPORTIONALITY

Can expected civilian harm be assessed against the anticipated military advantage?

## PRECAUTIONS

Can the user take feasible precautions before and during the attack?

## ACCOUNTABILITY

Can responsibility still be traced to the people and institutions making the decision to use force?

Brazil's argument is that autonomy must not make these requirements impossible to meet.

---

# 9. BRAZIL'S PROHIBITION LOGIC

Brazil's 2024 response provides a strong base for the prohibition side.

An autonomous weapon can be incompatible with IHL where, for example:

- it cannot be directed at a specific military objective;
- its effects cannot be limited as IHL requires;
- its functioning cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or explained;
- human control is absent;
- use could undermine attribution of State or individual legal responsibility. citeturn415268search12

### The simple version

> **If a State cannot keep the use lawful, bounded and humanly accountable, autonomy becomes unacceptable.**

---

# 10. BRAZIL'S REGULATION LOGIC

For systems that are not prohibited, Brazil's 2024 position supports positive rules to make human control practical. citeturn415268search12

Think of:

- geographical limits;
- time limits;
- environmental limits;
- target restrictions;
- reliable target identification;
- predictable system behaviour;
- human validation of mission-parameter changes;
- ability to intervene or deactivate where required;
- proper precautions during use.

### Simple sentence

> **"If a system is permitted, it must be bounded."**

---

# 11. THE TWO-TIER MODEL IN ONE LINE

> **Tier 1: Do not allow what cannot be made lawful and meaningfully controlled.**
>
> **Tier 2: Strictly control what can still be used.**

This is one of your most important Brazil arguments.

# 12. ARTICLE 36

## What is Article 36?

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires States Parties to the Protocol to determine whether the study, development, acquisition or adoption of a new weapon, means or method of warfare would be prohibited in some or all circumstances by applicable international law.

## Why Brazil cares

Brazil supports legal review as an important safeguard for emerging weapons.

For autonomous systems, the problem is:

> **A system may change after the original review.**

For example:

- software changes;
- model changes;
- sensor changes;
- mission changes;
- operating environment changes.

### Brazil's useful position

> **"For adaptive autonomous systems, legal assurance cannot be treated as a one-time box-check."**

### Legal caution

Article 36's treaty obligation does **not** apply identically to every State.

So do not say:

> "Every country is already legally required under Article 36 to do this."

Say:

> **"Article 36 provides an important legal-review model, while a future framework can encourage equivalent safeguards more broadly."**

---

# 13. CONTINUING REVIEW

The review idea should be:

**Review once → deploy forever**

is bad.

Better:

**Review → test → deploy → monitor → re-review after material change**

That is especially important when machine-learning or other software changes can affect critical functions.

The 2026 GGE rolling text includes legal review, significant modifications affecting target identification/selection/engagement, context-appropriate human judgement and control across the lifecycle, testing/evaluation and training. citeturn606922search8

# 14. BRAZIL AND ADAPTIVE AI

This is where your answer can become much stronger than generic MUN answers.

Imagine:

> System approved on Day 1.

Then:

> Software updated on Day 60.

Then:

> Target classifier changed on Day 90.

Then:

> Sensor fusion changed on Day 120.

Question:

Brazil should argue that material changes affecting critical functions can require renewed legal and technical assurance.

### Memory line

> **"If the weapon changes in a way that changes what it can do, the assurance must change too."**

# 24. BRAZIL'S POSITION ON DIRECT VS INDIRECT CONTROL

This is an important negotiation area.

### Direct control

Human directly approves specific engagements.

### Indirect/contextual control

Human establishes strict conditions, constraints and supervision within a defined environment.

Brazil should protect the **substance** of human control while allowing context to matter.

### Good line

> **"Brazil cares about whether human judgement has real effect, not whether every operational situation can be forced into one identical control model."**

This is especially useful for defensive or time-critical systems.

---

# 25. HUMAN CONTROL IS NOT A HEADCOUNT

Bad test:

> "Is there a human?"

Better test:

> "Can that human actually understand, constrain, monitor and intervene?"

This distinction can win you technical POIs because it is harder to dismiss than the slogan "human in the loop."

---

# 26. BRAZIL'S KEY TECHNICAL IDEAS

Keep these in your vocabulary.

### Predictability

Can we reasonably understand how the system will behave in expected conditions?

### Reliability

Does it perform consistently enough for the intended use?

### Explainability

Can relevant system behaviour be understood enough for legal and operational assurance?

### Traceability

Can we reconstruct what happened afterwards?

### Operating limits

Where, when and against what can the system operate?

### Intervention

Can humans alter, stop or deactivate the system where feasible?

### Testing

Was the system tested in realistic and difficult conditions?

### Lifecycle assurance

Does the review continue after deployment?

If someone says:

> "AI can never be 100% predictable."

Answer:

> **"Brazil is not asking for mathematical certainty. We are asking for enough understanding and predictability to show that the system can be used lawfully in its expected conditions."**

That is much more defensible.

# 37. BRAZIL'S POSITION ON DEFINITIONS

Do not obsess over finding one magic sentence.

The framework should distinguish:

### Automation

System follows pre-set rules.

### Autonomy

System can perform relevant functions without further human intervention after activation.

### AI

Technology that can support prediction, classification, decision-making or other functions.

### LAWS

A policy/legal category concerning autonomous weapon functions that warrant international regulation.

### Golden line

> **"Regulate the function that creates the risk, not the label attached to the technology."**

# 46. CYBERSECURITY

For autonomous systems, cybersecurity is not separate from accountability.

A cyber compromise can affect:

- target data;
- sensors;
- communications;
- software;
- update mechanisms;
- operating limits.

Therefore testing should consider:

- corrupted data;
- spoofing;
- communications loss;
- malicious inputs;
- compromised updates.

You do not need to demand public disclosure of vulnerabilities.

# 47. COMMUNICATIONS LOSS

This is a classic POI.

### Question

> "What happens if the operator loses communication with the system?"

Brazil's answer:

> **"The loss of communication should not silently expand the system's authority. The system should follow a predefined, tested response appropriate to its context."**

That might involve:

- stopping;
- returning;
- safe loiter;
- handover;
- another defined fail-safe state.

The exact response depends on the system.

# 48. AUTOMATION BIAS

Automation bias means humans can start trusting machine outputs too much.

Even if the human is technically in charge, the human may:

- accept recommendations automatically;
- ignore warnings;
- be unable to challenge the machine under time pressure.

Therefore meaningful control also requires:

- training;
- realistic testing;
- interface design;
- awareness of system limitations;
- ability to override where appropriate.

---

# 49. TESTING

Testing should not mean:

> "We ran a demonstration and it worked."

For autonomous systems, useful testing can include:

- expected conditions;
- unusual conditions;
- degraded sensors;
- communications loss;
- cyber interference;
- unexpected civilian presence;
- changes in the environment;
- interaction with other systems.

The 2026 GGE text specifically discusses testing/evaluation, realistic simulations, training and instructions alongside legal review and lifecycle human judgement/control. citeturn606922search8

# 54. SWARMS AND SYSTEM INTERACTION

A system can look safe on its own but behave differently when many systems interact.

So the framework can consider:

- number of systems operating together;
- shared geographic boundaries;
- aggregate engagement levels;
- communications;
- deconfliction;
- collective behaviour.
