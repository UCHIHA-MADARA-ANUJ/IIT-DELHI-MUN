> Committee: UNGA-DISEC  
> Agenda: Establishing an International Regulatory Framework for the Development and Deployment of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)  
> Country: Brazil  
> Conference: 26–27 September 2026  
> Status: Working command sheet — September 2026

---

# 1. BRAZIL IN 60 SECONDS

### The one-line position

**Brazil supports negotiating a legally binding international instrument on autonomous weapons, built around clear prohibitions for unacceptable systems/uses and strict regulations for other autonomous systems, with human judgement/control, accountability and IHL compliance at the centre.**

Brazil's own 2024 CCW submission and later national statements support prohibitions and regulations and describe a legally binding instrument as the desired outcome. In November 2025, Brazil again stated that it remained committed to a legally binding instrument addressing the humanitarian consequences of LAWS. [Sources: CCW/MSP/2024/WP.37; CCW/MSP/2025/WP.11]

### The five words to remember

**PROHIBIT — REGULATE — CONTROL — REVIEW — ACCOUNT**

If you forget everything else in committee, rebuild your answer around these.

---

# 2. WHAT BRAZIL ACTUALLY WANTS

## A. International legal instrument

Brazil's stated objective is a **legally binding instrument** addressing the consequences of autonomous weapons for international humanitarian law.

Brazil has repeatedly supported the CCW GGE process and the development of elements that can move toward negotiations.

The 2025 Brazil working paper said the CCW is a key forum and that Brazil remained committed to a legally binding instrument. The 2026 GGE process continued to develop elements of a possible instrument through the rolling text.

### MUN wording

> "Brazil believes the purpose of this process should ultimately be an effective, legally binding international instrument."

Do **not** say:
> "The world has already agreed to a treaty."

It has not.

---

## B. Prohibitions + regulations

Brazil does not treat the debate as simply "ban everything" versus "allow everything."

Its 2024 response supports:
- prohibiting systems that cannot be used consistently with IHL and meaningful human control;
- regulating other autonomous systems through positive obligations and safeguards.

### The basic model

**Tier 1 — Prohibit the unacceptable**

Examples from Brazil's 2024 response include systems/use cases where:
- meaningful human control cannot be maintained;
- the system cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or explained;
- effects cannot be limited as required by IHL;
- the system cannot be directed toward a specific military objective;
- the use could create attribution/accountability problems.

**Tier 2 — Strictly regulate the rest**

Brazil has identified measures such as:
- spatial limits;
- temporal limits;
- sufficient situational awareness;
- reliable/predictable target identification and engagement;
- human validation of mission-parameter changes;
- ability to intervene/deactivate;
- precautions during operations.

Source: Brazil's response to the 2024 CCW GGE Chair's guiding questions.

---

# 3. BRAZIL'S HUMAN CONTROL POSITION

## What Brazil means by "meaningful human control"

Brazil's 2024 answer says meaningful human control means the human operator retains **ultimate decision-making power over critical functions**, especially targeting decisions and use of force.

Brazil also links control with the operator's ability to:
- impose environmental, spatial and temporal limits;
- approve the operational context with sufficient situational awareness;
- rely on sufficient reliability and predictability in identification, selection and engagement;
- prevent autonomous changes to mission parameters without human validation.

### The important distinction

Brazil is **not** saying:

> "A human somewhere in the chain is enough."

Brazil's position is about whether human authority is actually meaningful in the system's real operating conditions.

### Good committee line

> "For Brazil, human control is not about having a human somewhere in the chain of command; it is about whether that human can understand the system's operation, set its limits, make the necessary judgement and intervene when required."

### Important wording caution

Do **not** say:

> "International law already universally defines meaningful human control as a legal requirement."

That overstates the current legal consensus.

Say:

> "Brazil argues that meaningful human control is necessary to ensure compliance with IHL."

That is much safer and accurately reflects Brazil's position.

---

# 4. BRAZIL AND IHL

Brazil's approach is grounded in existing IHL principles, especially:
- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- prevention of indiscriminate effects;
- continuing human responsibility.

Brazil's 2024 response says the legality of an autonomous weapon cannot be assessed only from inherent characteristics; the way the system functions and is used in a specific context matters.

### The argument

Brazil is **not** saying:

> "IHL does not apply to LAWS."

Brazil's argument is closer to:

> "IHL applies, but autonomy creates specific challenges that require additional rules and safeguards."

This distinction matters.

---

# 5. ARTICLE 36 — BRAZIL'S POSITION

Brazil supports legal review of new weapons and has linked Article 36 review with LAWS governance.

The key Brazilian position is that review should not end simply because a weapon passed an initial legal test.

For autonomous/AI-enabled systems, changes in:
- software,
- models,
- data,
- functions,
- operating environments,
- targeting behaviour

can affect the system's characteristics.

### MUN formulation

> "Article 36 should remain a central national safeguard, but for adaptive autonomous systems, legal assurance must be capable of responding to material changes in system function."

### Don't overclaim

Do not say:
> "Brazil has proposed a globally mandatory international Article 36 certification system."

Brazil's position supports legal review and additional regulation, but the exact international implementation model is still a negotiation question.

---

# 6. BRAZIL'S TECHNICAL SIDE

Brazil has not approached this issue only as an ethical question.

It has submitted technical/legal proposals concerning autonomy, control, predictability and verification.

Brazil also referenced **IEEE 7007-2021** in its 2024 work.

### Critical wording

Say:

> "Brazil has proposed IEEE 7007-2021 as a possible technical reference."

Do NOT say:

> "IEEE 7007 is the international LAWS verification standard."

It is not a universally adopted LAWS verification standard.

# 9. THE 42-STATE ROLLING-TEXT MOMENT

In 2025, Brazil was associated with a cross-regional 42-state statement supporting the CCW rolling text as a sufficient basis for moving toward negotiations.

In March 2026, Brazil told the GGE that the December 2025 version of the rolling text continued to be a sufficient basis for negotiations.

### Why this matters for Brazil

It gives Brazil a diplomatic argument that it is not operating alone.

### What NOT to say

Do not say:

> "42 countries support Brazil's exact treaty."

Say:

> "42 States supported using the rolling text as a basis for moving toward negotiations."

That distinction is important.

# 10. CURRENT 2026 PROCESS

The 2026 CCW GGE met:
- **2–6 March 2026**
- **31 August–4 September 2026**

The GGE continued working on elements of a possible instrument through a rolling text.

The mandate concerns formulating elements **by consensus without prejudging the legal nature of the instrument**.

### Next major milestone

The CCW Seventh Review Conference is scheduled for:

**16–20 November 2026**

Brazil's MUN occurs between the September GGE session and that Review Conference.

### Committee opportunity

Brazil can therefore argue for:
- preserving the rolling-text work;
- moving from elements toward negotiations;
- keeping the process practical;
- using the Review Conference as the next institutional milestone.

Do NOT say a binding treaty has already been negotiated.

# 11. BRAZIL'S 2026 DIPLOMATIC POSITIONING

A useful current distinction:

### Objective
**A binding international instrument.**

### Process
**Build from the existing CCW GGE work / rolling text rather than restarting from zero.**

### Implementation
**Prohibitions + strict regulations + human judgement/control + legal review + accountability.**

This lets Brazil be ambitious without sounding like it wants to throw away the existing negotiating process.

---

# 12. BRAZIL'S FIVE CORE COMMANDMENTS

These are the points you should be able to defend instantly.

### 1. PROHIBIT THE UNACCEPTABLE
Some autonomous systems or uses cannot reliably comply with IHL or preserve meaningful human control.

### 2. REGULATE THE REST
Systems that remain permissible require concrete operational and technical safeguards.

### 3. KEEP HUMAN JUDGEMENT REAL
The human must have the information, authority, context and ability to act needed for meaningful judgement.

### 4. REVIEW THROUGHOUT THE LIFECYCLE
Development, testing, deployment and material system changes must remain subject to legal and technical assurance.

### 5. KEEP RESPONSIBILITY WITH HUMANS
Autonomy must not become a mechanism for removing legal or command responsibility for consequences.

# 13. BRAZIL'S RED LINES — WORKING VERSION

These are the strongest positions to protect in negotiation.

## High priority
- eventual legally binding international rules;
- clear prohibition of autonomous uses that cannot satisfy IHL / meaningful human control requirements;
- meaningful human judgement/control over critical functions;
- continuing human responsibility and accountability;
- genuine legal and technical review;
- deployment limits appropriate to the operational context.

## Negotiable in wording
- exact definition of LAWS;
- exact wording of "meaningful human control";
- how direct versus indirect human control is described;
- institutional design;
- verification architecture;
- sequencing of national and international measures;
- timeline and implementation phases;
- confidentiality protections for sensitive technical information.

The point is not to turn every phrase into a red line.

# 14. WHERE BRAZIL CAN COMPROMISE

A strong Brazil delegate should know what can move.

### Definition
Brazil can discuss the scope and wording of autonomy while protecting the substance of human control and IHL compliance.

### Human control
Brazil can negotiate different formulations such as:
- meaningful human control;
- appropriate human judgement and control;
- context-appropriate human judgement.

The substance matters more than winning a vocabulary fight.

### Verification
Brazil can support:
- phased implementation;
- national reporting;
- confidential technical review;
- international assistance;
- confidence-building mechanisms.

### Forum
Brazil can emphasize the CCW as the practical negotiating foundation while recognizing UNGA's complementary political/reporting role.

### Treaty sequencing
Brazil can support a staged process from:
**elements → negotiating mandate → negotiation → implementation**

rather than pretending the final treaty can appear instantly.

# 15. BRAZIL'S BIGGEST ATTACK VECTORS

Prepare these before anyone asks.

## Attack 1 — "Why do you need new law if IHL already applies?"

### Answer direction
Brazil accepts that IHL applies.

The argument is that autonomy creates specific challenges around predictability, control, distinction, proportionality, precautions and accountability, so existing law should be complemented by specific rules.

---

## Attack 2 — "Isn't meaningful human control vague?"

### Answer direction
Brazil should respond with operational requirements rather than a slogan:

- situational awareness;
- spatial/temporal limits;
- predictable target identification;
- ability to intervene/deactivate;
- no autonomous mission-parameter changes without validation.

---

## Attack 3 — "Won't regulation kill military innovation?"

### Answer direction
Brazil can distinguish:
- autonomy that remains governable and lawful;
from
- autonomy that creates unacceptable or uncontrollable effects.

The objective is not to ban all military AI.

---

## Attack 4 — "How do you verify military systems without exposing classified information?"

### Answer direction
Support:
- protected technical review;
- confidential verification;
- national implementation;
- common testing standards;
- limited international reporting focused on compliance rather than operational secrets.

---

## Attack 5 — "Brazil is a BRICS country. Why disagree with Russia/China?"

### Answer direction

Brazil's foreign policy does not require identical positions with every grouping it belongs to.

Useful line:

> "Brazil cooperates across groupings, but its position on this question is based on its own assessment of humanitarian law and international security."

Do not turn this into a personal attack on other BRICS delegates.

---

# 16. BRAZIL'S BIGGEST SELF-CHECKS

Do not let an opponent catch you overstating these.

### Self-check 1
**166 YES ≠ 166 states supporting every treaty clause.**

### Self-check 2
**42 states ≠ 42 identical national positions.**

### Self-check 3
**CCW consensus ≠ "one state has a formal veto."**
More precise: unresolved disagreement can prevent consensus outcomes.

### Self-check 4
**MHC ≠ universally agreed single legal definition.**

### Self-check 5
**Article 36 ≠ a magic certificate making a weapon permanently lawful.**

### Self-check 6
**LAWS ≠ every AI-enabled military system.**
Do not accidentally call ordinary decision-support, logistics AI or surveillance AI "LAWS."

# 19. BRAZIL'S 30-SECOND ANSWER

> "Brazil supports a legally binding international instrument on autonomous weapons. We are not arguing that every form of military AI should be banned. We support clear prohibitions for systems or uses that cannot comply with IHL or preserve meaningful human control, while regulating other systems through operational limits, legal review, testing, human intervention and continuing accountability. We want to build from the CCW's existing work rather than start from zero."

---

# 20. BRAZIL'S 60-SECOND ANSWER

> "Brazil's approach has three layers. First, some autonomous systems or uses must be prohibited where meaningful human control and compliance with IHL cannot be ensured. Second, other systems require strict regulation through context-appropriate human judgement, operational limits, predictability, testing and legal review. Third, responsibility must remain traceable to human decision-makers and states throughout the lifecycle. Brazil therefore supports negotiations toward a legally binding international instrument, while using the CCW's existing rolling-text work as a practical foundation."
