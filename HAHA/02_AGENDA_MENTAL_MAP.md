# 02 — AGENDA MENTAL MAP

> Agenda: Establishing an International Regulatory Framework for the Development and Deployment of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)

## The one idea

This agenda is **not simply "ban killer robots."**

It is a regulatory-design problem:

**What capability is covered?**
→ **What law already applies?**
→ **Where does autonomy create a practical gap?**
→ **What must humans still control/judge?**
→ **What must be prohibited?**
→ **What can be allowed under strict conditions?**
→ **How is the system governed across its lifecycle?**
→ **How is responsibility preserved?**
→ **How can compliance be demonstrated?**
→ **What institution should carry the rules forward?**

That is the entire agenda.

---

# 1. SCOPE — WHAT ARE WE EVEN REGULATING?

The first mistake is treating every military AI system as LAWS.

AI can support:
- intelligence;
- image analysis;
- logistics;
- planning;
- decision support;
- cyber defence.

Those are not automatically LAWS.

Autonomy can also exist in different functions:
- navigation;
- detection;
- classification;
- tracking;
- target selection;
- engagement;
- battle-damage assessment;
- re-planning.

The most sensitive part of the agenda is **autonomy in critical functions connected to selecting and applying force**.

## The key definition fight

The international debate has not produced one universal definition covering every system.

The 2026 GGE working process used a capability/function-based characterization focused on systems capable of identifying, selecting and engaging a target without human intervention in those execution tasks, while negotiating questions around human parameter-setting, "identify," lethality and existing fire-and-forget systems.

### What to remember

**Platform ≠ autonomy.**

A drone is not automatically a LAWS.

A missile is not automatically a LAWS.

AI is not automatically a LAWS.

The better question is:

> **What is the system autonomously doing at the point where force is selected and applied?**

---

# 2. LEGAL BASELINE — IHL ALREADY APPLIES

LAWS do not exist in a legal vacuum.

Relevant IHL rules include:
- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- prohibition of indiscriminate attacks;
- rules concerning unnecessary suffering/superfluous injury.

### Article 36

For States Parties to Additional Protocol I, Article 36 requires legal review of new weapons, means and methods of warfare at the study, development, acquisition or adoption stage.

Important:

**Article 36 is not an identical treaty obligation for every UN Member State.**

### The real debate

States can agree:

> "IHL applies."

and still disagree over:

> "Do we need additional LAWS-specific international rules?"

That is exactly where the committee debate lives.

---

# 3. WHY AUTONOMY CREATES THE GOVERNANCE PROBLEM

Autonomy changes the targeting chain.

Traditional model:

**Human observes → human assesses → human decides → weapon acts**

Autonomous system:

**Sensors/data → machine processing → machine selection → machine engagement**

The legal problem is not simply that a machine is involved.

The problem is what happens to:

- context;
- human judgement;
- predictability;
- speed;
- information;
- intervention;
- accountability.

### The central question

> **Can a State still ensure lawful and responsible use of force when critical decisions are being made by an autonomous system?**

---

# 4. HUMAN JUDGEMENT + HUMAN CONTROL

This is the centre of the agenda.

Human control can happen at several layers:

### Strategic
Should this capability be possessed/used at all?

### Mission
What target category, geography, duration and engagement limits apply?

### Operational
Can humans reassess when circumstances change?

### Technical
Can the system be constrained, interrupted or safely deactivated where feasible?

## Direct vs indirect control

### Direct
A human directly authorizes each critical engagement/target.

### Indirect/contextual
A human establishes strict parameters and the system operates autonomously within those limits.

Neither model automatically solves every problem.

Direct control can be impractical in very fast defensive situations.

Indirect control can become meaningless if the human's prior authorization is too broad or the operator cannot realistically intervene.

### Therefore

Do not argue:

> "A human is present, therefore human control exists."

Ask:

- Did the human understand the system?
- Did they have enough information?
- Did they have enough time?
- Could they set meaningful limits?
- Could they respond to changing circumstances?
- Could they intervene or deactivate where feasible?
- Was the system predictable enough for the judgement to be meaningful?

---

# 5. THE AUTOMATION-BIAS PROBLEM

Even formal human authorization can become meaningless.

Example chain:

**AI recommends target**
→ operator trusts recommendation
→ operator has seconds to decide
→ interface hides uncertainty
→ operator approves almost automatically

Technically:
**human in the loop**

Functionally:
**machine heavily determines the outcome**

So regulation may need to cover:
- training;
- operator workload;
- interface design;
- uncertainty indicators;
- override authority;
- audit logs;
- escalation thresholds.

### The important idea

> **Human control is a human-agency problem, not a "one human button" problem.**

---

# 6. PROHIBITION — WHAT SHOULD NEVER BE ALLOWED?

There is no single universally agreed prohibition list.

The major policy candidates are:

### A. Direct autonomous force against humans
The ICRC advocates prohibiting autonomous weapons designed or used to apply force directly against people.

### B. Systems incapable of IHL compliance
If a system cannot reliably be used consistently with distinction, proportionality or precautions, a prohibition can be argued.

### C. Unpredictable/uncontrollable systems
If effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or controlled in the intended operating context.

### D. Systems without meaningful human control
Where no adequate human judgement/control can actually constrain critical force decisions.

### E. Autonomous alteration of critical parameters
For example, systems changing target categories, geographic boundaries or mission permissions without human validation.

## The real negotiation

The debate is not simply:

**BAN vs NO BAN**

It is:

> **What exact threshold turns a system/use from "regulatable" into "unacceptable"?**

---

# 7. REGULATION — WHAT ABOUT EVERYTHING ELSE?

This is where the agenda becomes much more interesting.

A possible two-tier architecture is:

## TIER 1
**Prohibit specified unacceptable systems/uses.**

## TIER 2
**Strictly regulate other autonomous systems.**

Possible controls:
- target restrictions;
- military-object limits;
- geographic limits;
- time/duration limits;
- engagement-scale limits;
- human judgement/control;
- operator training;
- testing;
- legal review;
- intervention/deactivation where feasible;
- cybersecurity;
- audit trails;
- incident reporting;
- lifecycle re-review.

This lets a State say:

> "We are not banning every form of military autonomy. We are drawing enforceable lines around dangerous or uncontrollable autonomy."

---

# 8. PROPORTIONALITY + DISTINCTION IN AUTONOMOUS SYSTEMS

## Distinction

Can the system reliably distinguish:
- civilian vs military objects;
- lawful military objectives;
- protected persons;
- changing circumstances?

## Proportionality

Can the system deal with context-sensitive civilian harm and anticipated military advantage?

The important question is not:

> "Can an algorithm calculate?"

It is:

> **Can the system be reliably used within the legal conditions of the actual attack environment?**

Urban environments are especially challenging because:
- civilians can enter the operating area;
- status can change;
- objects have multiple uses;
- battlefield information can become outdated quickly.

Therefore context and operating limits matter.

---

# 9. LIFECYCLE — THE WEAPON DOESN'T STAY STATIC

A LAWS framework cannot stop at development.

Think:

**Research**
→ **Development**
→ **Legal review**
→ **Procurement**
→ **Testing**
→ **Deployment**
→ **Operation**
→ **Modification**
→ **Retirement / transfer control**

### Why?

Because software can change.

Models can change.

Sensors can change.

Target classifiers can change.

Mission environments can change.

Therefore:

> **A system that was lawful or predictable at Time A may need reassessment after a material change at Time B.**

This is why the research frames Article 36 + technical assurance as potentially a continuing lifecycle process.

---

# 10. TESTING + ASSURANCE

Testing should not be a ceremonial box.

Possible tests include:
- expected environments;
- degraded sensors;
- communication loss;
- cyber/electronic interference;
- unexpected civilian presence;
- adversarial inputs;
- interaction with other autonomous systems;
- software/model updates.

Useful evidence:
- version identity;
- operating envelope;
- failure modes;
- testing records;
- known limitations;
- change history;
- operator training.

### Mental model

**Legal review asks: "Can this be lawfully used?"**

**Testing asks: "Does it actually behave within the assumptions behind that legal judgement?"**

You need both.

---

# 11. ACCOUNTABILITY — WHO IS RESPONSIBLE?

A machine cannot itself be the bearer of legal responsibility.

The challenge is preserving a traceable human/state responsibility chain.

Potential actors:
- State authorities;
- commanders;
- mission planners;
- operators;
- procurement authorities;
- developers/suppliers.

That does **not** mean every actor automatically has identical legal responsibility.

The point is to make relevant duties identifiable.

### Practical mechanisms

- responsible chain of command;
- mission authorization records;
- configuration logs;
- software/model version records;
- audit trails;
- incident investigation;
- evidence retention.

---

# 12. VERIFICATION — HOW DO WE KNOW STATES ARE COMPLYING?

This is one of the hardest parts.

You cannot realistically demand:

> "Every State publishes its military source code."

A more realistic ladder is:

### Level 1
National legal review + implementation.

### Level 2
Transparency about national frameworks.

### Level 3
Serious-incident reporting.

### Level 4
Expert/peer exchange.

### Level 5
Retention of auditable records.

### Level 6
Confidential technical review or international verification where feasible.

### Core idea

> **Verify processes and assurance evidence, not fantasy-level transparency.**

---

# 13. PROCUREMENT + PRIVATE INDUSTRY

Procurement is an underrated governance lever.

A State can require suppliers to provide:
- technical documentation;
- testing evidence;
- cybersecurity controls;
- update/change notifications;
- auditability;
- incident support.

The international obligation remains primarily on States, but procurement can make compliance practical.

This also helps with black-box systems and rapidly changing AI models.

---

# 14. AI DECISION SUPPORT — THE BOUNDARY PROBLEM

A system can recommend a target without autonomously engaging it.

That is **not automatically a LAWS**.

But it can still undermine human judgement through:
- automation bias;
- extreme time pressure;
- opaque recommendations;
- operator workload;
- organizational dependence.

So the international debate may eventually need two connected tracks:

**LAWS regulation**
+
**responsible military AI decision-support governance**

without pretending they are identical.

### Useful committee example

The Lavender/Habsora debate in the Israel context is useful for illustrating this boundary, but the evidence is disputed. The IDF says these are intelligence/targeting-support tools and denies autonomous target selection/attack-order generation; a UN Commission report separately discussed allegations and emphasized diligent assessment, validation and approval.

So say:

> "This illustrates the governance problem around AI-assisted targeting and human judgement."

Do NOT say:

> "This proves Israel deployed LAWS."

---

# 15. PROLIFERATION + NON-STATE ACTORS

Autonomy creates another problem because relevant technology can combine:
- commercial drones;
- software;
- sensors;
- processors;
- open-source tools;
- military components.

Possible concerns:
- illicit transfers;
- diversion;
- theft/capture;
- non-State adaptation;
- unauthorized model/software modification.

Possible responses:
- export/end-use controls;
- secure software distribution;
- transfer restrictions for prohibited systems;
- anti-diversion cooperation;
- reporting on loss/theft of high-risk systems.

---

# 16. ESCALATION + SWARMS

Autonomy can compress decision time.

Possible mechanisms:
- faster engagement cycles;
- simultaneous engagements;
- communications loss;
- false identification;
- cyber interference;
- cascading responses;
- autonomous-on-autonomous interaction.

Swarms make this more difficult because many systems can function as a coordinated mission system.

### Possible controls

- geographic containment;
- target restrictions;
- engagement-rate limits;
- maximum scale;
- communication-loss rules;
- automatic halt conditions;
- human authorization before expansion of target sets.

Do not claim that autonomy will automatically cause a "flash war."

Say it can **create escalation pathways** that regulation should address.

---

# 17. THE INSTITUTIONAL QUESTION

There are two different questions:

### SUBSTANCE
What rules should exist?

### FORUM
Where should those rules be negotiated?

## CCW

Best suited to:
- specialist negotiation;
- weapons law;
- technical discussion;
- GGE rolling-text work;
- future Protocol/treaty negotiations.

## UNGA / DISEC

Can:
- build political momentum;
- request reports;
- encourage negotiations;
- establish complementary discussion;
- promote transparency/capacity-building.

But:

**A UNGA resolution is not itself a binding LAWS treaty.**

### Strong institutional architecture

**UNGA**
→ political momentum + global reporting + complementary work

**CCW**
→ specialist negotiation + legal instrument

**National authorities**
→ legal reviews + procurement + testing + investigation

**Technical mechanism**
→ standards + testing methodologies + expert exchange

# 24. THE REAL QUESTION BEHIND THE ENTIRE AGENDA

A useful way to understand LAWS is to stop asking:

> "Are autonomous weapons good or bad?"

That question is too vague for a DISEC negotiation.

Ask instead:

> **"What decisions are being delegated from humans to machines, under what conditions, and what international rules should apply?"**

That gives you the real structure of the debate.

A machine can do many things:

- navigate;
- search;
- track;
- classify;
- recommend;
- select;
- engage.

The more the machine moves toward the **decision to apply lethal force**, the more serious the legal and accountability questions become.

---

# 25. THE DELEGATION-OF-DECISION MODEL

Think of autonomy as a chain.

**Human**

↓

sets mission

↓

sets limits

↓

activates system

↓

system detects objects

↓

system identifies objects

↓

system selects target

↓

system chooses engagement

↓

system applies force

The international debate is partly about:

> **At which points can the machine act, and at which points must human judgement remain effective?**

This is much more useful than simply asking whether a weapon is "autonomous."

---

# 26. THE THREE BASIC LEVELS OF AUTOMATION

For mental clarity, distinguish three broad ideas.

## LEVEL 1 — AUTOMATION

The system follows a defined rule or pre-set process.

Example:

> "If incoming object has these characteristics, activate the defensive response."

The system may be highly automated without independently deciding the wider mission.

## LEVEL 2 — HUMAN-SUPERVISED AUTONOMY

The system performs some functions independently inside human-defined conditions.

Humans establish:

- mission;
- target class;
- geography;
- duration;
- other limits.

## LEVEL 3 — HIGHLY AUTONOMOUS LETHAL FUNCTION

After activation, the system can independently perform critical target-selection and engagement functions without further human intervention in those execution tasks.

This is where the highest regulatory questions arise.

### Important

These are analytical categories for understanding the debate, not a universal legal classification adopted by all States.

---

# 27. WHY "AI" IS THE WRONG MASTER DEFINITION

AI is a broad technology family.

It can be used for:

- translation;
- intelligence analysis;
- logistics;
- maintenance;
- cyber defence;
- image processing;
- decision support;
- target recognition;
- weapons functions.

So the question:

> "Should we regulate military AI?"

is too broad for the LAWS agenda.

The better question is:

> **"Which autonomous functions create the lethal decision risk this framework is supposed to regulate?"**

---

# 28. THE FUNCTION-BASED TEST

When somebody names a weapon system, ask:

### 1
What does the human decide?

### 2
What does the machine decide?

### 3
When does the machine make that decision?

### 4
What information does it use?

### 5
Can the human change the result?

### 6
Can the machine change its own parameters?

### 7
What happens if conditions change?

Those seven questions are your definition tool.

---

# 29. FIRE-AND-FORGET IS NOT A COMPLETE ANSWER

A classic term in the debate is "fire-and-forget."

But "fire-and-forget" does not automatically mean:

> "LAWS."

A system may be launched without further communication while still operating under tightly bounded rules and previously selected targeting conditions.

The real questions remain:

- what autonomy does it have after launch?
- can it select targets?
- what target categories?
- where?
- for how long?
- under what assumptions?
- what can the human still control?

This is why function-based analysis is stronger than platform labels.

---

# 30. THE CRITICAL-FUNCTION MAP

For any system, map:

## FUNCTION A — DETECT

What is present?

## FUNCTION B — IDENTIFY

What is it?

## FUNCTION C — CLASSIFY

What category does it belong to?

## FUNCTION D — SELECT

Which object should be engaged?

## FUNCTION E — ENGAGE

Should force be applied?

## FUNCTION F — REASSESS

Does the decision remain valid?

The more critical the function, the more important human judgement, testing and assurance become.

---

# 31. WHERE THE HUMAN CAN EXIST IN THE LOOP

People often talk about:

- human in the loop;
- human on the loop;
- human out of the loop.

These labels are useful, but they are not enough.

## Human in the loop

Human directly approves each relevant action.

### Strength

Clear human role.

### Problem

May be operationally impossible in very fast environments.

## Human on the loop

Human supervises autonomous action and can intervene.

### Strength

More compatible with bounded autonomy.

### Problem

Intervention may be only theoretical if:

- reaction time is too short;
- information is poor;
- system speed is too high.

## Human out of the loop

No human intervention in the relevant execution decision.

### Core concern

Accountability and lawful control may become much harder.

### Better debate question

Do not ask only:

> "Is there a human in the loop?"

Ask:

> **"What can the human actually do, and how much time and information do they have?"**

---

# 32. THE HUMAN-CONTROL LADDER

Build the concept from weak to strong.

### Level 1
Human decided to acquire the system.

### Level 2
Human decided to activate it.

### Level 3
Human defined the mission.

### Level 4
Human defined target/context limits.

### Level 5
Human has enough information to judge the use.

### Level 6
Human can monitor and reassess.

### Level 7
Human can intervene/deactivate where feasible.

### Level 8
Human responsibility remains traceable after use.

A system can have a human somewhere in the chain but still fail several of these levels.

---

# 33. HUMAN CONTROL VS HUMAN AUTHORITY

These are not identical.

### Authority

The person is legally empowered to make the decision.

### Control

The person can practically constrain the system.

A commander may have formal authority but lack practical control if:

- the machine cannot be interrupted;
- the operator does not know what it is doing;
- the time window is impossible;
- the system can expand its own mission.

### Mental line

> **"Authority on paper is not control in practice."**

---

# 34. HUMAN CONTROL VS HUMAN RESPONSIBILITY

These are also different.

### Human control

Can the human meaningfully influence the use?

### Human responsibility

Can responsibility still be assigned to a person and institution?

You need both.

A system could theoretically be controlled but still have poor accountability records.

Or responsibility could be assigned formally while actual control is weak.

---

# 35. CONTEXT IS NOT AN EXCUSE — IT IS PART OF THE TEST

Human control must be assessed in context.

Relevant conditions include:

- speed;
- geography;
- target type;
- civilian presence;
- communications;
- environmental complexity;
- sensor quality;
- number of systems;
- duration of operation.

Therefore:

> **"Context-appropriate" does not mean "anything goes."**

It means the required safeguards can differ according to the real operating environment.

---

# 36. WHY DIRECT HUMAN APPROVAL MAY NOT ALWAYS WORK

Imagine a defensive system with extremely short reaction time.

If the rule says:

> "A human must approve every individual engagement in real time."

there may be a situation where the human cannot physically respond quickly enough.

So a serious regulatory framework may instead require:

- prior human mission authorization;
- narrow target classes;
- strict geographic bounds;
- limited duration;
- tested system behaviour;
- reliable intervention where feasible;
- predefined responses to failure.

The key is to preserve effective human judgement without writing an operationally impossible rule.

---

# 37. BUT INDIRECT CONTROL HAS A DANGER

Indirect control can also become a loophole.

Example:

Human says:

> "Attack anything matching this very broad profile for six hours across this huge area."

The system then makes almost every meaningful lethal choice.

Technically:

> "A human authorized the mission."

Practically:

> the machine may have received enormous decision-making freedom.

Therefore indirect control must be combined with:

- narrow target limits;
- geography;
- duration;
- scale;
- environmental assumptions;
- intervention;
- monitoring.

---

# 38. THE OPERATING-ENVELOPE IDEA

A useful mental model:

> **The machine can act autonomously only inside a human-defined box.**

The box may include:

- **WHERE**
- **WHEN**
- **AGAINST WHAT**
- **HOW MANY**
- **UNDER WHICH CONDITIONS**
- **WITH WHAT COMMUNICATIONS**
- **WHAT HAPPENS IF CONDITIONS CHANGE**

That makes abstract human control concrete.

---

# 39. THE "BOX GETS SMALLER AS RISK GETS HIGHER" MODEL

A useful negotiating principle:

> **The more unpredictable or consequential the autonomy, the tighter the operating envelope should become.**

For example:

More restrictive:

- shorter duration;
- narrower geography;
- narrower target category;
- smaller engagement scale;
- stronger intervention requirement.

Less restrictive:

- predictable defensive environment;
- narrow target class;
- highly validated system;
- constrained operating conditions.

This is a proposed regulatory principle, not an existing universal rule.

---

# 40. PROHIBITION IS A THRESHOLD QUESTION

Everyone can say:

> "Some systems should be prohibited."

The difficult part is:

> **Which ones?**

A serious prohibition test has to answer:

1. What characteristic triggers the prohibition?
2. Is the trigger about the design or the use?
3. Who determines that it is met?
4. What evidence proves it?
5. Does the prohibition apply to development, acquisition, stockpiling, transfer, deployment or use?
6. Are there exceptions?

---

# 41. DESIGN-BASED VS USE-BASED PROHIBITION

## Design-based

A system is prohibited because the system's characteristics make lawful use impossible or unacceptable.

### Advantage

Easier to prevent the problem before deployment.

### Difficulty

Technology may sometimes have different configurations or modes.

## Use-based

A system is prohibited only in specified circumstances.

### Advantage

More context-sensitive.

### Difficulty

A system may exist even if one particular use is prohibited.

### Strong framework

Some combination may be necessary.

---

# 42. THE HUMAN-TARGET QUESTION

One major policy proposal in international discussions is prohibition of autonomous use of force directly against people.

Why?

Because human beings are difficult to classify through fixed or learned rules in complex contexts, and because the legal and humanitarian stakes are especially high.

But be precise:

> **This is a proposed prohibition approach, not a universally accepted treaty rule already in force.**

That distinction matters.

---

# 43. MILITARY-OBJECTIVE QUESTIONS

Another difficult issue is autonomy against objects rather than people.

An autonomous system might engage:

- tanks;
- ships;
- aircraft;
- radar;
- launchers;
- other military objects.

The legal issue does not disappear.

Questions remain:

- Can the system reliably identify the object?
- Could civilians be present?
- Can the effects be limited?
- Could the object cease to be a military objective?
- Can proportionality be assessed?
- What happens if circumstances change?

---

# 44. DISTINCTION — THE SIMPLE VERSION

Distinction asks:

> **Can the attacker distinguish lawful targets from protected persons and objects?**

For autonomous systems, the concern is not simply:

> "Can the sensor recognize a shape?"

It is:

> **Can the system support a lawful targeting decision in the actual context?**

That includes:

- status;
- behaviour;
- surrounding circumstances;
- changing conditions.

---

# 45. PROPORTIONALITY — THE SIMPLE VERSION

Proportionality is not:

> "Did the machine hit accurately?"

It is about whether expected civilian harm is excessive in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage.

Autonomy can complicate this because:

- expected effects may be uncertain;
- target circumstances can change;
- multiple interactions may occur;
- civilian presence can change.

Therefore a technical accuracy claim does not automatically solve the proportionality question.

---

# 46. PRECAUTIONS — WHY THEY MATTER FOR LAWS

Precautions involve taking feasible steps to reduce civilian harm.

For autonomous systems, this may mean:

- choosing the right operating area;
- choosing appropriate timing;
- defining target types;
- setting limits;
- monitoring conditions;
- cancelling use when circumstances change.

### Simple line

> **"Autonomy does not remove the duty to take feasible precautions."**

---

# 47. "ACCURATE" DOES NOT MEAN "LAWFUL"

This is an extremely useful distinction.

A weapon can be:

- technically accurate;
- reliable;
- fast;

and still be unsuitable for a particular lawful use.

Accuracy answers:

> "Can it hit what it detects?"

Lawfulness asks:

> "Should it engage this target in these circumstances, with these expected effects?"

Do not confuse the two.

---

# 48. PREDICTABILITY VS ACCURACY

### Accuracy

How often does the system get a classification or effect right?

### Predictability

Can responsible humans reasonably anticipate how it will behave in the expected conditions?

A system could have high average accuracy but behave badly under unusual conditions.

That is why testing must include edge cases.

---

# 49. RELIABILITY VS PREDICTABILITY

### Reliability

Does the system perform consistently?

### Predictability

Can its behaviour be anticipated?

They overlap but are not identical.

A good framework should consider both.

---

# 50. EXPLAINABILITY — KEEP IT PRACTICAL

The goal is not necessarily to explain every mathematical step inside a model.

The practical question is:

> **Can the responsible people understand the system's relevant capabilities, limitations and expected effects sufficiently to use it lawfully?**

This is especially important for:

- target selection;
- engagement;
- warning signals;
- uncertainty;
- failure modes.

---

# 51. THE BLACK-BOX PROBLEM

If humans cannot understand enough about the system to make a lawful decision, then merely saying:

> "The human approved it"

may not solve the problem.

The black-box problem is therefore connected to:

- human control;
- predictability;
- legal review;
- training;
- accountability.

This is why these agenda questions should not be studied separately.

---

# 52. ADAPTIVE / LEARNING SYSTEMS

Machine-learning systems can complicate legal review if:

- the model changes;
- the environment changes;
- data changes;
- sensor fusion changes;
- the system adapts during deployment.

The key mental question:

> **"Is the system still sufficiently characterized after it changes?"**

If not, renewed assurance may be necessary.

---

# 53. ONLINE LEARNING

A particularly difficult case is learning during operation.

Imagine:

> The system changes part of its decision process while deployed.

Now ask:

- Was that change authorized?
- Was it tested?
- Can it be reversed?
- Does it affect target selection?
- Does it affect engagement?
- Does the operating envelope still hold?

This is one reason lifecycle assurance matters.

---

# 54. MATERIAL CHANGE

Not every software update needs the same response.

Think:

### Minor

No relevant change to critical functions.

### Significant

Possible effect on performance.

### Material

Could alter:

- target identification;
- selection;
- engagement;
- control;
- predictability;
- expected effects;
- operating boundaries.

A mature framework can trigger deeper review only for material changes.

---

# 55. WHY TESTING IS NOT A ONE-TIME EVENT

A system can change after approval.

Therefore:

> **Testing has to keep pace with the system.**

Relevant stages:

- initial development;
- pre-deployment;
- after material modification;
- after serious incidents;
- after major changes to operating conditions.

---

# 56. WHAT SHOULD TESTING INCLUDE?

Testing should move beyond ideal conditions.

Useful scenarios include:

- normal conditions;
- degraded sensors;
- communications loss;
- electronic interference;
- cyber interference;
- unexpected civilian presence;
- ambiguous targets;
- unusual weather/environment;
- interaction with other systems.

The point is to test the **conditions in which the system could fail**, not only the conditions in which it looks impressive.

---

# 57. REALISTIC SIMULATION

A useful test question:

> "Would this test reveal the failure modes we care about in real operation?"

If not, a perfect test score is not very useful.

This is why realistic simulations and operationally relevant environments matter.

---

# 58. TESTING VS LEGAL REVIEW

These are different.

### Testing asks:

> "How does the system behave?"

### Legal review asks:

> "Can the system be used consistently with applicable law?"

You need both.

A technically excellent system can still fail a legal review.

A legally reviewed system can still need stronger technical evidence.

---

# 59. PROCUREMENT AS A HIDDEN REGULATORY LEVER

The committee may spend hours debating treaties while ignoring procurement.

But procurement determines:

- what systems enter service;
- what documentation suppliers provide;
- whether updates are controlled;
- whether audit rights exist;
- who is responsible.

So ask:

> **"Can the State prove it can govern a system before purchasing it?"**

This is a practical regulatory question.

The agenda is about the **whole journey**, not just the moment the weapon fires.

---

# 61. WHY LIFECYCLE THINKING MATTERS

Suppose a State performs an excellent review at development.

Then:

- the model changes;
- the software changes;
- the sensor changes;
- the mission changes.

If there is no continued assurance, the original review may no longer describe the operational system.

Therefore:

> **A good framework must survive technology change.**

---

# 62. ACCOUNTABILITY — THE "WHO KNEW WHAT?" QUESTION

After an incident, ask:

1. Who authorized the use?
2. What did they know?
3. What did the system know?
4. What parameters were active?
5. What warnings existed?
6. What did the operator do?
7. What happened next?

This is why logs and records matter.

---

# 63. TRACEABILITY — THE EVIDENCE TRAIL

Traceability can include:

- system identity;
- software/model version;
- operating parameters;
- mission authorization;
- system warnings;
- interventions;
- relevant sensor data;
- incident records.

Not all information needs to be publicly released.

The idea is:

> **The State should be able to reconstruct the chain when necessary.**

---

# 64. ACCOUNTABILITY IS NOT THE SAME AS PUNISHMENT

Accountability can involve:

- identifying responsibility;
- investigating incidents;
- correcting systems;
- retraining personnel;
- changing doctrine;
- improving safeguards.

Do not reduce the issue to:

> "Who gets punished?"

First establish:

> **Can responsibility be identified and the system corrected?**

---

# 65. THE MACHINE-RESPONSIBILITY TRAP

Do not say:

> "The AI made the decision, so nobody is responsible."

That is exactly the governance gap the framework is trying to avoid.

Machines can perform autonomous functions.

They are not a substitute for:

- State responsibility;
- command responsibility;
- human legal responsibility.

---

# 66. INCIDENT INVESTIGATION

A strong lifecycle can include:

**Incident**

↓

**Preserve evidence**

↓

**Investigate**

↓

**Identify failure**

↓

**Assess legality**

↓

**Review system**

↓

**Correct / retrain / redesign**

↓

**Revalidate**

↓

**Deploy again only if justified**

This turns an incident into a governance feedback loop.

---

# 67. SERIOUS INCIDENTS

Potential examples:

- civilian harm;
- unauthorized target engagement;
- unexpected force application;
- loss of control;
- cyber compromise affecting critical functions;
- major software failure;
- violation of operating limits.

The exact reporting threshold is a negotiation question.

---

# 68. VERIFICATION — THE CENTRAL PROBLEM

There are two bad extremes.

## EXTREME A

> "Trust every State."

Too weak.

## EXTREME B

> "Publish everything and inspect everything."

Too intrusive for many States.

The useful middle is:

> **graduated verification.**

---

# 69. THE VERIFICATION LADDER

## LEVEL 1

National legal review.

## LEVEL 2

National testing and authorization.

## LEVEL 3

Aggregate transparency/reporting.

## LEVEL 4

Peer exchange.

## LEVEL 5

Serious-incident reporting.

## LEVEL 6

Protected technical review.

## LEVEL 7

More intrusive agreed inspection where politically and legally feasible.

This lets States build trust gradually.

---

# 70. WHY SOURCE-CODE DISCLOSURE IS NOT THE ONLY ANSWER

A State can demonstrate assurance through:

- test results;
- model/version records;
- operating restrictions;
- failure-mode analysis;
- legal review;
- incident records;
- protected expert review.

Source code may sometimes matter, but treating publication of source code as the default requirement could:

- expose vulnerabilities;
- reveal military secrets;
- discourage participation;
- oversimplify verification.

The core question is:

> **"Is there enough evidence to assess the safeguard?"**

---

# 71. CONFIDENTIALITY AND TRANSPARENCY

Think in layers.

### PUBLIC

- broad national policy;
- aggregate information.

### CONFIDENTIAL

- technical evidence;
- detailed testing information.

### PROTECTED / CLASSIFIED

- sensitive military information;
- vulnerabilities;
- operational details.

A mature regime can use different information channels for different purposes.

---

# 72. REGISTRY QUESTIONS

A possible registry could record:

- system category;
- assurance status;
- national responsible authority;
- broad operating restrictions;
- review status.

But a registry is not automatically necessary.

Its value depends on:

- who can access it;
- what is reported;
- what happens if the report is incomplete;
- how confidentiality is protected.

### Mental rule

> **Do not create a registry just because registries sound diplomatic.**

Create one only if it solves a real problem.

---

# 73. NON-STATE ACTORS

Why do they matter?

Because technology can potentially spread through:

- diversion;
- illicit procurement;
- captured systems;
- unauthorized modification;
- commercial supply chains;
- stolen software or components.

The framework therefore cannot stop at State development.

---

# 74. PROLIFERATION

Ask:

> How does the system move from State A to actor B?

Possible controls:

- export rules;
- end-user controls;
- secure software;
- update authentication;
- transfer documentation;
- post-transfer review.

The precise legal obligations require negotiation.

---

# 75. ESCALATION

Autonomy may affect escalation by changing:

- decision speed;
- reaction time;
- quantity of simultaneous actions;
- human intervention windows.

The safe analytical approach is:

> **"Autonomy can alter escalation pathways; therefore safeguards should address speed, scope, communication and human intervention."**

Avoid saying:

> "Autonomy will definitely start wars."

---

# 76. SWARMS

One autonomous system is one problem.

Many autonomous systems interacting can create another.

Potential concerns:

- aggregate engagement scale;
- rapid collective decisions;
- communications failures;
- deconfliction;
- emergent behaviour.

Therefore testing may need to consider:

> **system-of-systems behaviour**

not just one weapon at a time.

---

# 77. CYBER AND ADVERSARIAL INPUTS

Autonomous systems can be affected by:

- spoofed sensors;
- corrupted data;
- malicious inputs;
- navigation interference;
- communication disruption;
- compromised updates.

This creates a simple question:

> **"What happens when the system receives bad information?"**

A responsible framework should require testing and safeguards against foreseeable failure conditions.

---

# 78. COMMUNICATIONS LOSS

This deserves special attention because it connects:

- human control;
- autonomy;
- cybersecurity;
- fail-safe design.

Ask:

> **"Does losing communication give the system more freedom or less?"**

A strong regulatory principle is:

> **Loss of communication should not silently expand lethal authority.**

The exact fail response depends on the system and operational context.

---

# 79. DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS

Defensive systems may have:

- shorter reaction times;
- predefined threat types;
- specialized environments.

That can justify different operational requirements.

But it should not automatically mean:

> "Defensive = exempt."

Still ask:

- what is the target?
- what is the operating environment?
- how predictable is the system?
- what is the area?
- what is the duration?
- what happens if communications fail?
- can the system be constrained?

---

# 80. AI DECISION SUPPORT

Another important boundary:

An AI system that recommends:

> "Potential target detected."

is not identical to a system that autonomously:

> "selects and engages the target."

The human role can remain much more direct in decision-support systems.

This distinction matters because otherwise the LAWS framework becomes so broad that it starts regulating every military AI application.

---

# 81. AUTOMATION BIAS CONNECTS DECISION SUPPORT TO LAWS

Even decision support can create risks if:

- the human treats the recommendation as automatically correct;
- uncertainty is hidden;
- time pressure is extreme.

Therefore the broader AI discussion can connect to the LAWS agenda through:

- human judgement;
- interface design;
- training;
- accountability.

But do not collapse every decision-support system into LAWS.

---

# 82. MARTENS CLAUSE / HUMANITY

The Martens Clause can enter the debate as a reminder that where specific treaty rules do not fully address a situation, principles of humanity and public conscience remain relevant in the conduct of hostilities.

For MUN:

> Use it as a **supporting legal principle**, not as a magic sentence that automatically bans a technology.

The precise legal effect is contested and should be discussed carefully.

---

# 83. ETHICS VS LAW

This is a common debate trap.

### Legal question

> Is this use consistent with applicable law?

### Ethical question

> Is this use morally acceptable even if no existing rule clearly prohibits it?

They can overlap.

But do not say:

> "Ethics and law are identical."

During negotiation, distinguish:

- legal obligations;
- policy preferences;
- ethical concerns.

This prevents legal overstatement.

---

# 84. THE LEGAL-FORM QUESTION

The committee is not only debating:

> "What should States do?"

It is also debating:

> **"What kind of international instrument should contain the rules?"**

Possible outcomes include:

- political declaration;
- principles;
- voluntary commitments;
- reporting measures;
- protocol;
- treaty;
- amendment;
- future negotiating mandate.

Brazil wants the eventual binding path.

But you must understand the difference between:

> **substantive agreement**

and

> **legal-form agreement.**

Two countries can agree on testing and human control but disagree about whether those rules belong in a treaty.

## UNGA

- broader participation;
- political momentum;

- reporting;
- wider diplomatic engagement.

# 86. WHY DISEC MATTERS

Your committee is UNGA First Committee / DISEC.

That means you should not act as if you are literally sitting inside the CCW GGE.

Your job is to:

- debate international security;
- develop political recommendations;

Example:

or:

rather than pretending DISEC can directly command a weapons test laboratory.

# 88. DIPLOMATIC DEADLOCK

Why has LAWS been difficult?

Because States may disagree on:

- definition;
- legal form;
- human-control terminology;
- military utility;
- acceptable autonomy;
- verification;
- pace of negotiation.

But disagreement does not mean:

> "There is no common ground."

There may be substantial overlap on:

- IHL;
- human responsibility;
- testing;
- lifecycle management;
- safeguards;
- incident investigation.

A smart negotiator identifies the overlap first.

# 89. THE "SUBSTANCE BEFORE LABEL" METHOD

Suppose one State rejects:

> "meaningful human control"

Ask:

> "Would you support human judgement over critical functions, target limits, legal review and intervention?"

If they say yes:

You have substantive convergence even if the label differs.

This is one of the most useful MUN negotiation skills in the whole agenda.

---

# 90. THE "CLAUSE BEFORE TREATY" METHOD

Do not begin every negotiation with:

> "Do you support a treaty?"

Instead ask:

> "Would you support renewed review after material changes?"

or:

> "Would you support operating limits?"

or:

> "Would you support serious-incident reporting?"

Once substantive overlap exists, legal form becomes a separate conversation.

# 91. CASE-STUDY DISCIPLINE

Real-world cases are useful because they show why the debate matters.

But do not use a disputed case as if it proves everything.

The research audit gives three important examples.

## Libya / Kargu-2

Use the UN Panel of Experts as the primary institutional source.

Say:

> "The UN report described the relevant system and incident."

Do not casually say:

> "This proves fully autonomous lethal attacks definitely happened exactly as alleged."

The evidence and interpretation require care.

# 92. LOITERING MUNITIONS / NAGORNO-KARABAKH

- sensor-based functions;
- varying levels of human involvement;
- target-selection questions;
- why platform labels are insufficient.

But:

> **Not every loitering munition is automatically a LAWS.**

The key question remains:

> **Which critical functions are autonomous?**

# 93. ISRAEL / AI-ENABLED TARGETING CASE

This is a particularly sensitive case.

The IDF's public position describes systems such as Lavender and Habsora as intelligence-processing tools and denies that they autonomously select attack targets or generate attack orders.

A UN Commission report discussed allegations around recommendations and emphasized the need for validation and approval procedures.

### MUN rule

When discussing this case:

- separate claims from documented facts;
- attribute contested claims;
- do not use it as automatic proof that the systems are LAWS;
- use it to discuss **human validation, automation bias and accountability**.

That is much safer and more impressive.

# 94. WHY CASE STUDIES DO NOT REPLACE THE FRAMEWORK

What failed or became uncertain?

### Rule

What safeguard would address it?

### Evidence

How would a State show compliance?

### Institution

Who would maintain the rule?

That is how you turn examples into policy.

# 117. THE ENTIRE DEBATE IN ONE SENTENCE

> **"Who gets to decide when lethal force is used, how much decision-making can be delegated to a machine, what limits remain on that delegation, and how States prove that those limits are being respected?"**

That is the core philosophical and regulatory question.

# 119. THE "PROBLEM → RULE → MECHANISM" HABIT

Whenever you study any issue, force yourself to complete three sentences.

### Example 1

Problem:
> Autonomous systems may operate beyond human expectations.

Rule:
> Human control must remain effective.

Mechanism:
> Operating envelope + testing + intervention.

### Example 2

Problem:
> Software can change.

Rule:
> Assurance must survive material change.

Mechanism:
> Change-control trigger + renewed review.

### Example 3

Problem:
> Military data is sensitive.

Rule:
> Verification should still create evidence.

Mechanism:
> Confidential technical review.

### Example 4

Problem:
> Developing States may lack technical capacity.

Rule:
> The framework must be implementable.

Mechanism:
> Regional testing/legal-review hubs.

This is how you start thinking like a drafter.

# 120. THE "FIVE QUESTIONS AFTER EVERY MECHANISM"

For every proposed solution ask:

1. What problem does it solve?
2. Who implements it?
3. When does it trigger?
4. What evidence proves compliance?
5. What happens if the system changes?

If you cannot answer those, the mechanism is probably incomplete.

---

# 121. WHAT "GOOD REGULATION" LOOKS LIKE

A strong international framework should be:

### Clear

States know what is covered.

### Proportionate

Requirements fit the risk.

### Adaptable

Technology changes do not break the rules.

### Evidence-based

Compliance can be demonstrated.

### Implementable

States can actually follow it.

### Accountable

Responsibility remains traceable.

### Negotiable

Enough States can accept it.

---

# 122. WHAT "BAD REGULATION" LOOKS LIKE

Bad framework:

> "States should ensure responsible AI."

Why weak?

- responsible how?
- who?
- when?
- which systems?
- what evidence?
- what happens after failure?

Better:

> "States should define operational limits, conduct legal review, test relevant failure modes, retain evidence and reassess after material change."

Specificity is power.

---

# 123. THE "NO EMPTY PRINCIPLE" TEST

Whenever you hear:

> "States should ensure responsible autonomy."

Ask:

> "What changes operationally because of that sentence?"

If answer is:

> "Nothing specific."

You need a mechanism.

---

# 124. THE "NO MECHANISM WITHOUT PRINCIPLE" TEST

The opposite is also true.

Do not create:

> "International LAWS Registry."

and then ask later:

> "Why?"

Every mechanism should have a clear reason.

---

# 125. THE BEST WAY TO USE CASE STUDIES

Do not memorize stories.

Memorize:

**Case → problem → safeguard**

Example:

Kargu-2 discussion

→ uncertainty around autonomous operation / evidence

→ need for careful terminology, verification and attribution.

Israel AI-targeting controversy

→ uncertainty around human validation and automation

→ stronger human judgement, validation and accountability safeguards.

Loitering systems

→ mixed human/autonomous functions

→ function-based definition.

That is enough.

---

# 126. THE "TECHNOLOGY WILL CHANGE" RULE

Avoid writing rules around:

- one model;
- one platform;
- one algorithm;
- one manufacturer.

Use:

- functions;
- effects;
- control;
- predictability;
- operating conditions;
- lifecycle assurance.

This makes the framework more durable.

---

# 127. THE "NO ONE-SIZE-FITS-ALL" RULE

Do not assume:

> One control method works for every system.

Different requirements may be needed depending on:

- speed;
- target;
- environment;
- duration;
- scale;
- defensive/offensive use.

But context must not become a loophole.

The correct balance is:

> **Context-sensitive controls + fixed core principles.**

---

# 128. FIXED CORE, FLEXIBLE IMPLEMENTATION

### Fixed

- compliance with applicable law;
- human responsibility;
- no unacceptable autonomous use;
- traceability;
- lifecycle assurance.

### Flexible

- exact control mechanism;
- technical implementation;
- reporting format;
- frequency;
- institutional arrangement.

This distinction will be extremely useful later in negotiation.

---

# 129. THE "LEGALITY BEFORE OPTIMIZATION" PRINCIPLE

A system should not be justified by:

> speed;
> accuracy;
> cost;
> efficiency;

before asking:

> **Can it be used lawfully?**

Operational advantage does not override legal obligations.

---

# 130. THE "CAPABILITY DOES NOT EQUAL AUTHORITY" PRINCIPLE

A system may be technically capable of:

> selecting any target in an area.

That does not mean it should be **authorized** to do so.

This distinction between:

**what the system can do**

and

**what the system is permitted to do**

is central to regulation.

---

# 131. THE "AUTHORIZED ENVELOPE" PRINCIPLE

Think:

**Capability**

≠

**Permission**

A responsible framework asks:

> "What capabilities exist?"

and separately:

> "What actions are authorized in this mission?"

The gap between those two is where operating limits, controls and legal review operate.

---

# 132. THE "CHANGE CAN EXPAND AUTHORITY" PROBLEM

Software changes can affect not only:

> performance

but potentially:

> what the system can be authorized to do.

Example:

Old system:
- target class A.

New classifier:
- target class A + B.

That is potentially a material change.

So change review should ask:

> **Did capability expand?**

not simply:

> **Did code change?**

---

# 133. THE "EVIDENCE TRIANGLE"

For a regulated system, think in three evidence categories.

### LEGAL EVIDENCE

- legal review;
- legal obligations;
- authorization.

### TECHNICAL EVIDENCE

- testing;
- reliability;
- predictability;
- system limitations.

### OPERATIONAL EVIDENCE

- parameters;
- logs;
- training;
- interventions;
- incident records.

A mature assurance process brings all three together.

---

# 134. WHY RECORDS MATTER BEFORE AN INCIDENT

People often think logs are only for investigations.

But records also help:

- operators;
- commanders;
- legal reviewers;
- auditors;
- trainers.

If the State never records what was authorized, it cannot later show whether the system stayed within those conditions.

---

# 135. "AUDIT TRAIL" IN SIMPLE WORDS

An audit trail means:

> **a record of what happened and when.**

For LAWS:

- who authorized;
- what version;
- what limits;
- what warnings;
- what interventions;
- what outcome.

Simple.

---

# 136. THE "PROOF OF CONTROL" IDEA

Do not only ask:

> "Did the human have control?"

Ask:

> **"What evidence would prove that the human had control?"**

Possible evidence:

- authorized operating envelope;
- training records;
- interface logs;
- interventions;
- mission parameters;
- after-action review.

This question is extremely useful in debate.

---

# 137. THE "PROOF OF TESTING" IDEA

Do not only ask:

> "Was the system tested?"

Ask:

> **"Tested against what?"**

Possible:

- expected conditions;
- edge cases;
- degraded sensors;
- communications loss;
- cyber interference;
- ambiguous targets;
- interactions with other systems.

Again:

> **Evidence beats slogans.**

---

# 138. THE "PROOF OF LAWFULNESS" IDEA

Do not only ask:

> "Was there an Article 36 review?"

Ask:

> **"What did the review examine?"**

Possible:

- target identification;
- target selection;
- engagement;
- expected effects;
- operating environment;
- limitations;
- modifications.

This makes legal review meaningful instead of ceremonial.

---

# 139. THE "REVIEW IS A PROCESS, NOT A STAMP" IDEA

A legal review should not be:

> Approved = permanently lawful.

Better:

> Approved for defined conditions, subject to continued assurance and reassessment when material factors change.

This connects Article 36, lifecycle and adaptive AI.

---

# 140. THE "PROHIBITION + REGULATION" LOGIC IS NOT CONTRADICTORY

Think of aviation.

Some actions are forbidden.

Other aircraft are allowed under:

- certification;
- operating limits;
- maintenance;
- pilot training;
- records.

LAWS regulation can use a similar conceptual structure:

> **some capabilities/uses are beyond acceptable limits; others are allowed under strict conditions.**

This is only an analogy, not a legal comparison.

---

# 141. WHY INTERNATIONAL RULES ARE NEEDED

States already have:

- IHL;
- national laws;
- weapons reviews;
- military doctrine.

So why international regulation?

Because the problem includes:

- different national standards;
- technology transfer;
- arms-race incentives;
- common terminology;
- international trust;
- cross-border effects;
- shared expectations.

A future framework can reduce fragmentation.

---

# 142. BUT INTERNATIONAL LAW CANNOT SOLVE EVERYTHING

A treaty cannot automatically solve:

- poor training;
- bad data;
- broken sensors;
- bad doctrine;
- weak procurement;
- careless operators.

That is why regulation needs:

- institutions;
- national implementation;
- technical capacity;
- training;
- testing.

---

# 143. STATE IMPLEMENTATION

The international framework ultimately depends on States.

They may need:

- national review procedures;
- procurement rules;
- military instructions;
- testing programs;
- incident procedures;
- reporting mechanisms.

So every international provision should have a practical national implementation question.

---

# 144. CAPACITY-BUILDING AS A CORE PART OF FAIRNESS

Imagine a rule that requires very sophisticated:

- testing;
- technical review;
- cybersecurity;
- model evaluation.

A wealthy State may already have the necessary institutions.

A smaller State may not.

Therefore:

> **capacity-building is not charity; it can be part of making the framework genuinely implementable across States.**

---

# 145. REGIONAL COOPERATION

Regional cooperation can reduce duplication.

Potential functions:

- testing centres;
- legal-review support;
- training;
- shared experts;
- common terminology;
- confidence-building.

This is particularly useful where national technical institutions are limited.

---

# 146. THE "SECURITY VS HUMANITARIAN" FALSE CHOICE

A common debate trap is:

> "Security requires autonomy."

versus:

> "Humanitarian protection requires banning everything."

A more useful question is:

> **"Can we preserve legitimate military functions while setting boundaries that prevent unacceptable autonomous use?"**

That is where Brazil's two-tier architecture fits.

---

# 147. THE "MILITARY UTILITY" QUESTION

If a delegate says:

> "Autonomous systems save soldiers' lives."

Acknowledge the point.

Then ask:

- under what circumstances?
- what target?
- what environment?
- what safeguards?
- what happens when conditions change?
- what evidence supports the claimed reliability?

The existence of military utility does not automatically answer the legal or accountability questions.

---

# 148. THE "OPERATIONAL REALISM" TEST

Every proposed rule should survive:

### SPEED

Can humans actually do it in time?

### INFORMATION

Does the human have enough information?

### TECHNOLOGY

Can the system enforce the rule?

### SECURITY

Does implementation expose unacceptable vulnerabilities?

### COST

Can States realistically implement it?

### LEGALITY

Does it support compliance?

A good proposal survives all six.

---

# 149. THE "MUN OUT-OF-THE-BOX" TEST

The Chair will like solutions more when they connect multiple problems.

Example:

### Material-change registry

Solves:

- lifecycle problem;
- software-change problem;
- accountability problem;
- verification problem.

### Protected technical panel

Solves:

- verification problem;
- sovereignty concern;
- confidentiality concern;
- trust deficit.

### Regional assurance hub

Solves:

- capacity gap;
- testing gap;
- training gap;
- implementation inequality.

That's what "out-of-the-box" should mean.

# 150. THE AGENDA'S BIGGEST TRAP

The biggest trap is to turn LAWS into a philosophical debate with no operational content.

Statements like:

> "Humanity must remain at the centre."

are fine as rhetoric.

But the committee eventually asks:

> **How?**

Answer with:

- target limits;
- operating envelopes;
- legal review;
- testing;
- intervention;
- logs;
- incident investigation.

---

# 151. THE SECOND BIGGEST TRAP

The opposite mistake:

> Make the framework so technical that nobody knows what political principle it serves.

Every technical mechanism should connect back to:

- civilian protection;
- lawful use;
- human judgement;
- accountability;
- international security.

---

# 152. THE THIRD BIGGEST TRAP

Trying to define every possible future technology today.

You cannot.

Instead:

> regulate functions and risk conditions.

That is why technology-neutral language matters.

---

# 153. THE "FOUR LEVELS OF LANGUAGE"

In debate, you can speak at four levels.

### LEVEL 1 — Simple

> "Humans need real control."

### LEVEL 2 — Policy

### LEVEL 3 — Technical

> "Material changes to target-selection logic should trigger renewed testing and legal review."

### LEVEL 4 — Institutional

> "States could share assurance methodologies through a protected technical review mechanism under the CCW process."

A strong delegate moves between levels depending on who is listening.

# 163. THE MASTER "WHY?" CHAIN

## Why regulate LAWS?

Because autonomous lethal decisions raise legal, humanitarian and security concerns.

## Why human control?

Because humans remain responsible for lawful use of force.

## Why limits?

Because broad autonomy can make human control only theoretical.

## Why testing?

Because claimed control must be supported by evidence.

## Why lifecycle review?

Because technology and missions change.

## Why traceability?

Because accountability needs records.

## Why verification?

Because international rules need credibility.

## Why capacity-building?

Because States need the ability to implement the rules.

## Why institutions?

Because the framework needs a place to continue evolving.

# 164. THE MASTER "WHAT CAN GO WRONG?" MAP

### Definition failure

We regulate the wrong systems.

### Legal failure

We assume IHL alone answers every implementation problem.

### Control failure

Human authority becomes nominal.

### Technical failure

System behaviour is not sufficiently predictable.

### Lifecycle failure

Changes escape review.

### Accountability failure

Nobody can reconstruct the decision chain.

### Verification failure

Either no evidence or unrealistic disclosure.

### Proliferation failure

Systems move to unauthorized actors.

### Capacity failure

Some States cannot implement the regime.

### Institutional failure

The process gets stuck without a next step.

A complete framework tries to close all ten.
