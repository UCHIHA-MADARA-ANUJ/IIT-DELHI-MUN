# 03 — LEGAL + TECHNICAL AMMUNITION

> **Purpose:** turn the legal and technical side of LAWS into answers you can actually use in committee.
>
> The goal is **not** to memorize 30 pages of theory.
> The goal is to be able to move from:
>
> **LEGAL RULE / TECHNICAL FACT → LAWS PROBLEM → PRACTICAL SAFEGUARD → BRAZIL POSITION**
>
> For almost every technical/legal POI, use:
>
> **DEFINE → EXPLAIN THE RISK → GIVE THE RULE → GIVE THE SAFEGUARD**

# 0. PART 3 — THE MASTER IDEA

Brazil does **not** need to argue:

> "AI is dangerous."

Brazil needs to argue:

> **"Existing international humanitarian law applies, but autonomous functions create specific problems of predictability, human judgement, control, accountability and lifecycle assurance. International rules should therefore prohibit clearly unacceptable autonomous uses and strictly regulate permissible autonomy."**

That distinction is the backbone of your entire technical debate.

Memorize:

**IHL APPLIES**
→ autonomy creates specific governance problems
→ **PROHIBIT** unacceptable uses
→ **REGULATE** permissible uses
→ **ASSURE** the system throughout its lifecycle
→ **ACCOUNT** for what happens
→ **VERIFY** compliance realistically

# 1. LAWS: KNOW EXACTLY WHAT YOU ARE TALKING ABOUT

## 1.1 No single universal definition

Do **not** say:

> "The UN defines LAWS as..."

There is no single universally accepted definition covering every system.

The debate usually focuses on **autonomy in critical functions**, especially target selection and engagement.

The ICRC generally describes autonomous weapons as systems that, once activated, can select and engage one or more targets without further human intervention.

The 2026 CCW process also worked with a capability/function-oriented characterization rather than a simple platform label.

## 1.2 Automation ≠ autonomy

**Automation**
= a machine automatically performs a defined task.

**Autonomy**
= after activation, a system can perform a function using information from its environment without a person directing each individual action.

Examples:

- autonomous navigation + human-controlled engagement;
- automated target tracking + human target selection;
- autonomous target selection + autonomous engagement.

The last category is obviously much more central to the LAWS debate.

## 1.3 AI ≠ LAWS

AI can be used for:

- intelligence analysis;
- image recognition;
- logistics;
- planning;
- decision support;
- cyber defence;
- target recommendation.

That does **not** automatically make the system a LAWS.

Likewise, autonomy can exist without modern machine learning.

### Brazil line

> **"Brazil supports capability-based regulation. We should regulate the autonomous function that creates the risk, not simply label every drone, missile or AI system as a LAWS."**

---

# 2. IHL IS THE LEGAL BASELINE

Your strongest legal position starts here:

> **International humanitarian law already applies to the use of weapons and means/methods of warfare. The policy question is whether specific autonomous functions require additional rules.**

Do not accidentally make these three claims identical:

### Claim A
Existing IHL applies.

### Claim B
A particular autonomous system may be unlawful in particular circumstances.

### Claim C
States may negotiate additional prohibitions/restrictions specifically for autonomous weapons.

All three can be true at the same time.

This is much stronger than saying:

> "IHL is not enough."

Say:

> **"Existing IHL remains the legal foundation; specific rules can complement it by addressing autonomy-related risks that existing rules do not regulate in an operationally detailed way."**

---

# 3. THE FOUR IHL CONCEPTS YOU MUST NEVER MIX UP

## 3.1 Distinction

Attacks must be directed against lawful military objectives.

### LAWS problem

An autonomous system may have to:

- detect;
- classify;
- track;
- select.

Errors can arise from:

- ambiguous objects;
- civilian presence;
- changing circumstances;
- degraded sensors;
- bad or manipulated data.

### Brazil line

> **"The issue is not whether a machine can recognize patterns. The issue is whether the system can support lawful distinction in the actual environment in which it is used."**

---

## 3.2 Proportionality

Expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage.

### LAWS problem

This is highly context-sensitive.

The machine may process data quickly, but the legal issue is whether the information and system behaviour are sufficiently reliable for the circumstances of the attack.

### POI

**"Why can't an algorithm simply calculate proportionality?"**

### Answer

> **"The challenge is not computational ability alone. The challenge is whether the system has sufficiently reliable information, context and predictability to support the proportionality judgement required in that operation."**

Never say:

> "AI cannot understand proportionality."

That is too absolute.

---

## 3.3 Precautions in attack

Those who plan or decide upon attacks must take feasible precautions to avoid or minimize civilian harm.

### LAWS problem

Precautions cannot become a one-time checkbox.

They can include:

- appropriate operating limits;
- testing;
- target restrictions;
- geographic restrictions;
- time limits;
- monitoring;
- intervention/deactivation arrangements;
- responses to changed conditions.

### Brazil line

> **"Precautions should be built into the conditions of autonomous operation, not reduced to a signature before deployment."**

---

## 3.4 Indiscriminate attacks

A system raises a fundamental legality problem if it cannot be directed against a specific military objective or cannot limit its effects as required by IHL.

### Brazil line

> **"Where a system cannot reliably constrain the application of force to what IHL permits, the problem may move from regulation to prohibition."**

This is one of the cleanest links between:

**IHL → prohibition tier**

# 4. ARTICLE 36 — KNOW THIS COLD

## What is Article 36?

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a State Party, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would be prohibited by the Protocol or another applicable rule of international law.

## The critical caveat

Do **not** say:

> "Article 36 binds every UN Member State."

Say:

> **"States Parties to Additional Protocol I have an Article 36 treaty obligation."**

That distinction matters.

## Why Article 36 matters for LAWS

Autonomous systems can depend on:

- software;
- models;
- sensors;
- target-classification logic;
- operating environments;
- update cycles.

So legal review requires an understanding of how the system is expected to behave in its intended/expected circumstances of use.

## What Article 36 does NOT automatically solve

Article 36 is a legal-review mechanism.

It does not, by itself, create detailed international rules for:

- human-control conditions;
- operating envelopes;
- lifecycle change management;
- common verification;
- international incident reporting;
- procurement standards;
- shared technical assurance.

### POI

**"Article 36 already exists. Why create new LAWS rules?"**

### Answer

> **"Article 36 is essential, but it is a review obligation. It does not by itself establish the international operational standards, prohibitions, lifecycle safeguards and accountability mechanisms that States are debating for autonomous systems."**

# 5. THE HUMAN-CONTROL ISSUE

## 5.1 Do not overclaim

Meaningful human control is a major policy concept.

Do **not** casually say:

> "Meaningful human control is already a universally codified treaty obligation."

It is not a single universally accepted treaty definition.

The 2026 GGE discussions instead focused on **context-appropriate human judgement and control** and on making human involvement practically effective.

## 5.2 The six-part mental model

Memorize:

**UNDERSTAND**
→ **PREDICT**
→ **LIMIT**
→ **DECIDE**
→ **INTERVENE**
→ **ACCOUNT**

### Understand
The human needs sufficient information about:

- the system;
- its limitations;
- the operating environment;
- the relevant target/context.

### Predict
The State/operator needs enough confidence about likely system behaviour in the approved environment.

### Limit
Autonomy should operate within meaningful constraints:

- target;
- geography;
- time;
- scale;
- environment.

### Decide
A human must retain a real decision-making role appropriate to the context.

### Intervene
Where technically and operationally feasible, there should be a genuine capacity to:

- modify;
- stop;
- deactivate.

### Account
Responsibility must remain attributable to people and State institutions.

# 6. DIRECT VS INDIRECT HUMAN CONTROL

This is a BIG POI area.

## Direct control

A human authorizes each critical engagement.

### Benefit
Very clear human role.

### Problem
May be infeasible for:

- extremely fast defensive systems;
- communications-constrained situations;
- environments where reaction time is shorter than human decision time.

## Indirect / contextual control

Humans set conditions and the system operates autonomously within them.

Possible conditions:

- defined target category;
- defined geography;
- defined duration;
- limited scale;
- tested environment;
- deactivation/intervention conditions.

### Benefit
More workable in some real operational contexts.

### Risk
Human control can become nominal if the limits are too broad.

### Brazil bridge

> **"Brazil supports context-appropriate human judgement and control: flexible enough to account for operational realities, but concrete enough to ensure that human control has practical effect."**

This is much stronger than blindly demanding:

> "A human must press the button every time."

---

# 7. PREDICTABILITY — YOUR BEST TECHNICAL WORD

Predictability does **not** mean perfection.

It means sufficient understanding and assurance that the system can be used lawfully under the conditions for which it was approved.

Factors that can reduce predictability:

- unfamiliar environments;
- degraded sensors;
- unexpected target patterns;
- software/model changes;
- adversarial inputs;
- communications loss;
- interaction with other autonomous systems;
- swarm behaviour.

### Brazil line

> **"Predictability does not require perfection; it requires sufficient assurance for lawful use in the intended operating environment."**

This line is extremely useful because it avoids two extremes:

**"AI is perfectly predictable."**
and
**"AI is inherently unpredictable."**

Neither is a useful blanket claim.

---

# 8. EXPLAINABILITY + BLACK BOXES

## Why does explainability matter?

Operators and reviewers need enough understanding to assess:

- capabilities;
- limitations;
- likely behaviour;
- uncertainty;
- failure modes.

A highly opaque system can make:

- legal review harder;
- operator judgement harder;
- investigation harder;
- accountability harder.

## But do NOT say:

> "Every black-box model must be banned."

That jumps too far.

A more workable question is:

> **"Can the system be sufficiently understood and assured for the task and environment for which it is being used?"**

## Important confidentiality point

International assurance does **not** have to mean public release of:

- source code;
- military vulnerabilities;
- proprietary model weights;
- sensitive operational details.

Possible alternatives:

- documented limitations;
- assurance evidence;
- confidential expert review;
- protected testing information;
- audit trails.

### Brazil line

> **"The objective is sufficient technical understanding for legal assurance and responsible operation, not public disclosure of sensitive military source code."**

---

# 9. AUTOMATION BIAS — THE HUMAN IS NOT MAGIC

This is one of the most important concepts from the Background Guide.

A system can be formally:

**human-in-the-loop**

while being practically:

**machine-determined.**

Why?

- time pressure;
- huge recommendation volume;
- unclear uncertainty;
- operator overconfidence in the system;
- institutional pressure;
- inadequate training;
- inability to meaningfully override.

### The key question

Not:

> "Was a human present?"

But:

> **"Could the human actually exercise judgement?"**

### Brazil safeguards

- operator training;
- visible uncertainty/error indicators;
- workload controls;
- meaningful override authority;
- logging;
- additional review for high-risk situations where appropriate.

### Killer line

> **"A human signature is not automatically meaningful human judgement."**

Or:

> **"A rubber stamp is not human control."**

---

# 10. AI DECISION SUPPORT ≠ LAWS

An AI system may:

- fuse sensor feeds;
- prioritize objects;
- recommend targets;
- accelerate intelligence analysis.

That does **not** automatically make it a LAWS.

But the interface matters.

Possible chain:

**AI recommendation**
→ **human approval**
→ **force applied**

The human may technically approve while practically following the machine.

So Brazil should avoid two mistakes:

### Mistake 1
Treat all military AI as LAWS.

### Mistake 2
Pretend AI decision-support has no relevance to human control.

### Brazil line

> **"The legal scope should focus on relevant autonomous weapon functions, while addressing the human-agency risks created when AI decision-support materially shapes lethal targeting decisions."**

---

# 11. ADAPTIVE / SELF-LEARNING SYSTEMS

This is where normal weapon review becomes more complicated.

A system can change through:

- model updates;
- retraining;
- altered target classifiers;
- new sensor fusion;
- changed engagement logic;
- autonomy-mode changes;
- online adaptation.

## The central question

> **When does a change become material enough to require renewed legal and technical assurance?**

A sensible trigger is a change affecting:

- target selection;
- effects;
- operating conditions;
- autonomy level;
- major safety/assurance assumptions.

## Strong Brazil position

> **"For adaptive autonomy, legal review should function as continuing assurance rather than a one-time certificate."**

Be careful with wording.

Do not imply that Article 36 itself universally creates a detailed continuous-review procedure for every State.

Instead say:

> **"Brazil supports a framework in which material changes trigger renewed legal and technical assurance, building on existing legal-review practice."**

---

# 12. TESTING: WHERE THEORY BECOMES EVIDENCE

A system should not only be tested in ideal conditions.

Useful test categories:

### Normal conditions
Expected operating environment.

### Edge cases
Unusual but foreseeable situations.

### Degraded conditions
- poor sensors;
- communications loss;
- environmental degradation.

### Hostile conditions
- spoofing;
- jamming;
- cyber intrusion;
- adversarial inputs.

### Civilian-complexity conditions
- unexpected civilian presence;
- ambiguous objects;
- changing circumstances.

### System interaction
- multiple autonomous systems;
- swarm behaviour;
- unexpected interaction effects.

### Change testing
Material software/model updates.

### Core principle

**LEGAL ASSUMPTION**
→ testing
→ evidence
→ approved operating conditions

### Brazil line

> **"Testing is the bridge between what a legal review assumes and what a system actually does."**

---

# 13. THE AUTONOMY OPERATING ENVELOPE

This is one of your best drafting concepts.

Instead of writing:

> "Ensure human control."

Define the conditions within which the system may operate.

An operating envelope can specify:

- **TARGET** — what category may be engaged;
- **SPACE** — where it may operate;
- **TIME** — how long autonomy may remain active;
- **SCALE** — how many/what volume of engagements are permitted;
- **ENVIRONMENT** — what conditions were tested;
- **COMMUNICATIONS** — what command-link conditions are required;
- **INTERVENTION** — when/how humans must be able to stop or restrict the system.

### Core drafting idea

> **"A regulated autonomous system may operate only within a predefined, tested and legally reviewed operating envelope."**

Outside the envelope:

> **the system should not continue autonomous lethal engagement unless the framework explicitly provides a lawful, tested and context-appropriate exception.**

Do not invent a universal fail-safe mode for every system.

---

# 14. COMMUNICATIONS LOSS / FAIL-SAFE BEHAVIOUR

Possible failures:

- command link lost;
- GPS/GNSS unavailable;
- sensor quality falls;
- communications disrupted;
- operator cannot intervene.

Possible context-dependent responses:

- abort;
- deactivate;
- safe return;
- loiter;
- restricted mode.

The correct policy depends on:

- weapon;
- environment;
- mission;
- reaction time;
- risk.

### Brazil line

> **"The framework should require tested, context-appropriate behaviour when critical assumptions fail, rather than prescribing one identical default for every autonomous weapon."**

---

# 15. CYBER + ADVERSARIAL INPUTS

A system that performs well in a laboratory may behave differently under attack or interference.

Consider:

- spoofing;
- jamming;
- adversarial inputs;
- sensor manipulation;
- cyber intrusion;
- data corruption;
- conflicting sensor feeds.

### The regulatory implication

Testing and assurance must cover the conditions that can cause the approved assumptions to fail.

### POI answer

**"Why does cybersecurity belong in a LAWS treaty?"**

> **"Because an autonomous system relies on data, sensors and communications. If those inputs can be manipulated or degraded, the system's predictability, target selection and ability to remain within its approved operating conditions can also be affected."**

---

# 16. SWARMS — THE PROBLEM IS SYSTEM-OF-SYSTEMS CONTROL

A swarm is not merely "many drones."

The governance issue is that:

- multiple systems may function as one mission system;
- interaction can change aggregate behaviour;
- engagement volume can grow quickly;
- accountability can become distributed;
- platform-by-platform testing may miss system-level risks.

Possible regulatory tools:

- maximum swarm/engagement scale;
- geographic limits;
- engagement-rate limits;
- target restrictions;
- human authorization for expansion of target sets;
- system-wide logs;
- tested emergency-abort behaviour.

Do not automatically argue:

> "All swarms must be banned."

The better question is:

> **"What system-level safeguards are needed to keep aggregate behaviour predictable, controllable and accountable?"**

---

# 17. ESCALATION / "FLASH WAR"

Autonomy can compress the time available for:

- interpretation;
- communication;
- human intervention;
- de-escalation.

Multiple systems interacting rapidly can increase the problem.

But do NOT say:

> "LAWS will definitely cause a flash war."

That overstates the evidence.

Say:

> **"Autonomous systems can create escalation pathways by compressing decision time and accelerating interaction, so safeguards should address communication loss, engagement rates, deconfliction and human intervention."**

That is defensible.

---

# 18. ACCOUNTABILITY — MACHINES DO NOT BECOME THE LEGAL ACTOR

The governance problem is not simply:

> "Who pressed the button?"

It is the whole responsibility chain.

Possible relevant actors:

- political authorities;
- commanders;
- mission planners;
- operators;
- procurement authorities;
- technical personnel;
- suppliers.

Do not assume every actor has identical legal responsibility.

Instead ask:

> **"Can the State identify who authorized, configured, deployed, supervised and investigated the use?"**

---

# 19. TRACEABILITY

For an autonomous incident, you want enough evidence to reconstruct:

### WHO
authorized the mission?

### WHAT
system/configuration was used?

### WHERE
did it operate?

### UNDER WHAT LIMITS
did it operate?

### WHAT CHANGED
before or during the mission?

### WHAT HAPPENED
after the incident?

Potential evidence:

- authorization records;
- active parameters;
- system/model version;
- warnings;
- interventions;
- logs;
- incident records.

The objective is **accountability through traceability**, not indiscriminate disclosure.

---

# 20. VERIFICATION — DO NOT JUMP STRAIGHT TO "INSPECT EVERYTHING"

A realistic verification system can use a ladder.

## Level 1 — National processes
- legal review;
- testing;
- certification;
- command procedures.

## Level 2 — Transparency
- national frameworks;
- aggregate information;
- implementation reports.

## Level 3 — Incident reporting
- serious autonomous incidents;
- loss of control;
- major unexpected engagement;
- significant system failure.

## Level 4 — Expert exchange
States compare:

- testing methodologies;
- review procedures;
- terminology;
- lessons learned.

## Level 5 — Auditable records
States retain evidence for later investigation/review.

## Level 6 — Protected review
Confidential expert review or narrow verification where politically and operationally feasible.

### Key principle

> **"Verification should test compliance without demanding unrealistic disclosure of sensitive military information."**

This creates a bridge between States worried about sovereignty/security and States demanding accountability.

---

# 21. PROCUREMENT — YOUR PRACTICAL GOVERNANCE LEVER

Procurement is where broad international promises can become concrete requirements.

A procurement framework could require:

- legal-review documentation;
- test evidence;
- technical documentation;
- cybersecurity controls;
- configuration management;
- change notification;
- auditability;
- incident support.

## Why it matters

If the military buyer does not know:

- what a system can do;
- how it was tested;
- what changes after delivery;
- how incidents are investigated;

then the international rule becomes difficult to implement.

### Brazil line

> **"Compliance should begin before deployment. Procurement is a practical gate through which States can require legal review, testing, cybersecurity, change control and accountability."**

---

# 22. SUPPLIERS + PRIVATE TECHNOLOGY

States remain the key actors under international law.

But contracts can require suppliers to:

- document system limitations;
- disclose material changes;
- preserve audit rights;
- maintain update security;
- support incident investigations;
- prevent unauthorized modification.

This is especially important where systems depend on:

- commercial software;
- AI models;
- sensors;
- data;
- communications;
- robotics.

### Do not say

> "Companies are internationally legally responsible in the same way as States."

Instead:

> **"Supplier obligations can support States in fulfilling their international responsibilities."**

---

# 23. PROLIFERATION + NON-STATE RISKS

Relevant pathways include:

- commercial drones;
- open-source software;
- off-the-shelf sensors;
- illicit procurement;
- stolen/captured systems;
- transfer of autonomy software;
- modification of civilian platforms.

Potential tools:

- end-use controls;
- transfer controls;
- secure software/update practices;
- reporting of loss/theft;
- cooperation against diversion;
- restrictions on transfer of prohibited systems.

### Brazil line

> **"A framework should address not only State deployment, but also the pathways through which high-risk autonomous capabilities can be transferred, modified or diverted."**

---

# 24. CAPACITY-BUILDING

Do not build a regime that only technologically advanced militaries can implement.

Possible support:

- legal-review assistance;
- technical testing;
- cybersecurity training;
- procurement guidance;
- expert exchanges;
- regional testing hubs.

### Diplomatic value for Brazil

This connects the technical debate to:

**capacity-building + equity + implementation**

without turning the debate into a generic development speech.

### Brazil line

> **"Universal rules require realistic implementation capacity. Legal obligations without technical assistance can leave a major compliance gap."**

---

# 25. DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS — HANDLE THIS CAREFULLY

A common attack:

> "What about systems that have almost no time for human reaction?"

Do not answer with a blanket ban.

Instead say:

> **"The regulatory test should be contextual: what target is involved, what environment is involved, how bounded is the autonomy, how predictable are the effects, and what human constraints exist?"**

This keeps Brazil practical.

A defensive system may justify:

- indirect/contextual human control;
- strict operating limits;
- tightly bounded target classes;
- tested environments;
- fail-safe behaviour.

But "defensive" should not automatically mean "safe."

# 26. CASE STUDIES — NEVER USE THEM AS CHEAP RHETORIC

## Libya / Kargu-2
Use the UN reporting carefully.

Say:

> "The Libya case is relevant to debates about autonomous functions and uncertainty in attribution of actual autonomy."

Do not say:

> "The UN proved all Kargu-2 systems autonomously killed people."

That goes beyond the evidence.

## Nagorno-Karabakh
Useful for:

- varying levels of autonomy;
- loitering systems;
- target-selection questions;
- functional definitions.

Do not equate every loitering munition with LAWS.

## AI-enabled targeting
The Lavender/Habsora debate is useful for:

- AI decision support;
- human approval;
- automation bias;
- validation/approval requirements.

Do not present disputed claims as settled proof of LAWS deployment.

## Defensive systems
Phalanx/Aegis can illustrate:

- reaction-time constraints;
- defensive contexts;
- bounded autonomy.

Do not say:

> "Defensive autonomy is automatically safe."

# 27. BRAZIL'S TECHNICAL FRAMEWORK — THE SIMPLE VERSION

When you are drafting, think in two tiers.

## TIER 1 — PROHIBIT

Prohibit autonomous uses/systems where the State cannot ensure the necessary baseline of:

- lawful target restriction;
- adequate predictability;
- meaningful human judgement/control;
- ability to constrain effects;
- accountability.

Also consider clear prohibited categories/uses where autonomy creates unacceptable risks.

## TIER 2 — STRICTLY REGULATE

For systems that remain permissible:

### Before use
- legal review;
- technical assurance;
- testing;
- operating envelope;
- trained personnel.

### During use
- human judgement/control;
- bounded target/space/time/scale;
- monitoring;
- intervention/deactivation where feasible.

### After use
- logs;
- incident review;
- investigation;
- accountability.

### After change
- reassessment when material changes affect critical functions.

That gives you a complete chain:

**PROHIBIT**
→ **REVIEW**
→ **TEST**
→ **BOUND**
→ **CONTROL**
→ **TRACE**
→ **INVESTIGATE**
→ **REASSESS**

# 29. THE 12 POIs YOU SHOULD BE ABLE TO ANSWER

## POI 1
**"Isn't existing IHL already sufficient?"**

> "IHL remains the legal foundation. The question is whether specific rules are needed to make autonomy-related safeguards, prohibitions, lifecycle assurance and accountability operational."

## POI 2
**"Does Brazil want to ban all AI weapons?"**

> "No. Brazil's approach is capability-based: prohibit clearly unacceptable autonomous uses and strictly regulate permissible autonomy. Military AI as a whole is not synonymous with LAWS."

## POI 3
**"What does meaningful human control actually mean?"**

> "It means human judgement must have practical effect. That requires sufficient understanding, predictability, operational limits, decision authority and intervention or deactivation capability where feasible."

## POI 4
**"Can indirect control really be human control?"**

> "Yes, where autonomy is tightly bounded and humans retain meaningful authority and practical control over the circumstances of use. Indirect control cannot become a purely formal authorization."

## POI 5
**"Does Article 36 already cover everything?"**

> "It is an essential legal-review mechanism, but it does not by itself create common international rules for autonomous operating limits, lifecycle change, verification and accountability."

## POI 6
**"How do you verify compliance without exposing military secrets?"**

> "Use a layered model: national review, transparency, incident reporting, expert exchange, auditable records and protected review where appropriate."

## POI 7
**"What happens if communications are lost?"**

> "The system should follow a tested, context-appropriate response defined before deployment — such as abort, deactivate, restricted mode or another safe behaviour appropriate to the environment."

## POI 8
**"Are AI targeting systems LAWS?"**

> "Not automatically. AI decision support and autonomous weapon functions are distinct, but automation bias and human-agency risks at their interface require governance."

## POI 9
**"Why regulate software updates?"**

> "Because a material change can alter the system's function, effects, operating conditions or autonomy. The reviewed system must remain within its lawful and technically assured assumptions."

## POI 10
**"Why regulate swarms?"**

> "Because system-level behaviour can differ from platform-level behaviour. Scale, interaction and engagement rates can affect predictability and accountability."

## POI 11
**"Why involve procurement?"**

> "Because procurement is where legal review, testing, cybersecurity, change management and supplier requirements can be turned into enforceable national procedures."

## POI 12
**"Why not simply require one human to approve everything?"**

> "Because a formal approval is not enough. The human must have the information, time, authority and practical ability to exercise judgement."

# 31. THE 8 THINGS YOU MUST NEVER SAY CASUALLY

## TRAP 1
> "Meaningful human control is already a universally binding treaty rule."

**Why wrong:** no single universally accepted treaty formulation.

## TRAP 2
> "Article 36 applies identically to every UN Member State."

**Why wrong:** its treaty obligation comes from Additional Protocol I.

## TRAP 3
> "Every AI targeting tool is LAWS."

**Why wrong:** decision support and autonomous weapon functions are not identical.

## TRAP 4
> "Every loitering munition is autonomous."

**Why wrong:** systems differ in autonomy and human involvement.

## TRAP 5
> "Defensive systems are safe."

**Why wrong:** defensive context changes the analysis but does not remove governance questions.

## TRAP 6
> "LAWS will definitely cause a flash war."

**Why wrong:** escalation is a risk pathway, not an inevitable outcome.

## TRAP 7
> "Verification means publishing source code."

**Why wrong:** assurance can use protected/confidential mechanisms.

## TRAP 8
> "The 2026 GGE created a treaty."

**Why wrong:** its mandate concerned elements of an instrument **without prejudging its nature**; the GGE outcome is not itself a treaty.

# 36. THE LEGAL-TECHNICAL AMMUNITION SYSTEM

Part 3 should not be studied as isolated facts.

Use this sequence for almost every difficult question:

**TERM**
→ What does the term mean?

**LAW**
→ What legal rule or principle is relevant?

**RISK**
→ What problem does autonomy create?

**EVIDENCE**
→ What would we need to know to assess it?

**SAFEGUARD**
→ What can States actually do?

**BRAZIL**
→ What does Brazil support?

### Example

**Term:** autonomy

**Law:** IHL applies to means and methods of warfare.

**Risk:** human decision-making may be delegated too far.

**Evidence:** system behaviour, operating limits, testing, logs.

**Safeguard:** human judgement/control + operating envelope + review.

**Brazil:** prohibit unacceptable autonomy and strictly regulate the rest.

That is the basic machine for answering POIs.

---

# 37. THE MOST IMPORTANT LEGAL DISTINCTION: LAW VS POLICY

This is one of the easiest ways to accidentally overclaim.

### Existing legal rule

Example:

> IHL applies to weapons and their use.

### Interpretation / application

Example:

> A State argues that effective human judgement is needed to ensure compliance with IHL.

### Policy proposal

Example:

> States should adopt a specific prohibition on a category of autonomous systems.

### Negotiating text

Example:

> A draft GGE paragraph proposes a particular control or reporting mechanism.

These are four different things.

### Golden rule

> **Never turn a policy proposal into a statement of existing law.**

---

# 38. "PROHIBITED BY IHL" VS "SHOULD BE PROHIBITED"

These are also different.

### "Prohibited by IHL"

Means an existing legal rule already makes the conduct unlawful.

### "Should be prohibited"

Means the speaker is advocating a new rule or clearer restriction.

Brazil can say:

> "Certain systems may be incapable of lawful use and should be prohibited."

That is safer than pretending that every proposed prohibition already exists universally.

---

# 39. "IHL APPLIES" DOES NOT ANSWER EVERY QUESTION

A delegate may say:

> "IHL already applies, so we don't need another framework."

The correct response is not:

> "IHL doesn't work."

Instead:

> **"IHL applies, but States still disagree about how existing rules apply to specific autonomous functions. Specific international rules can reduce uncertainty and create common safeguards."**

The 2026 GGE's own Box II work reaffirmed that IHL applies fully to LAWS and that legal obligations apply to States and individuals, not machines. citeturn193042view0

The ICRC's 2026 position paper similarly argues that strong legal review is essential but not by itself a sufficient response to all the humanitarian, legal and ethical questions raised by autonomous weapons. citeturn280913search27turn893256view0

---

# 40. WHY IHL DOES NOT BECOME OBSOLETE

This is another trap.

Do not say:

> "Autonomous weapons make IHL irrelevant."

The opposite is closer to the truth.

Autonomy makes it even more important to ask:

- Can distinction be respected?
- Can proportionality be assessed?
- Can precautions be taken?
- Can responsibility remain attributable?
- Can the human operator understand the system?

The challenge is **application**, not disappearance of the legal framework.

---

# 41. DISTINCTION — DEEPER TEST

A simple definition:

> Distinction requires parties to distinguish between civilians/civilian objects and military objectives and to direct attacks accordingly.

For autonomous systems, ask:

### Identification

Can the system identify the relevant object or person?

### Classification

Can it distinguish the legally relevant category?

### Context

Can the system account for circumstances that change status?

### Targeting

Does the system make the final selection?

### Reassessment

Can the decision change if the situation changes?

### Consequence

Can the use still comply with IHL?

### Key insight

> **Target recognition is not automatically the same thing as lawful target identification.**

A computer can recognize a vehicle.

That does not answer every legal question about whether attacking that vehicle is lawful.

---

# 42. DISTINCTION — THE SENSOR PROBLEM

Sensors can observe:

- shape;
- heat;
- movement;
- radar signature;
- electromagnetic emissions.

But legal status can depend on:

- function;
- status;
- behaviour;
- circumstances;
- changes over time.

Therefore technical classification is only part of the process.

### Brazil line

> "Brazil is concerned not only with whether the sensor can recognize an object, but whether the system can support a lawful targeting decision in the circumstances in which force is applied."

---

# 43. PROPORTIONALITY — DEEPER TEST

Proportionality is not a simple damage prediction exercise.

A serious analysis may require considering:

- expected civilian harm;
- expected military advantage;
- timing;
- surrounding civilian presence;
- weapon effects;
- foreseeable system behaviour.

### LAWS problem

If the system's behaviour is uncertain, the user may have less confidence about expected effects.

### Brazil line

> "Predictability matters because proportionality requires a reasonable assessment of expected civilian harm."

Do not say:

> "An AI can never do proportionality."

The legal question is more nuanced than that.

---

# 44. PRECAUTIONS — DEEPER TEST

The question is:

> **What feasible steps can be taken to reduce harm before and during the attack?**

For autonomous systems this can include:

- target restrictions;
- geographic restrictions;
- time restrictions;
- environmental restrictions;
- weapon-mode restrictions;
- monitoring;
- cancellation;
- intervention.

### Important

Precautions can happen **before activation**, not only after the system starts acting.

That is why mission planning and operating envelopes matter.

---

# 45. INDISCRIMINATE ATTACKS

A simple mental distinction:

### Distinction

Can you tell lawful targets from protected people/objects?

### Indiscriminate use

Is the method or attack unable to distinguish or is it employed in a way prohibited by IHL?

Autonomy raises questions about whether system behaviour can be sufficiently controlled and constrained.

### Brazil line

> "If the system's behaviour cannot be adequately constrained to meet IHL requirements, that is not merely a technical problem; it can become a legal problem."

---

# 46. MILITARY OBJECTIVES DO NOT END THE ANALYSIS

Suppose the target is a military objective.

You still need to ask:

- Is the target correctly identified?
- Is the attack proportionate?
- Are precautions feasible?
- Could circumstances have changed?
- Are effects sufficiently predictable?
- Is the system operating within its authorized limits?

### Memory line

> **"Military objective" is a legal category, not a blank cheque.**

---

# 47. ARTICLE 36 — EXACT STRUCTURE

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I concerns legal review of new weapons, means and methods of warfare.

The core review question is whether employment of the new weapon, means or method would be prohibited in some or all circumstances by:

- the Protocol;
- other applicable international law.

### Key legal point

The treaty obligation belongs to **States Parties to Additional Protocol I**.

Do not universalize it.

---

# 48. ARTICLE 36 — WHAT DOES A GOOD REVIEW NEED?

A useful review needs enough understanding of the system to assess:

- capabilities;
- limitations;
- normal/expected circumstances;
- expected effects;
- legal constraints.

The ICRC's 2026 position paper says a reviewer should be satisfied about legality in the system's normal or expected circumstances and manner of use, and that the system should be understandable, predictable and explainable enough for that assessment. citeturn280913search27turn893256view0

### Simple version

> **"You can't legally review what you cannot sufficiently understand."**

---

# 49. ARTICLE 36 — THE MODIFICATION PROBLEM

A new review may be needed when a weapon is modified in a way that alters its function or effects.

The ICRC specifically points to modifications affecting selection or engagement functions, including relevant machine-learning software changes. citeturn280913search27turn893256view0

### This gives you a powerful answer

> "If a material change creates a materially different system, the assurance process should respond to that change."

Do not say every line of code requires a new legal review.

Focus on **material changes**.

---

# 50. ARTICLE 36 — REVIEW IS NOT CERTIFICATION

This is a useful distinction.

### Certification model

> "Pass once, then forget it."

### Lifecycle review

> "Review for intended conditions, monitor, and reassess when material circumstances or functions change."

Brazil's later solution architecture should favour the second model.

---

# 51. ARTICLE 36 — WHAT IT DOES NOT SOLVE

Even strong national legal review does not automatically solve:

- international definition disagreements;
- common prohibition thresholds;
- shared reporting;
- verification;
- cross-border transfers;
- capacity differences;
- treaty negotiations.

Therefore Article 36 should be viewed as:

> **one building block, not the entire international regime.**

---

# 52. HUMAN JUDGEMENT — THE SIX-PART DEEP TEST

Use:

### 1. UNDERSTAND

Does the human understand relevant capabilities and limitations?

### 2. PREDICT

Can the human reasonably anticipate behaviour in expected conditions?

### 3. LIMIT

Can the human establish target/time/geographic/other operational boundaries?

### 4. DECIDE

Does the human make the relevant authorization or judgement?

### 5. INTERVENE

Can the human stop/change/deactivate when required and feasible?

### 6. ACCOUNT

Can responsibility be traced afterwards?

### Brazil line

> **"Human control is a capability of the whole human-machine system, not merely the presence of a person."**

---

# 53. HUMAN-CONTROL POI: "DO YOU REQUIRE A HUMAN TO APPROVE EVERY TARGET?"

Do not answer immediately with yes or no.

Answer:

> "Brazil requires meaningful human judgement and control appropriate to the context. The key is that humans must have real authority, information, limits and practical ability to intervene where required; the exact operational model may differ depending on the environment and reaction time."

Then ask:

> "Would the delegation accept those safeguards even where it does not prefer the same terminology?"

This turns a binary argument into substantive negotiation.

---

# 54. HUMAN-CONTROL POI: "ISN'T HUMAN CONTROL JUST VAGUE?"

Answer:

> "The phrase can be debated. The substance can be specified: mission authorization, operating limits, situational awareness, predictable behaviour, intervention capability, training and traceability."

Then:

> "Brazil is happy to negotiate the wording if the safeguards remain real."

---

# 55. DIRECT VS INDIRECT CONTROL — DETAILED TRADEOFF

## Direct control

Human approves each relevant engagement.

### Strengths

- clear;
- easy to explain;
- strong human role.

### Weaknesses

- reaction-time problem;
- communications problem;
- may reduce usefulness in certain defensive systems.

## Indirect/contextual control

Human defines a bounded mission and supervises.

### Strengths

- more realistic in some contexts;
- can work with high-speed systems.

### Weaknesses

- can become a broad delegation;
- intervention may be too slow;
- accountability can become unclear.

### Brazil compromise

> **Direct when required; indirect only when sufficiently bounded and effective.**

That is a useful conceptual formulation.

---

# 56. THE "HUMAN AUTHORITY" TEST

Ask:

> Could the human say "no"?

If the answer is technically yes but practically no because:

- the reaction time is impossible;
- interface blocks intervention;
- command structure discourages intervention;
- the system cannot accept the override;

then authority may be only theoretical.

---

# 57. THE "HUMAN INFORMATION" TEST

Ask:

> Did the human know enough?

Relevant information can include:

- system status;
- uncertainty;
- environment;
- target category;
- expected effects;
- warnings;
- limitations.

A human cannot meaningfully judge a situation they cannot adequately understand.

---

# 58. THE "HUMAN TIME" TEST

Ask:

> Did the human have enough time?

Human judgement is not meaningful if the operator receives:

> "System will act in 0.2 seconds. Decide."

and is unable to intervene realistically.

This does not establish a universal required response time.

It means the proposed control model must be **operationally credible**.

---

# 59. THE "HUMAN TRAINING" TEST

A trained operator should know:

- what the system can do;
- what it cannot do;
- common failure modes;
- the operating envelope;
- intervention methods;
- warning signs.

The 2026 GGE Chair's summary specifically connects human responsibility with understandable interfaces, guidance and personnel training. citeturn280913search24

---

# 60. AUTOMATION BIAS — DEEPER TEST

Suppose:

> the operator can override the system.

But:

- the machine is right 95% of the time;
- the interface displays the recommendation prominently;
- the operator is under severe time pressure;
- doctrine says "trust the system."

The operator may almost never override it.

This creates a human-agency problem even without formal removal of human authority.

### Safeguards

- training;
- interface design;
- uncertainty display;
- override practice;
- realistic simulations;
- after-action review.

---

# 61. PREDICTABILITY — THE BEST TECHNICAL WORD

Do not use "perfectly predictable."

Use:

> **sufficiently predictable for the intended circumstances of use.**

That allows realistic technology.

The legal review question is not:

> "Can the State know exactly what happens in every imaginable future?"

It is:

> **"Does the State know enough about expected behaviour and limitations to assess lawful use?"**

This is consistent with the ICRC's 2026 discussion of understandability, predictability and explainability. citeturn280913search27

---

# 62. PREDICTABILITY VS UNCERTAINTY

All systems have uncertainty.

The question is:

> **How much uncertainty can the State responsibly tolerate for the specific use?**

A high-risk, high-uncertainty use may require:

- narrower operating limits;
- stronger human judgement;
- more testing;
- more frequent review;
- prohibition if sufficient assurance cannot be established.

This is a proposed regulatory logic.

---

# 63. EXPLAINABILITY — DON'T MAKE THE SILLY ARGUMENT

Do not say:

> "Every AI decision must be perfectly explainable."

That is easy to attack.

Better:

> "The responsible authorities and reviewers need enough understanding of the system's capabilities, limitations and expected effects to assess its lawful use."

That is much harder to dismiss.

---

# 64. BLACK-BOX SYSTEMS

The black-box concern is not merely:

> "AI is complicated."

It is:

> **"If we cannot sufficiently characterize relevant behaviour, how can we responsibly set legal and operational limits?"**

That links:

black box
→ predictability
→ legal review
→ human control
→ accountability.

---

# 65. TESTING — FIVE LEVELS

## LEVEL 1 — NORMAL

Does it work under expected conditions?

## LEVEL 2 — EDGE CASES

What happens near the boundary of normal conditions?

## LEVEL 3 — DEGRADED

What happens with:

- poor sensors;
- weather;
- communications issues?

## LEVEL 4 — HOSTILE

What happens under:

- cyber interference;
- spoofing;
- adversarial inputs?

## LEVEL 5 — COMPLEX CIVILIAN ENVIRONMENT

What happens when:

- civilians are nearby;
- objects are mixed;
- conditions change rapidly?

A good test programme covers more than the happy path.

---

# 66. TESTING — WHAT IS THE TEST SUPPOSED TO PROVE?

Testing should answer a question.

Examples:

### Test question

> "Can the system stay inside its geographic boundary?"

Evidence:

> geofencing / parameter control results.

### Test question

> "Can the system recognize permitted targets?"

Evidence:

> validated test results under representative conditions.

### Test question

> "What happens when communication is lost?"

Evidence:

> fail-safe behaviour testing.

### Test question

> "Does a material update change target-selection behaviour?"

Evidence:

> regression testing + comparison.

---

# 67. VALIDATION VS VERIFICATION

A simple useful distinction:

### Verification

> Did we build the system according to the specified requirements?

### Validation

> Does the system actually behave appropriately for its intended use?

Different organisations use these terms differently, so do not fight over the labels.

The broader point is:

> **Both requirements checking and real-world performance matter.**

---

# 68. TESTING DOES NOT PROVE EVERYTHING

A test cannot establish:

> "This system is lawful forever."

Testing provides evidence about:

- relevant scenarios;
- known configurations;
- expected conditions.

That evidence needs to be combined with:

- legal review;
- operating limits;
- lifecycle monitoring.

---

# 69. THE AUTONOMY OPERATING ENVELOPE — TECHNICAL VERSION

For each regulated system, define:

### Target

What can be engaged?

### Area

Where?

### Time

How long?

### Scale

How many / what level of engagement?

### Environment

Under what assumptions?

### Communications

What connectivity is assumed?

### Human control

What human actions are required?

### Failure response

What happens if assumptions break?

This is how "human control" becomes machine-readable and auditable.

---

# 70. "OUTSIDE THE ENVELOPE" RULE

The most important design principle:

> **If a critical operating condition falls outside the validated envelope, the system must not silently continue as if nothing changed.**

Possible responses:

- halt;
- abort;
- return;
- safe loiter;
- hand over;
- request reassessment;
- use another predefined safe response.

Exact implementation depends on the system.

---

# 71. COMMUNICATIONS LOSS

This is both a technical and legal issue.

Potential consequences:

- human loses awareness;
- intervention fails;
- system receives stale information;
- system continues using old assumptions.

Therefore the framework should define the system's behaviour during communications loss.

### Key line

> **"Loss of communication should not silently expand autonomy."**

---

# 72. CYBERSECURITY — FOUR FAILURE CHANNELS

### Sensor manipulation

System sees false information.

### Data manipulation

System receives altered data.

### Software/update compromise

System itself changes.

### Communications disruption

Human control link breaks.

These are different problems and should be tested separately.

---

# 73. ADVERSARIAL INPUTS

An attacker may try to make the system misclassify something.

Examples:

- spoofed signatures;
- deceptive objects;
- manipulated data;
- false identification cues.

You do not need to explain offensive cyber techniques.

Your policy question is:

> **Was the system tested against foreseeable ways in which its input could become unreliable?**

---

# 74. SWARM CONTROL

For swarms, individual-system safety may not equal swarm-level safety.

Consider:

- number of active units;
- shared information;
- collective target selection;
- engagement speed;
- deconfliction;
- communications loss.

### Key line

> **"A swarm creates a system-of-systems problem."**

---

# 75. SWARM-LEVEL OPERATING LIMITS

Potential safeguards:

- maximum active units;
- common operating area;
- common target categories;
- aggregate engagement limits;
- shared abort conditions;
- deconfliction rules;
- swarm-level logs.

These are proposal options, not existing universal rules.

---

# 76. ESCALATION — DON'T OVERSTATE

Autonomous systems could alter:

- the speed of decision-making;
- number of simultaneous actions;
- human intervention windows;
- uncertainty during crises.

But do not say:

> "LAWS will automatically cause a flash war."

Better:

> **"Autonomy may compress decision and response times, which can create escalation or miscalculation risks that a regulatory framework should address."**

The 2025 UNGA resolution itself identified possible risks including miscalculation, lowering conflict thresholds, escalation and proliferation. citeturn280913search26

---

# 77. ACCOUNTABILITY — FIVE QUESTIONS

After an incident:

### WHO

Who authorized the use?

### WHAT

What system/version was used?

### WHERE

Where was it authorized to operate?

### LIMITS

What parameters applied?

### WHAT HAPPENED

What did the system actually do?

If these cannot be reconstructed, accountability becomes harder.

---

# 78. TRACEABILITY

Traceability is not necessarily:

> public disclosure.

It can be:

> retained internal evidence that allows a responsible authority to reconstruct the decision chain.

Potential records:

- system identity;
- software/model version;
- mission authorization;
- target parameters;
- alerts;
- intervention;
- incident record.

The 2026 GGE Chair's summary records support for a traceable chain of human command and control and discusses tools such as data logs and audit trails. citeturn280913search24

---

# 79. INCIDENT INVESTIGATION

A serious incident mechanism can ask States to:

1. preserve evidence;
2. investigate;
3. assess legal compliance;
4. determine whether a system or mission should be re-reviewed;
5. implement corrective action;
6. share appropriate lessons.

This is a proposed international mechanism.

It is not being presented as an existing treaty requirement.

---

# 80. VERIFICATION — WHY PROCESS EVIDENCE MATTERS

A State may not be willing to publish:

- model weights;
- source code;
- operational maps;
- vulnerabilities.

But it may still be able to demonstrate:

- legal review exists;
- testing was performed;
- an operating envelope exists;
- material changes are tracked;
- incidents are investigated.

Therefore:

> **Verify governance processes and assurance evidence where full technical disclosure is unnecessary.**

---

# 81. VERIFICATION — THE LADDER

### Level 1
National records.

### Level 2
National reporting.

### Level 3
Peer exchange.

### Level 4
Incident reporting.

### Level 5
Protected technical review.

### Level 6
Agreed targeted inspection.

The higher you go, the greater the sovereignty/security concerns become.

This makes a phased model easier to negotiate.

# 82. VERIFICATION VS TRUST

A State saying:

> "Trust us."

is not verification.

A State providing:

> testing evidence + legal review + defined limits + records

is stronger.

But verification also has to be reciprocal and realistic.

### Good line

> **"The goal is not distrust for its own sake; it is enough evidence to make international commitments credible."**

# 83. PROCUREMENT — WHY IT BELONGS IN LEGAL/TECHNICAL DEBATE

A system can pass research and still fail implementation.

Procurement is where a State can require:

- documentation;
- test evidence;
- cybersecurity;
- change notification;
- update control;
- audit rights;
- training.

Therefore procurement can act as an **upstream compliance gate**.

# 84. SUPPLIER RESPONSIBILITY

Suppliers can support:

- technical documentation;
- notification of material changes;
- secure updates;
- incident investigation;
- training.

But be precise:

> International legal responsibility remains governed primarily by applicable international law and State obligations.

Do not say:

> "Companies replace State responsibility."

# 85. TRANSFERS

A controlled system can become a different risk after transfer because:

- new operators;
- new environment;
- new mission;
- new legal framework;
- new technical configuration.

Therefore post-transfer assurance can matter.

### Proposal

A transfer package could include:

- system/version;
- limitations;
- testing status;
- approved environment;
- operator requirements;
- update conditions.

This is a design option, not existing law.

### What should not be claimed casually

# 88. CASE-STUDY AMMUNITION — LOITERING SYSTEMS

Use them to demonstrate:

- different degrees of human involvement;
- target-selection questions;
- sensor-based functions;
- why platform labels are insufficient.

Do not say:

> "loitering munition = LAWS."

Ask:

> **"Which functions are autonomous after activation?"**

# 89. CASE-STUDY AMMUNITION — AI DECISION SUPPORT

Where AI systems are publicly described as producing recommendations rather than autonomous attack orders, use the example carefully.

The debate becomes:

- What does the AI recommend?
- Who validates it?
- How much discretion remains?
- Can the human reject it?
- Is the human decision meaningful under time pressure?

This is a useful bridge between military AI and LAWS without collapsing the categories.

# 90. CASE-STUDY AMMUNITION — DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS

Use defensive systems to show why:

> **context matters.**

A short-reaction defensive environment may require a different human-control design.

But the question remains:

- target limits;
- predictability;
- operating conditions;
- intervention;
- fail-safe behaviour.

# 91. MARTENS CLAUSE — USE CAREFULLY

The Martens Clause can be relevant where specific treaty rules do not fully resolve a problem, invoking principles of humanity and public conscience.

### Good use

> "The framework should not ignore broader humanitarian principles merely because technology developed faster than specific treaty language."

### Bad use

> "The Martens Clause automatically makes LAWS illegal."

Do not use it as a magic legal switch.

# 92. HUMAN DIGNITY — LEGAL/ETHICAL BOUNDARY

Arguments about dignity can support stronger restrictions.

But distinguish:

### Legal claim

> A specific rule of international law applies.

### Ethical/policy claim

> A practice may be considered unacceptable even beyond existing black-letter rules.

Brazil can use both, but should know which one it is making.

# 100. THE "SO WHAT?" TEST FOR EVERY FACT

Before using a technical fact in debate, ask:

> **"So what should States do differently because of this fact?"**

Example:

Fact:
> Model updates can change system behaviour.

So what?

> **Material updates affecting critical functions should trigger renewed assurance.**

Fact:
> Humans can suffer automation bias.

So what?

> **Training and interface requirements should be part of human-control assurance.**

Fact:
> Communications can fail.

So what?

> **Define and test fail-safe behaviour.**

That is how facts become ammunition.

That keeps the debate technical instead of personal.

# 107. THE "OPERATING ENVELOPE" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "Why do you need geography limits?"

### Answer

> "Because a wider geographic area gives autonomous systems more opportunities to encounter unexpected conditions and targets."

### POI

> "Why time limits?"

### Answer

> "Because the operating environment can change after activation."

### POI

> "Why target limits?"

### Answer

> "Because human authorization becomes less meaningful as the target category becomes broader."

### POI

> "Why scale limits?"

### Answer

> "Because many individually bounded systems can create aggregate effects that a single-system test may miss."

# 108. THE "ADAPTIVE AI" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "Why should software updates trigger review?"

### Answer

> "Not every update should. Material changes affecting critical functions can change the system being assured."

### POI

> "Does Brazil want to review every line of code?"

### Answer

> "No. The trigger should focus on material changes in function or expected effects."

### POI

> "What counts as material?"

### Answer

> "Changes that could affect target identification, target selection, engagement, control, predictability or operating boundaries."

# 109. THE "VERIFICATION" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "Why should another State trust your report?"

### Answer

> "Because the framework can combine national evidence with transparency, peer exchange, incident reporting and protected technical review."

### POI

> "Why not inspect everything?"

### Answer

> "Because realistic verification must balance assurance with sovereignty and legitimate national-security concerns."

### POI

> "Why not trust national systems completely?"

### Answer

# 110. THE "ACCOUNTABILITY" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "What if the machine makes an unexpected decision?"

### Answer

> "The machine does not become the legal actor. The State should maintain a traceable chain of authorization, control and investigation."

### POI

> "Who exactly is responsible?"

### Answer

> "Responsibility depends on the facts and applicable law, but the framework should prevent responsibility from disappearing into an untraceable machine decision."

### POI

> "Why logs?"

### Answer

> "Because accountability without evidence becomes very difficult after the event."

# 111. THE "PROLIFERATION" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "Why regulate transfers?"

### Answer

> "Because assurance can be lost when a system moves to a new operator, environment or configuration."

### POI

> "What about non-State actors?"

### Answer

> "The international framework should address diversion and unauthorized transfer, especially because autonomous systems can potentially proliferate beyond the intended command structure."

# 112. THE "DEFENSIVE SYSTEM" POI PACKAGE

### POI

> "Would Brazil ban automated missile defence?"

### Answer

> "Brazil is not proposing an automatic platform-wide ban. The system should be assessed according to its autonomous functions, predictability, operating context, human control and IHL compliance."

### POI

> "But reaction time is too short for direct approval."

### Answer

> "That is why context-appropriate control matters. The framework can rely on bounded autonomy and predefined safeguards where direct approval is operationally impossible."

# 113. THE "SWARM" POI PACKAGE

### Answer

> "Because collective behaviour can create aggregate risks that are not visible in single-system testing."

### POI

> "So do you ban swarms?"

### Answer

> "Not automatically. The question is whether swarm-level behaviour remains sufficiently bounded, predictable and controllable."

# 130. THE "CLAUSE WRITING" AMMUNITION

A technically strong operative clause should usually answer:

### ACTOR

Who acts?

### ACTION

What do they do?

### TRIGGER

When does it apply?

### SCOPE

To which systems?

### EVIDENCE

What must be documented?

### REVIEW

What happens after a change or incident?

### CONFIDENTIALITY

What sensitive information can remain protected?

### Example

> "Calls upon States to establish procedures for renewed legal and technical review of covered autonomous weapon systems following material modifications affecting critical target-identification, selection or engagement functions, with appropriate records maintained for accountability and sensitive technical information protected under applicable national procedures."

This is a mechanism clause, not merely a principle.

# 131. "CALLS UPON" VS "RECOMMENDS" VS "INVITES"

In MUN drafting, wording changes political strength.

### "Calls upon"

Stronger request.

### "Urges"

Strong political pressure.

### "Encourages"

More flexible.

### "Invites"

Usually softer / exploratory.

### Use carefully

If the bloc is not ready to impose the strongest wording, do not destroy consensus by choosing language everyone will reject.

# 132. THE "MINIMUM VIABLE SAFEGUARD" IDEA

When negotiations become difficult, preserve the minimum substance.

For a regulated system:

1. defined operating scope;
2. human judgement/control;
3. legal review;
4. testing;
5. material-change review;
6. traceability.

That is a strong minimum package.

# 133. THE "HIGH-RISK SYSTEM" ESCALATION

If uncertainty or risk increases, the framework can move toward:

- narrower target classes;
- smaller geography;
- shorter duration;
- stronger human involvement;
- more testing;
- more frequent review;
- restricted deployment;
- prohibition if adequate assurance cannot be demonstrated.

This gives you a **graduated regulatory model** without claiming one universal legal formula.

# 134. THE "SAFE SYSTEM" DOES NOT MEAN "SAFE FOREVER"

A system may be lawful under one:

- mission;
- environment;
- software version;
- target class.

That does not make every later use lawful.

This is why:

> **context + lifecycle**

matter.

# 135. THE "STATE OF THE ART" PROBLEM

A rule written around today's technology may become obsolete.

Therefore write around:

- function;
- effect;
- control;
- risk;
- evidence.

Not:

- current hardware model;
- one algorithm;
- one manufacturer.

---

# 136. THE "FUTURE-PROOF" FORMULA

Use:

> **technology-neutral principle + concrete evidence requirement + periodic review**

Example:

> "States should ensure systems remain within validated operating conditions."

Then let technical methods evolve.

---

# 137. WHY "CONTEXT-APPROPRIATE" IS IMPORTANT

It protects the framework from absurdity.

A system operating:

- in a narrow defensive corridor;

may require a different intervention method than:

- a system operating over a huge urban area for hours.

Context should affect:

- supervision;
- time;
- geography;
- target class;
- intervention.

But not eliminate core legal safeguards.

# 138. THE "NO SELF-EXPANDING AUTHORITY" PRINCIPLE

A powerful way to explain mission-parameter safeguards:

> **The system should not gain broader lethal authority merely because its own software decides that broader action would be useful.**

Human validation should remain relevant when critical parameters change.

This is a proposed governance principle.

# 139. THE "FAIL-SAFE" VS "FAIL-OPEN" AMMUNITION

### Fail-safe idea

When critical assumptions fail, the system moves toward a predefined safer condition.

### Fail-open idea

When control fails, the system continues or becomes more permissive.

Brazil should favour the first logic for lethal autonomy, with context-specific engineering.

### Strong line

> **"A loss of control should not become an expansion of lethal authority."**

# 140. THE "CONTROL CONTINUITY" IDEA

Human control should be checked at:

**before activation**
→ **during operation**
→ **when conditions change**
→ **when communications fail**
→ **after the event**

This is stronger than only checking the moment the weapon is launched.

# 146. THE "INTERNATIONAL REVIEW" TRAP

Do not assume international experts should:

> command national militaries.

- examine evidence;

- provide technical advice;
- identify lessons.

This respects national command authority.

# 147. THE "REGISTRY" TRAP

A registry is useful only if it solves something.

Possible purpose:

- transparency;
- confidence;
- assurance status;
- incident trends.

Possible concern:

- sensitive information;
- duplication;
- bureaucracy.

So defend the function, not the registry label.

# 148. THE "TECHNICAL PANEL" TRAP

A panel can improve verification.

But ask:

- who appoints experts?
- what information can they access?
- how are conflicts of interest handled?
- who receives findings?
- what stays confidential?

A serious design must answer governance questions too.

# 149. THE "INCIDENT MECHANISM" TRAP

Do not create mandatory reporting for every tiny technical anomaly.

That can overwhelm the system.

A more practical approach is:

> define a threshold for serious incidents and preserve records for other events internally.

The exact threshold can be negotiated.

---

# 150. THE "REVIEW TRIGGER" TRAP

Do not trigger a complete legal review for:

> every keyboard update.

Focus on:

> **material changes affecting critical functions or expected effects.**

That is more implementable.

# 151. THE "HUMAN TARGET" PROHIBITION — HOW TO DEFEND IT

If a delegate says:

> "Why prohibit autonomous force against humans specifically?"

Answer:

> "Because human status and behaviour can be context-dependent and changing, and the humanitarian and accountability stakes are particularly serious. Brazil can discuss precise drafting thresholds, but the concern is strongest where autonomous systems directly determine the use of force against persons."

Be clear that this is a policy proposal, not a universally existing treaty prohibition.

---

# 152. THE "MILITARY OBJECTIVE" COUNTER

If a delegate says:

> "Then just permit autonomous force against military objects."

Ask:

> "Under what conditions? How is the object identified? What happens if the circumstances change? What limits the effects?"

This shows that removing human targets does not remove every legal question.

# 153. THE "PREDICTABILITY" PROHIBITION — HOW TO DEFEND IT

If a delegate says:

> "No system is fully predictable."

Answer:

> "Brazil is not demanding absolute certainty. The threshold is whether the State can establish sufficient understanding and predictability for the specific intended circumstances. If it cannot, stronger restrictions become necessary."

# 154. THE "UNCONTROLLABLE EFFECTS" PROHIBITION — HOW TO DEFEND IT

Ask:

This connects the prohibition to operating-envelope logic.

# 156. THE "SELF-LEARNING" POI

Question:

> "Why not let the system learn in real time?"

Answer:

> "It may be technically possible in some applications, but the regulatory question is whether the State can still characterize, constrain and assure critical lethal functions after the system changes."

That is the real question.

# 157. THE "WHY LOGS?" POI

Question:

> "Why store all this data?"

Answer:

> "Because without records, the State may not be able to reconstruct what system version, parameters or authorization were active when an incident occurred."

# 158. THE "WHY TRAINING?" POI

Question:

> "Why does operator training belong in an arms-control framework?"

Answer:

> "Because meaningful human judgement depends on understanding the system's capabilities and limitations. The 2026 GGE work expressly discusses training and guidance as part of human responsibility and accountability." citeturn280913search24

# 159. THE "WHY INTERFACE?" POI

Question:

> "Is software interface design really an international-security issue?"

Answer:

> "If a human's ability to understand warnings, system status or intervention options determines whether human control is meaningful, then interface design can affect the practical effectiveness of that control."

# 160. THE "WHY CAPACITY-BUILDING?" POI

Question:

> "Why should the framework spend resources on training other States?"

Answer:

> "Because international rules depend on implementation. Legal review, testing and cybersecurity require expertise, and capacity gaps can undermine consistent compliance."

# 161. THE "WHY REGIONAL CENTRES?" POI

Question:

> "Why build regional testing hubs?"

Answer:

> "They can pool expertise and reduce the cost of technical and legal assurance for States that lack large national testing institutions."

This is a proposal, not an existing international institution.

# 162. THE "WHY PROCUREMENT?" POI

Question:

> "Why regulate before deployment?"

Answer:

> "Because procurement is one of the earliest points at which a State can require documentation, testing, update controls and accountability arrangements."

# 163. THE "WHY TRANSFERS?" POI

Question:

> "Why should the treaty care about exports?"

Answer:

> "Because control can be weakened when the system moves to a different user, environment or configuration. Transfer rules help preserve the assurance built during development."

# 164. THE "WHOLE LIFECYCLE" POI

> "Why not regulate only battlefield use?"

Answer:

> "Because many risks arise earlier or later: poor design, weak testing, uncontrolled software updates, procurement gaps and failure to learn from incidents can all affect battlefield safety."

# 171. THE "WHAT THE 2026 GGE DOES NOT MEAN" CARD

It does NOT mean:

- the final treaty already exists;
- every phrase is universally accepted law;
- every technical proposal was accepted;
- every State has identical policy;
- legal form has been settled.

It DOES mean:

- States have produced substantial negotiating material;
- terminology and safeguards have been debated in detail;
- important areas of convergence exist;
- the next institutional step is politically significant.

The first-session Chair's summary itself records both broad support and remaining disagreements across several elements. citeturn193042view0

# 172. THE "MOST IMPORTANT 2026 GGE DETAIL" FOR YOU

The GGE's working characterization in Box I focused on a **functionally integrated combination of weapons and technological components** capable of identifying, selecting and engaging a target without human intervention in execution of those tasks. The March 2026 Chair's summary also records continuing debate over how human target-parameter setting, identification, existing fire-and-forget systems and possible loopholes should affect the characterization. citeturn193042view0

### Why this is gold for MUN

It gives you a very precise answer if the Chair asks:

> "What exactly is the definition debate?"

You can say:

> "The current GGE work is trying to characterize the system by critical functions, particularly identify, select and engage, while still working through where human target-setting and existing systems such as fire-and-forget weapons should sit."

That is much better than saying:

> "Nobody knows what LAWS are."

# 173. THE "GGE DEFINITION" CAUTION

Do not call the March 2026 characterization:

> "the universally agreed definition of LAWS."

The Chair's summary records that it remained a **rolling text under discussion**, with delegations raising concerns about scope, loopholes and existing weapons.

### Correct wording

> "The 2026 GGE rolling text proposed a functional characterization..."

---

# 174. THE "DEFINITION LOOPHOLE" PROBLEM

A definition can become too narrow.

Example:

> A human clicks "approve."

If that technically counts as "human target selection," a functionally autonomous system might fall outside the intended scope.

Therefore a serious definition must distinguish:

> **genuine human selection**

from:

> **nominal human validation of a machine-generated choice.**

This is one of the sharpest technical/legal debates in the current GGE process. citeturn193042view0

---

# 175. THE "TEMPORAL DECOUPLING" PROBLEM

A human may define something long before the system actually engages.

Example:

> Human sets a target profile at 10:00.

> System identifies and engages at 14:00.

The human may not know the actual target chosen at 14:00.

That raises the question:

> **How much does prior human programming count as human selection?**

This is one reason the control debate cannot be reduced to:

> "Did a human set parameters once?"

---

# 176. THE "FIRE-AND-FORGET" DEFINITION PROBLEM

Some delegates may argue that a fire-and-forget system should fall outside LAWS if a human selected the specific target before launch.

Others worry that this could leave functionally autonomous systems outside the intended framework.

### Brazil response

> **"The right question is whether the human genuinely selected the specific target or merely authorized a system to choose later within broad parameters."**

This is a strong distinction.

---

# 177. THE "LETHALITY" DEFINITION DEBATE

The 2026 GGE discussion also records disagreement over whether "lethal" should be an explicit defining characteristic.

Some delegations argued for keeping it.

Others argued that lethality is an effect of use rather than an intrinsic technical characteristic and that focusing too tightly on lethality could narrow the scope. citeturn193042view0

### Committee takeaway

Do not assume:

> "If it cannot kill, it cannot matter."

A system can create serious injury or destructive effects even without the precise word "lethal."

---

# 178. THE "NON-LETHAL EFFECT" LOOPHOLE

The current GGE discussion explicitly considered whether systems causing injury or damage rather than death should still fall within the working characterization. citeturn193042view0

### Why this matters

Otherwise a rule could accidentally create:

> "No death = outside the regime."

That could be too narrow.

### Brazil line

> "The framework should not create loopholes merely because the resulting harm is not described with one specific word."

---

# 179. THE "DUAL-USE" DEFINITION SAFEGUARD

The 2026 GGE discussion also records suggestions that the future framework should not hamper peaceful uses of emerging technologies and should account for dual-use/civilian technology. citeturn193042view0

### Why this matters

A definition should focus on:

- military use;
- critical autonomous functions;
- weapon integration;

rather than accidentally regulating ordinary civilian technology.
