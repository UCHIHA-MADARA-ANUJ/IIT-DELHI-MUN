# 1. THE MASTER IDEA

Do not walk into committee thinking:

> "Country X is with me."

Think:

> **"Country X overlaps with Brazil on some dimensions and differs on others. Which clause can we write together?"**

Every delegation should be mentally mapped on **four separate dimensions**:

### A. PROCEDURAL
Do they support using the 2026 GGE elements / rolling text as the basis for further negotiations?

### B. SUBSTANTIVE
Do they support:
- prohibitions;
- restrictions;
- lifecycle safeguards;
- human judgement/control;
- accountability?

### C. LEGAL FORM
Do they want:
- a legally binding instrument;
- a protocol;
- a political instrument;
- more discussion before deciding?

### D. IMPLEMENTATION
What do they think about:
- testing;
- legal review;
- operational limits;
- verification;
- reporting;
- confidentiality;
- transfers;
- capacity-building?

A country can be:

**very close to Brazil substantively**
while still being:

**uncertain on legal form.**

That difference is where good diplomacy happens.

# 26. THE 5 QUESTIONS TO ASK EVERY DELEGATE

When lobbying, do not give them a 90-second Brazil speech.

### 1
> "What legal form are you comfortable with?"

### 2
> "What exactly should be prohibited?"

### 3
> "What can still be used under strict conditions?"

### 4

### 5
> "What evidence would you accept as proof of compliance?"

These five questions map almost the entire agenda.

# 27. THE 4 TYPES OF AGREEMENT

When another delegate says:

> "I agree with you."

Do not stop there.

Find out **what kind** of agreement.

### TYPE A — PROCEDURAL AGREEMENT
"We should build on the GGE text."

Useful, but not enough.

### TYPE B — SUBSTANTIVE AGREEMENT
"We need prohibited + regulated categories."

Much stronger.

### TYPE C — OPERATIONAL AGREEMENT
"We need target, time, geography, scale limits, testing, intervention."

Very useful for drafting.

### TYPE D — LEGAL-FORM AGREEMENT
"We support a binding instrument."

This matters for the eventual mandate.

A delegate can agree with you on A+B+C while disagreeing on D.

That is still valuable committee common ground.

# 28. HOW TO TURN A COUNTRY INTO A DRAFTING PARTNER

Use this sequence:

**1. Identify overlap**

> "We already agree that..."

**2. Isolate the difference**

> "The only remaining question seems to be..."

**3. Offer flexible wording**

> "What if we phrase it as..."

**4. Protect their concern**

> "That would preserve your concern about..."

**5. Secure a clause**

> "Can we put that directly into the working paper?"

This is how you turn conversation into text.

# 37. THE SIX BIG DIPLOMATIC QUESTIONS IN THE ROOM

Most countries can be located by asking six questions.

## QUESTION 1
**Do you want a legally binding instrument?**

This separates:
- binding-oriented states;
- bridge states;
- existing-law states.

## QUESTION 2
**What should actually be prohibited?**

Possible answers include:
- systems inherently incapable of IHL compliance;
- systems outside meaningful human control;
- direct autonomous targeting of humans;
- systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently limited/predicted.

## QUESTION 3
**What can remain permissible?**

This is where:
- defensive systems;
- bounded autonomy;
- target restrictions;
- time/space/scale limits

enter the debate.

## QUESTION 4
**What does human control actually require?**

Possible answers:
- direct engagement approval;
- contextual/indirect control;
- pre-use constraints;
- monitoring;
- intervention;
- deactivation;
- responsible chain of command.

## QUESTION 5
**How do we verify?**

Possible:
- national reviews;
- testing;
- records;
- incident reporting;
- peer review;
- protected technical review.

## QUESTION 6
**How do we implement globally?**

This opens:
- capacity-building;
- regional testing;
- training;
- procurement support;
- technology assistance.

# 38. THE BEST BRAZIL NEGOTIATION FORMULA

When a country disagrees with Brazil:

## DO NOT SAY

> "Then you are blocking progress."

## SAY

> **"Let's separate legal form from operational safeguards. Which of these safeguards can we agree on now?"**

Then write down:

- testing;
- target restrictions;
- time/geography/scale limits;
- human judgement;
- intervention/deactivation;
- logging;
- material-change review.

Once those clauses exist, return to:

> "Now what legal instrument should carry them?"

That is a much stronger negotiation sequence.

# 39. THE "GIVE / GET" TABLE

| Brazil gives flexibility on | Brazil gets in return |
|---|---|
| Direct vs indirect human control | Human judgement has practical effect |
| Exact definition wording | Functional/capability-based scope |
| Confidential vs public reporting | Real traceability and records |
| Verification sequencing | Auditable compliance evidence |
| Treaty timetable | Concrete path to negotiation |
| Detailed operating envelope wording | Mandatory operational restrictions |
| National implementation method | Common minimum safeguards |
| Phased implementation | Clear endpoint and review mechanism |

This is how you compromise without abandoning Brazil's spine.

# 45. WHAT THE 2026 GGE CHANGES FOR YOUR DIPLOMACY

Do not lobby as if the international community has zero material to work from.

The 2026 GGE process was explicitly tasked with formulating elements of an instrument **without prejudging its nature**. Its September discussion covered human judgement/control, direct and indirect control, operational criteria, responsible command, limits on scale/targets/duration/geography and other safeguards. citeturn454113search0turn454113search1turn454113search4

The diplomatic consequence:

Your first pitch can be:

> **"We are not asking you to invent a new architecture from zero. Let's take the agreed negotiating material, decide which elements should become obligations, and draft the remaining details."**

That is much harder to dismiss.
