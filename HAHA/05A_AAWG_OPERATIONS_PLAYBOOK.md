# 05A — AAWG OPERATIONS PLAYBOOK

> **This is the tactical extension of Part 5.**
>
> Part 05 = what AAWG believes.
>
> Part 05A = **how AAWG actually functions in committee.**
>
> This file is intentionally operational. It tells you what to do when:
> - the bloc is still forming;
> - two delegates disagree;
> - someone wants softer wording;
> - a bridge State joins;
> - another bloc copies your idea;
> - drafting becomes chaotic;
> - sponsors need to be chosen;
> - the Chair calls for a document;
> - the room suddenly changes direction.

---

# 1. THE AAWG MISSION

AAWG has one practical mission:

> **Turn shared concerns about autonomous weapons into a coherent, negotiable and defensible text.**

That means the bloc must perform four jobs:

### JOB 1 — FIND COMMON GROUND

Not:

> "Who agrees with Brazil?"

But:

> "Which provisions can this group defend together?"

### JOB 2 — BUILD LANGUAGE

Do not stop at principles.

Convert:

> "human control is important"

into:

> a clause that defines who must do what, at which stage, with what evidence.

### JOB 3 — EXPAND THE COALITION

Keep the document usable for States outside the initial group.

### JOB 4 — DEFEND THE DOCUMENT

Every major clause should have:
- an explanation;
- a legal basis;
- a practical justification;
- an answer to the strongest objection.

---

# 2. THE THREE LEVELS OF AAWG AGREEMENT

A delegate can agree with AAWG at three different levels.

## LEVEL 1 — PRINCIPLE

Example:

> "Human judgement should remain central."

Good.

## LEVEL 2 — POLICY

Example:

> "Autonomous systems should have target, geographic and temporal limits."

Much better.

## LEVEL 3 — DRAFTING

Example:

> "States shall define and document, before deployment, the target categories, geographic area, maximum duration and engagement scale within which an autonomous weapon system may operate."

This is what the bloc actually needs.

### Rule

**Never leave a Level 1 agreement stranded when it can be converted into Level 2 and then Level 3.**

---

# 3. THE AAWG PIPELINE

Every issue should move through:

**CONCERN**
→ **PRINCIPLE**
→ **POLICY**
→ **MECHANISM**
→ **CLAUSE**
→ **EVIDENCE**
→ **DEFENCE**

Example:

**Concern:** software changes can alter system behaviour.

**Principle:** lifecycle assurance must continue after deployment.

**Policy:** material changes trigger renewed review.

**Mechanism:** Model/Software Change Control.

**Clause:** States identify material modifications affecting critical functions and conduct renewed legal/technical assurance before redeployment.

**Evidence:** version history + testing + legal review.

**Defence:** "Without this, the system approved on Day 1 may no longer be the system being used on Day 100."

That is the AAWG way of thinking.

---

# 4. THE BLOC FORMATION PHASE

When AAWG first gets everyone together, do not immediately write a 20-page paper.

Use:

## PHASE A — IDENTIFY

Ask every delegation for:

- 2 red lines;
- 2 priorities;
- 1 compromise they can make.

Write it on one page.

## PHASE B — CLUSTER

Group answers into:

- scope;
- prohibition;
- regulation;
- human judgement/control;
- lifecycle;
- accountability;
- verification;
- capacity.

## PHASE C — SEPARATE

Mark every issue:

**AGREED**
**BROADLY AGREED**
**OPEN**
**RED**

## PHASE D — DRAFT

Draft only the **AGREED** and **BROADLY AGREED** parts first.

## PHASE E — NEGOTIATE

Return to OPEN issues after the architecture is visible.

---

# 5. THE RED / ORANGE / YELLOW / GREEN SYSTEM

Use:

### GREEN — AGREED

No major objections.

### YELLOW — BROAD SUPPORT

Principle accepted; wording is open.

### ORANGE — CONTESTED

Two or more viable models.

### RED — CORE DISAGREEMENT

A delegation says the provision conflicts with a non-negotiable position.

### Why this matters

Without status labels, every disagreement feels like a crisis.

With labels, the bloc knows:

> "This is orange. We can return to it."

---

# 6. AAWG'S INTERNAL DECISION RULE

Do not vote on every sentence.

Use three levels.

## LEVEL A — Consensus

Use for:
- common principles;
- identity;
- core structure.

## LEVEL B — Broad agreement

Use for:
- technical implementation;
- non-core wording.

Record objections and preserve alternatives.

## LEVEL C — Bracketed choice

Use for:
- unresolved legal form;
- terminology;
- exact prohibition thresholds.

Example:

> "[legally binding instrument / negotiating process toward a legally binding instrument]"

The group can keep drafting while the political disagreement remains open.

---

# 7. WHEN CONSENSUS BREAKS

Use this exact sequence:

### STEP 1
Ask:

> "What exact word or proposition is the problem?"

### STEP 2
Ask:

> "Is the problem principle, scope, implementation or legal form?"

### STEP 3
Split the sentence.

### STEP 4
Protect the shared part.

### STEP 5
Bracket the disputed part.

### STEP 6
Move forward.

This prevents:

> "We have been arguing over one adjective for 12 minutes."

---

# 8. THE AAWG FACILITATOR ROLE

Someone should quietly facilitate.

The facilitator:

- keeps speakers short;
- stops repeated arguments;
- identifies agreement;
- records objections;
- moves unfinished issues to an open-items list;
- ensures quieter delegations speak;
- keeps the bloc on the clock.

### Brazil's best role

Brazil can facilitate without announcing:

> "Brazil is leading."

Instead:

> "Let's lock the agreed points and bracket the rest."

That feels like coordination, not control.

---

# 9. THE AAWG DRAFTER ROLE

The main drafter should:

- type live;
- integrate amendments;
- preserve version history;
- mark brackets;
- keep clause numbering stable;
- maintain a source note for controversial claims.

### Drafters must never:

- silently delete a country's concern;
- replace agreed wording without telling the group;
- turn a proposal into "AAWG consensus" without checking;
- cite an unofficial claim as established fact.

---

# 10. THE AAWG EVIDENCE CHECKER

This role is underrated.

Before an external claim enters the resolution or speech bank, ask:

### Source
Who says this?

### Date
When?

### Status
Current? Historical?

### Type
Treaty rule? State proposal? GGE text? Analyst opinion?

### Scope
Does the source prove the exact claim?

### Wording
Does our draft overstate it?

Example:

Wrong:

> "The GGE established that meaningful human control is international law."

Better:

> "The GGE discussions addressed human judgement and control as part of the emerging operational framework."

Evidence discipline makes AAWG much harder to attack.

---

# 11. THE OUTREACH ROLE

One or two delegates should continuously monitor outside States.

Their job:

- identify potential supporters;
- ask what they need changed;
- record objections;
- return with exact wording;
- bring interested States into the drafting room.

### Bad outreach

> "Hey join our bloc?"

### Good outreach

> "We already have agreement on testing, target limits and lifecycle review. Which one of those would you be willing to co-draft?"

The second one gives the delegate a reason to enter.

# 16. THE FIRST INTERNAL QUESTION

Do not start with:

> "What should the resolution say?"

Start with:

> **"What problem are we solving that another paragraph of general IHL language does not solve operationally?"**

Possible answers:

- autonomy changes the human decision chain;
- system behaviour may change with environment/data/software;
- human approval can become nominal;
- lifecycle modifications can invalidate earlier assurance;
- accountability requires records;
- technical verification is difficult without confidentiality protections.

Then build clauses around those problems.

---

# 17. THE AAWG "NO EMPTY WORDS" RULE

Avoid clauses made almost entirely of:

- reaffirming;
- emphasizing;
- recognizing;
- encouraging;
- stressing;
- calling upon.

Those can be useful politically but do not constitute the technical architecture.

For every major concept, ask:

> **"What changes after this clause exists?"**

If the answer is:

> "States feel encouraged."

the clause probably needs stronger implementation logic.

---

# 18. THE AAWG OPERATIVE-CLAUSE FORMULA

Use:

**ACTOR**
+
**ACTION**
+
**OBJECT**
+
**CONDITION**
+
**TIMING**
+
**EVIDENCE**
+
**CONFIDENTIALITY WHERE NEEDED**

Example:

> "Calls upon States to establish national procedures requiring legal and technical review of autonomous weapon systems before deployment and after material modifications affecting critical functions, with appropriate records retained for accountability and protected from unnecessary public disclosure."

This is far stronger than:

> "Encourages States to review LAWS."

---

# 19. THE "CLause OWNER" METHOD

Give each major clause one owner.

Owner responsibilities:

- propose first wording;
- defend it;
- collect objections;
- revise it;
- explain why the clause exists.

Example:

**OP4 Human Judgement/Control**
Owner: Norway / Sweden / Brazil
Support: Mexico / New Zealand / Australia

**OP7 Testing**
Owner: Japan / Australia
Support: Pakistan / Switzerland

This gives people ownership and prevents one person from writing everything.

---

# 20. HOW BRAZIL SHOULD EARN DRAFTING CENTRALITY

Do not say:

> "Brazil is the leader."

Instead repeatedly do these things:

### Bring the first clean draft.

### Merge two delegates' ideas.

### Keep version numbers.

### Explain why every major clause exists.

### Ask for exact objections.

### Return with revised text quickly.

### Preserve other States' contributions.

Eventually delegates naturally treat Brazil as the consolidation point.

---

# 21. THE MERGE FORMULA

When two delegates bring different drafts:

### Draft A
More legally strong.

### Draft B
More operationally flexible.

Do not choose A or B immediately.

Create:

> **Common core + flexible implementation**

For example:

**Common core:**
Human judgement must remain effective.

**Flexible implementation:**
Direct or indirect control depending on operational context.

This is one of Brazil's highest-value bloc skills.

---

# 22. HOW TO HANDLE "WE NEED A STRONGER BAN"

Answer:

> "What precise condition triggers the ban?"

Then ask:

> "Can we make that condition legally and technically testable?"

Then:

> "Can that be expressed without unintentionally covering systems you still consider lawful?"

This moves the discussion from emotion to architecture.

---

# 23. HOW TO HANDLE "DON'T CREATE NEW LAW"

Answer:

> "We can frame the operational safeguards as measures that help States implement existing IHL while separately discussing the legal form of any future instrument."

Then ask:

> "Which safeguards do you accept?"

This can bring cautious States into the room without abandoning Brazil's long-term position.

---

# 24. HOW TO HANDLE "I DON'T LIKE 'MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL'"

Do not fight over the phrase.

Say:

> "Fine. Let's define the substance instead."

Then propose:

- understanding;
- predictability;
- bounded target/time/space parameters;
- responsible authority;
- monitoring;
- intervention/deactivation where feasible;
- reassessment;
- accountability.

If they accept the substance, the vocabulary can be negotiated later.

---

# 25. HOW TO HANDLE "HUMAN MUST APPROVE EVERY SHOT"

AAWG should avoid making this an automatic universal rule.

Ask:

> "What about systems where reaction time is shorter than human decision time?"

Then propose:

> "Let's specify the human function that must remain effective in the relevant context."

That preserves human judgement without creating an operationally impossible universal rule.

---

# 26. HOW TO HANDLE "DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS ARE DIFFERENT"

Say:

> "They can be treated differently where their operational context genuinely requires it."

Then require analysis of:

- target category;
- environment;
- predictability;
- human constraints;
- operating envelope;
- fail-safe behaviour;
- intervention feasibility.

The principle becomes:

> **contextual treatment, not automatic exemption.**

---

# 27. HOW TO HANDLE "VERIFICATION WILL EXPOSE SECRETS"

Say:

> "Then let's protect the sensitive information rather than eliminate verification."

Possible model:

**PUBLIC**
- aggregate compliance framework.

**CONFIDENTIAL**
- detailed technical evidence.

**PROTECTED EXPERT**
- specialist review.

**CLASSIFIED**
- retained nationally where necessary.

That is a more realistic negotiating ladder.

---

# 28. HOW TO HANDLE "THIS IS TOO EXPENSIVE FOR DEVELOPING STATES"

Say:

> "Then implementation capacity should be part of the framework."

Possible response:

- regional testing hubs;
- legal-review training;
- technical assistance;
- shared methodologies;
- procurement guidance;
- expert exchanges.

Do not answer:

> "They should figure it out themselves."

That destroys the bloc's cross-regional credibility.

---

# 29. BRIDGE-STATE INVITATION MODEL

For Australia, Japan, India, China, UK, Canada, South Korea or other cautious delegations:

> **"We are not asking you to settle the legal form in this conversation. We want your input on the safeguards that are technically and operationally workable."**

Then give them three choices:

> "Testing?"
>
> "Operating limits?"
>
> "Lifecycle review?"

Once they choose one, give them ownership.

---

# 30. HOW TO KEEP A BRIDGE STATE WITHOUT DILUTING AAWG

Use:

**CORE TEXT**
+
**OPTIONAL IMPLEMENTATION LANGUAGE**

Example:

Core:

> "States shall maintain effective human judgement and control."

Implementation:

> "Such control may be exercised directly or through defined and tested operational constraints appropriate to the context."

A bridge State gets flexibility.

AAWG retains the principle.

---

# 31. HOW TO HANDLE TWO DELEGATES FIGHTING

Do not mediate by saying:

> "Both are right."

Instead:

### 1
Restate Delegate A's exact concern.

### 2
Restate Delegate B's exact concern.

### 3
Find the shared objective.

### 4
Draft one sentence satisfying the shared objective.

### 5
Bracket the disputed implementation.

Example:

A:
> "Direct human approval."

B:
> "Impossible in defensive systems."

Shared objective:
> "Human judgement must have practical effect."

Draft:
> "States shall ensure context-appropriate human judgement and control, including effective operational constraints and intervention where feasible."

Then continue.

---

# 32. HOW TO PREVENT DOMINANT DELEGATES FROM TAKING OVER

Do not challenge them personally.

Redirect:

> "That's useful. Can we get the operative wording onto the document?"

or:

> "Which exact clause are you proposing?"

or:

> "Who would like to co-draft that?"

This converts talking into output.

---

# 33. HOW TO HELP QUIETER DELEGATES

Ask targeted questions:

> "Would you prefer target restrictions or geographic restrictions as the main safeguard?"

or:

> "Would your delegation accept protected technical review?"

or:

> "Which capacity-building provision would make this implementable?"

Quiet delegates often have useful country-specific constraints but may not volunteer them.

# 36. OUTSIDE-BLOC EXPANSION

After the core AAWG text exists, do not ask 40 countries to join the whole bloc.

Ask them to adopt **one provision**.

Example:

> "Would your delegation support the lifecycle review clause?"

If yes:

> "Would you co-sign that section?"

Then:

> "Would you like to discuss the wider text?"

This is much easier than asking them to make an all-or-nothing bloc decision.

---

# 37. PROVISION-BASED COALITION BUILDING

AAWG can create mini-coalitions around clauses.

### Human-control cluster
Sweden + Norway + Mexico + New Zealand + Brazil

### Technical-assurance cluster
Japan + Australia + Switzerland + Pakistan + Brazil

### Lifecycle cluster
New Zealand + Switzerland + Germany + Sweden + Brazil

### Global South implementation cluster
South Africa + Philippines + Mexico + Brazil + African/Asian partners

These are **working drafting clusters**, not permanent alliances or political labels.

---

# 38. WHY THIS MATTERS

Suppose Australia refuses the treaty.

That does not mean Australia is useless.

If Australia writes the strongest testing clause in the document, it has become part of the substance.

Suppose Japan remains cautious on legal form.

If Japan helps write deactivation and operating-envelope provisions, AAWG gains technical credibility.

Suppose India remains cautious on pace.

If India helps build technology-neutral definitions, AAWG gets a broader scope.

The skill is:

> **Convert disagreement on one dimension into cooperation on another.**

---

# 39. THE AAWG "DO NOT WASTE A DELEGATE" RULE

Every serious delegate in the room should have one of these:

- clause;
- objection;
- source;
- amendment;
- country to contact;
- technical question;
- implementation idea.

If someone is sitting silently for 20 minutes, ask them a concrete question.

---

# 40. THE AAWG "NO EMPTY SUPPORT" RULE

When someone says:

> "I support."

Reply:

> **"Which clause would you like to own?"**

When someone says:

> "This is good."

Reply:

> **"What would you change?"**

When someone says:

> "I don't agree."

Reply:

> **"Which exact part?"**

When someone says:

> "This won't work."

Reply:

> **"What would make it workable?"**

Those four responses turn vague diplomacy into usable information.

---

# 41. HOW AAWG SHOULD HANDLE SOURCE DISPUTES

If two delegates cite different sources:

### Step 1
Identify the primary source.

### Step 2
Check publication date.

### Step 3
Check whether the statement is national or group-level.

### Step 4
Check whether the statement is current.

### Step 5
Do not force certainty if the evidence is genuinely unresolved.

Use:

> "The current public record is mixed; let's draft around the safeguard rather than overstate the national position."

That protects the bloc.

---

# 42. HOW AAWG SHOULD HANDLE A COUNTRY'S POSITION CHANGING

This can happen in real committee.

If a delegate says:

> "Our delegation has changed its position."

Do not immediately quote their earlier position back at them as if nothing changed.

Ask:

> "What part has changed — legal form, scope, human control or implementation?"

Then update the matrix.

The country map is a **live document**, not a museum.

---

# 43. HOW TO HANDLE AN UNEXPECTED CHAIR DIRECTION

Suppose the Chair calls a MOD on:

> "AI in warfare generally."

AAWG should avoid losing scope.

Use:

> "AAWG recognizes broader military AI concerns, but today's agenda requires us to connect those concerns to autonomous weapon functions, human agency and accountability."

Then propose:

- AI-DSS / automation bias;
- autonomy boundaries;
- lifecycle assurance;
- escalation.

This keeps the group inside agenda.

---

# 44. HOW TO HANDLE A MOD ON DEFINITIONS

AAWG should emphasize:

- no universal definition yet;
- capability/function-based scope;
- critical functions;
- distinction between automation and autonomy;
- distinction between AI and LAWS;
- treatment of human-supervised systems.

Possible bloc line:

> **"We need a definition precise enough to govern the risk, but flexible enough to survive technological change."**

---

# 45. HOW TO HANDLE A MOD ON HUMAN CONTROL

AAWG should break the concept into:

**understand**
→ **predict**
→ **limit**
→ **decide**
→ **intervene**
→ **account**

Then ask:

> "Which of these would your delegation remove?"

That makes vague objections concrete.

---

# 46. HOW TO HANDLE A MOD ON VERIFICATION

Break it into:

- national process;
- transparency;
- incident reporting;
- audit trail;
- expert exchange;
- protected technical review.

This gives cautious States a way into the discussion.

---

# 47. HOW TO HANDLE A MOD ON NON-STATE ACTORS

AAWG should connect:

- software;
- commercial components;
- transfers;
- captured/stolen systems;
- illicit procurement;
- modification.

Then propose:

- transfer controls;
- secure updates;
- diversion reporting;
- capacity-building.

Do not let the MOD become generic counterterrorism.

---

# 48. HOW TO HANDLE A MOD ON ESCALATION

Do not claim:

> "autonomy guarantees war."

Instead:

> "Autonomy may compress decision time and alter escalation pathways, so the framework should address engagement rates, communications failure, deconfliction and human intervention."

That is safer and technically stronger.

---

# 49. THE AAWG EXTERNAL MESSAGE

When someone asks:

> "What is AAWG?"

Say:

> **"We're building a two-tier LAWS framework: clear prohibitions where autonomous use is unacceptable, strict regulation for permissible systems, practical human judgement and control, lifecycle assurance, traceability and realistic verification."**

Then:

> **"We're still negotiating the exact legal form and some implementation details."**

That second sentence makes you sound collaborative rather than ideological.

---

# 50. THE AAWG PRIVATE MESSAGE

When a potential member asks:

> "What do you guys actually agree on?"

Say:

> "We have common ground on five things: unacceptable autonomous uses need prohibition, remaining systems need strict regulation, human judgement/control has to be practical, lifecycle assurance matters, and responsibility has to stay traceable. We're still working out exact definitions, verification and legal-form wording."

---

# 51. THE AAWG BRIDGE MESSAGE

For a cautious State:

> "You don't have to sign onto every Brazil position to contribute. Pick the operational safeguard you think is most realistic and let's write that together."

---

# 52. THE AAWG HARD-LINE MESSAGE

For a State demanding a stronger ban:

> "We agree the prohibition has to be meaningful. Help us define the exact test so it can survive legal and technical scrutiny."

---

# 53. THE AAWG TECH MESSAGE

For a technical delegate:

> "We're trying to turn human control from a slogan into requirements around predictability, operating limits, testing, intervention and lifecycle change."

---

# 54. THE AAWG GLOBAL-SOUTH MESSAGE

> "The framework cannot become a set of obligations only technologically advanced militaries can implement. We need legal-review capacity, testing support, training and technical cooperation built into the regime."

---

# 55. THE AAWG SPONSOR STRATEGY

Choose sponsors based on:

### SUBSTANTIVE
They shaped the text.

### TECHNICAL
They understand the mechanisms.

### DIPLOMATIC
They can negotiate amendments.

### REPRESENTATIONAL
The sponsorship visibly reflects the cross-regional character of the document.

Do not choose sponsorship merely by:
- popularity;
- number of MUNs;
- who joined earliest;
- friendship.

---

# 56. THE DRAFTING CREDIT RULE

If another delegate contributes a useful clause:

Say:

> "Let's put your wording in and mark you as co-owner of the provision."

This gives them a reason to defend the paper later.

AAWG should reward contribution with visible authorship.

---

# 57. THE "STEAL THEIR IDEA" RULE — ETHICALLY

Another bloc may propose something AAWG also likes.

Do not waste time saying:

> "That was our idea."

Instead:

> "AAWG also supports that mechanism; let's compare the implementation details and merge the strongest version."

The goal is a stronger committee product, not ownership of vocabulary.

---

# 58. WHEN ANOTHER BLOC INVITES YOUR MEMBER AWAY

Do not pressure them.

Ask:

> "Which part of their proposal fits your country's priorities that ours doesn't?"

Then fix the substantive gap if reasonable.

This avoids turning bloc politics into personal loyalty.

---

# 59. WHEN A MEMBER WANTS TO LEAVE

Ask:

> "Which provision is making the framework unacceptable for your delegation?"

Then classify:

- wording problem;
- implementation problem;
- legal-form problem;
- principle problem.

Try to solve the first three.

If it is genuinely a principle problem, record it and continue.

A bloc should not collapse because one member disagrees on one provision.

# 66. THE CURRENT EXTERNAL CONTEXT — WHY THIS MATTERS

The 2026 GGE's agreed elements are an important negotiating foundation, but the GGE mandate expressly did **not prejudge the nature** of any eventual instrument. The EU's 7 September 2026 PrepCom statement described the agreed elements as an important milestone and said the EU would build on them ahead of the November Review Conference. citeturn912142search0

That gives AAWG a very useful line:

> **"We are building from an existing multilateral negotiating product rather than inventing an entirely new debate."**

At the same time, Sri Lanka's September 2026 statement shows that some delegations are already pushing explicitly for a legally binding negotiating mandate, including lifecycle human judgement/control and strict prohibitions on autonomous systems outside human control or directly targeting humans. citeturn912142search1

So the room genuinely contains a legal-form debate **inside** a much wider area of substantive operational overlap.

That is exactly the space AAWG should occupy.

# 67. THE JAPAN LESSON

Japan's official public description continues to say that LAWS have no universally settled definition and focuses the discussion on systems that, once activated, can identify, select and engage targets without further operator intervention. citeturn912142search11

AAWG can therefore talk to Japan through:

- function-based scope;
- predictability;
- target limits;
- duration;
- geography;
- scale;
- deactivation;
- CCW process.

The lesson is:

> **Do not make the legal-form disagreement the first conversation.**

# 71. THE AAWG "EVERY CLAUSE MUST HAVE A DEFENDER" RULE

Before a clause survives into the sponsor-ready text, ask:

> "Who will defend this if the Chair points at us?"

Every major clause needs a person who can answer:

- why is it needed?
- what problem does it solve?
- why is this wording used?
- what is the implementation mechanism?
- what happens if a State objects?

No owner = clause is not ready.

---

# 72. THE AAWG "EVERY CLAUSE MUST HAVE AN OBJECTION" RULE

Also ask:

> **"What is the strongest argument against this clause?"**

Then write the answer under it.

Example:

### Clause
Material software changes trigger renewed review.

### Objection
"That creates excessive administrative burden."

### Response
> "The trigger is limited to material changes affecting critical functions; it does not require re-review for routine maintenance."

Now the clause is negotiation-ready.

# 75. WHAT BRAZIL SHOULD NOT DO

Same intellectual leadership.

Much better diplomacy.
