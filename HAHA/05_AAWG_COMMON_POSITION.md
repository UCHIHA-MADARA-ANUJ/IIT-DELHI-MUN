# 05 — AAWG COMMON POSITION

> **Working bloc:** Autonomy & Accountability Working Group (AAWG)
>
> **Purpose:** Give the bloc a common negotiating baseline that delegates can actually defend, amend and turn into operative clauses.
>
> **Important:** This is a **working common position**, not a Brazil document disguised as a bloc document. The group should ratify the final wording together.
>
> AAWG succeeds when members can say:
>
> **"We disagree on some legal-form details, but we already agree on the safeguards that matter."**

---

# 1. AAWG IDENTITY

## Name

**Autonomy & Accountability Working Group (AAWG)**

## Working description

> **A cross-regional group working on practical international rules for LAWS, with IHL, human judgement/control, accountability and implementability at the core.**

## What AAWG is NOT

AAWG is not:

- a "pro-ban at all costs" group;
- an anti-technology group;
- a Europe-only group;
- a Brazil-only drafting team;
- an ethics-only discussion circle;
- a WhatsApp group where everyone automatically agrees;
- a promise that every member supports identical treaty language.

## What AAWG IS

AAWG is:

- cross-regional;
- technically grounded;
- IHL-based;
- drafting-oriented;
- open to different implementation models;
- focused on concrete rules;
- willing to engage delegates who agree on safeguards even when they disagree on legal form.

---

# 2. THE AAWG PROBLEM STATEMENT

The group should be able to explain the agenda in one paragraph.

> **Autonomous weapon functions can create specific challenges for distinction, proportionality, precautions, predictability, human judgement, intervention, accountability and lifecycle assurance. Existing international humanitarian law remains the legal foundation, but States may need specific international rules to identify unacceptable autonomous uses and establish practical safeguards for systems that remain permissible.**

That is the AAWG common frame.

Notice what it avoids:

- saying IHL is irrelevant;
- saying all military AI is LAWS;
- claiming one universal definition;
- assuming every autonomous system is equally risky;
- assuming every State must use the same control architecture.

---

# 3. THE AAWG CORE — FIVE COMMON PRINCIPLES

These are the **first things to test and ratify** with the group.

## PRINCIPLE 1 — CLEAR PROHIBITIONS FOR UNACCEPTABLE AUTONOMY

AAWG supports clear international prohibitions for autonomous systems or uses that cannot satisfy the baseline requirements of lawful, predictable, bounded and accountable use of force.

Examples the bloc may examine include systems/uses that:

- cannot be used consistently with IHL;
- cannot sufficiently distinguish or limit effects;
- operate beyond meaningful human judgement/control;
- have unacceptable predictability/control failures;
- directly delegate critical lethal decisions in circumstances where human judgement cannot have practical effect.

### Important

The exact prohibition test is still an **open drafting question**.

Do not tell another delegate:

> "AAWG has already decided the exact Tier 1 definition."

Say:

> **"AAWG agrees that some autonomous systems/uses require prohibition; we are still finalising the precise threshold."**

---

# 4. PRINCIPLE 2 — STRICT REGULATION OF PERMISSIBLE AUTONOMY

AAWG does not treat "not prohibited" as "unregulated."

Systems that remain permissible should be subject to appropriate safeguards including:

- defined target categories;
- geographic limits;
- temporal limits;
- scale/engagement limits;
- environmental assumptions;
- human judgement/control arrangements;
- testing and evaluation;
- legal review;
- intervention/deactivation arrangements;
- traceability and accountability.

### AAWG line

> **"The choice is not ban versus no rules. It is prohibition where necessary and strict regulation everywhere else."**

---

# 5. PRINCIPLE 3 — HUMAN JUDGEMENT/CONTROL MUST HAVE PRACTICAL EFFECT

AAWG should avoid debating a slogan.

The common position should focus on conditions that make human judgement/control meaningful in practice.

Possible common elements:

### UNDERSTANDING
Relevant personnel understand the system's capabilities and limitations.

### PREDICTABILITY
The State has sufficient assurance about expected system behaviour in its approved environment.

### LIMITS
Autonomy is bounded by target, space, time, scale and other relevant operational conditions.

### AUTHORITY
An identifiable human chain of command retains responsibility and decision authority.

### INTERVENTION
Intervention/deactivation is available where technically and operationally feasible and appropriate to the system.

### REASSESSMENT
Humans can reconsider use when relevant conditions change.

### ACCOUNTABILITY
Responsibility remains attributable to humans and States, not to the machine.

### AAWG wording

> **"Human judgement and control must be effective in the conditions of use, rather than merely formal on paper."**

---

# 6. PRINCIPLE 4 — LIFECYCLE ASSURANCE

AAWG should treat autonomy as a lifecycle problem.

A common lifecycle chain:

**Research**
→ **Development**
→ **Legal review**
→ **Procurement**
→ **Testing**
→ **Deployment**
→ **Operation**
→ **Modification**
→ **Retirement / transfer**

At each relevant stage, States should ask:

- What changed?
- What evidence exists?
- Are the original legal assumptions still valid?
- Does the system remain within its approved operating conditions?
- Has a material modification triggered renewed review?

### Common AAWG position

> **"Legal and technical assurance should not end the day a system is first approved."**

---

# 7. PRINCIPLE 5 — ACCOUNTABILITY + IMPLEMENTABILITY

AAWG should pair accountability with realistic implementation.

## Accountability

States should maintain sufficient traceability to determine:

- who authorized the system;
- what system/configuration was used;
- what operating limits applied;
- what material changes occurred;
- what warnings/interventions occurred;
- what happened during a serious incident.

## Implementability

The regime should also consider:

- technical assistance;
- legal-review capacity;
- training;
- regional testing support;
- cybersecurity capacity;
- procurement guidance;
- expert exchanges.

### Common line

> **"Universal rules require both accountability and the capacity to implement them."**

---

# 8. AAWG'S SHARED TWO-TIER ARCHITECTURE

The current working architecture is:

## TIER 1 — PROHIBIT

Systems/uses meeting the agreed prohibition threshold.

Potential grounds:

- inability to comply with IHL;
- inability to sufficiently constrain effects;
- unacceptable lack of predictability/control;
- autonomous use of force against humans where AAWG concludes human judgement cannot have practical effect;
- other characteristics the bloc collectively identifies as unacceptable.

## TIER 2 — STRICTLY REGULATE

Systems that are not prohibited but still require:

- legal review;
- testing;
- operational envelope;
- human judgement/control;
- trained personnel;
- monitoring;
- traceability;
- lifecycle change control;
- incident investigation.

### Crucial drafting rule

Do not decide the exact Tier 1 wording before hearing all AAWG members.

The **architecture** can be common even if the **threshold language** remains open.

---

# 9. AAWG COMMON OPERATIONAL PACKAGE

This is the section that can make the bloc much more than an "ethics" bloc.

## 9.1 Operating envelope

A covered autonomous system operates inside a predefined and tested envelope covering, as relevant:

- target category;
- geography;
- duration;
- engagement scale;
- environmental conditions;
- communications assumptions;
- intervention/deactivation conditions.

## 9.2 Legal review

States conduct legal review under applicable international law.

For States Parties to Additional Protocol I, Article 36 remains a central legal-review obligation.

AAWG should avoid presenting Article 36 as a universal treaty obligation for every UN Member State.

## 9.3 Testing

Testing should cover realistic conditions, including where relevant:

- edge cases;
- sensor degradation;
- communications loss;
- cyber/electronic interference;
- unexpected civilian presence;
- adversarial inputs;
- interaction with other autonomous systems;
- material software/model changes.

## 9.4 Material change control

A material modification affecting critical functions should trigger renewed assurance.

Potential triggers:

- target selection;
- engagement logic;
- effects;
- autonomy level;
- operating environment;
- critical safety assumptions.

## 9.5 Logging / traceability

States should retain appropriate records for investigation and accountability.

The exact retention period and disclosure model remain open.

## 9.6 Serious incidents

AAWG should consider a mechanism for serious incidents involving, for example:

- civilian harm;
- major loss of control;
- unexpected autonomous engagement;
- unauthorized operation;
- serious cyber/software failure.

## 9.7 Protected review

AAWG should explore confidential technical/expert review rather than assuming that compliance requires public disclosure of source code or sensitive military information.

---

# 10. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS

A defensive system should not automatically be treated as:

> "safe"

or

> "outside the topic."

AAWG should instead ask:

- What target is involved?
- What is the operating environment?
- How bounded is the autonomy?
- How predictable are the effects?
- What human constraints exist?
- What happens if communications fail?
- What intervention/deactivation options exist?
- What evidence supports the system's approved use?

This permits context-appropriate approaches for extremely time-sensitive defensive systems without creating a blanket exemption.

---

# 11. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT AI DECISION SUPPORT

Common baseline:

> **AI decision support is not automatically LAWS.**

However, the group should recognize the human-agency problem where AI materially influences lethal targeting decisions.

Possible safeguards for high-risk AI decision-support include:

- sufficient information for the reviewer;
- uncertainty/error indicators;
- appropriate operator training;
- workload management;
- meaningful authority to reject recommendations;
- auditability of recommendations and human decisions where appropriate.

This lets AAWG avoid the trap of regulating every military AI application as if it were an autonomous weapon.

---

# 12. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT ADAPTIVE AI

AAWG should treat adaptive systems as a lifecycle-assurance issue.

The key question is:

> **Has the system changed enough that the original legal/technical assurance may no longer apply?**

Potential controls:

- model/version identification;
- update logs;
- defined change thresholds;
- rollback capability where feasible;
- revalidation after material changes;
- post-deployment monitoring.

AAWG does not need to ban every adaptive capability.

It needs a defensible rule for when adaptation becomes **material**.

---

# 13. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT COMMUNICATIONS LOSS

AAWG should support **tested, context-appropriate failure behaviour**.

Possible behaviours can include:

- abort;
- deactivate;
- safe return;
- loiter;
- restricted mode.

Do not impose one universal default without considering the system and operational environment.

The common principle is:

> **Critical loss-of-control conditions should have a predefined and tested response.**

---

# 14. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT CYBER / ADVERSARIAL INPUTS

AAWG should recognize that autonomy depends on:

- sensors;
- data;
- communications;
- software;
- models.

Therefore assurance should consider:

- spoofing;
- jamming;
- sensor manipulation;
- adversarial inputs;
- cyber intrusion;
- degraded communications;
- conflicting sensor inputs.

Again:

> **Test the conditions that can break the system's assumptions.**

---

# 15. WHAT AAWG SHOULD SAY ABOUT SWARMS

AAWG should treat swarms as a possible **system-of-systems** problem.

Relevant safeguards may include:

- engagement-scale limits;
- target-set restrictions;
- geographic containment;
- communication-loss behaviour;
- human authorization before expanding target sets;
- system-wide logs;
- system-level testing.

The common position should not automatically be:

> "Ban every swarm."

The correct question is:

> **"What system-level conditions are necessary to keep aggregate behaviour sufficiently predictable, bounded and accountable?"**

---

# 16. AAWG POSITION ON VERIFICATION

The bloc should not begin with:

> "international inspectors must see everything."

Nor should it accept:

> "national self-assessment is enough."

Instead consider a layered model:

### Level 1
National legal review and implementation.

### Level 2
General transparency about national frameworks.

### Level 3
Serious-incident reporting.

### Level 4
Expert exchange / peer learning.

### Level 5
Retention of auditable records.

### Level 6
Protected/confidential technical review where appropriate.

The exact international verification mechanism remains an open drafting question.

---

# 17. AAWG POSITION ON TRANSFERS / NON-STATE ACTORS

The bloc should consider:

- diversion;
- illicit procurement;
- stolen/captured systems;
- transfer of autonomous software;
- modification of civilian platforms;
- non-State actor access.

Possible measures:

- transfer controls;
- end-use controls;
- secure software/update practices;
- cooperation against diversion;
- reporting of loss/theft;
- restrictions on transfer of prohibited systems.

This should be developed without accidentally turning AAWG into a generic arms-control bloc disconnected from LAWS.

---

# 18. AAWG POSITION ON CAPACITY-BUILDING

AAWG should support practical implementation assistance, potentially including:

- legal-review training;
- technical testing support;
- cybersecurity assistance;
- regional expert centres;
- procurement guidance;
- operator training;
- model/system assurance expertise.

### Global South framing

> **"The obligations should be universal, but the implementation burden cannot be ignored."**

This is especially important when working with African, Asian, Latin American and smaller States.

---

# 19. THE AAWG LEGAL-FORM QUESTION

This is one of the biggest internal differences.

Brazil's documented position:
- intended destination = legally binding instrument;
- CCW rolling text = practical negotiating basis.

AAWG should therefore distinguish:

### Shared procedural point
Build on the 2026 GGE elements / rolling text.

### Shared substantive point
Prohibition + strict regulation + practical human judgement/control + accountability.

### Internal open point
Exactly what legal instrument the November Review Conference should mandate and how quickly negotiations should proceed.

### Suggested common wording for now

> **"AAWG supports moving from exploratory discussion toward a structured negotiating process based on the GGE's existing elements, with the objective of durable international rules."**

This lets members participate while their precise legal-form preferences are still being reconciled.

Brazil can still advocate the stronger binding endpoint.

---

# 20. AAWG NON-NEGOTIABLES

These are the principles Brazil should try to protect inside the bloc.

## A. No "no-rules" Tier 2

Not prohibited ≠ unrestricted.

## B. No purely symbolic human control

A human must have genuine decision-making relevance appropriate to context.

## C. No transfer of responsibility to the machine

Accountability remains with human actors and States.

## D. No one-time-only assurance for material changes

Material changes need reassessment.

## E. No public-source-code requirement as the default

Verification must protect legitimate confidentiality.

## F. No blanket equation of all military AI with LAWS

Scope must remain functional/capability-based.

## G. No blanket exemption simply because a system is defensive

Context matters.

---

# 21. AAWG COMPROMISE SPACE

This is where the bloc needs discipline.

Members can negotiate over:

- exact definition;
- wording of "meaningful" human control;
- direct vs indirect control;
- precise prohibition threshold;
- operating-envelope detail;
- reporting frequency;
- confidentiality;
- verification sequencing;
- technical-expert structure;
- capacity-building mechanism;
- transfer language;
- timing of the legal negotiations.

### Principle

> **Trade implementation wording, not the core safety architecture.**

---

# 22. THE AAWG "COMMON GROUND FIRST" RULE

When a new country joins the discussion, do NOT ask:

> "Do you support Brazil's position?"

Ask:

1. Do you agree IHL applies?
2. Do you agree some autonomous uses require prohibition?
3. Do you agree permissible systems need operational restrictions?
4. Do you agree human judgement/control must have practical effect?
5. Do you agree States need lifecycle assurance?
6. Do you agree accountability must remain traceable?
7. Which of these do you want stronger/weaker/different?

That identifies common ground without forcing premature alignment.

---

# 23. AAWG MEETING AGENDA

When the bloc sits down, use this order.

## ROUND 1 — 5 minutes
Each delegation gives:

> "Our top two red lines."

## ROUND 2 — 10 minutes
Agree on:

- five shared principles;
- five open questions.

## ROUND 3 — 10 minutes
Take each open question:

- definition;
- prohibition;
- human control;
- verification;
- legal form.

Record:

**shared**
vs.
**needs drafting compromise**

## ROUND 4 — 15 minutes
Start drafting actual operative language.

Do not spend the entire meeting discussing philosophy.

## ROUND 5 — final 5 minutes
Assign:

- one person to consolidate;
- clause owners;
- outreach targets;
- next meeting/time;
- unresolved questions.

# 24. HOW BRAZIL SHOULD BEHAVE INSIDE THE BLOC

Brazil should:

- bring structure;
- bring researched wording;
- provide evidence;
- draft clauses;
- connect delegates who share different pieces of the architecture;
- ask quieter delegates for input;
- summarize disagreements neutrally;
- keep the document moving.

Brazil should **not**:

- call itself "leader" repeatedly;
- decide every clause unilaterally;
- treat weaker/less experienced delegates as irrelevant;
- assume the group must follow Brazil;
- monopolize speaking;
- turn every disagreement into a red line.

### The desired effect

Other delegates should start saying:

> **"Let's ask Brazil how we can phrase this."**

That is more valuable than claiming leadership.

# 26. THE "ONE DOCUMENT" RULE

Do not let AAWG produce:

- five competing papers;
- three definitions;
- seven different prohibition tests.

The goal should be:

**ONE SHARED BASE TEXT**

with clearly marked:

- agreed clauses;
- bracketed options;
- unresolved issues.

That gives the bloc a real negotiating product.

---

# 27. BRACKET MANAGEMENT

When AAWG cannot agree, use brackets.

Example:

> "States shall ensure [meaningful human judgement and control / context-appropriate human judgement and control] throughout the lifecycle..."

Do not fight for an hour over wording that can be bracketed while the bloc resolves the broader structure.

---

# 28. WHAT AAWG CAN PRESENT TO OTHER BLOCS

A simple public-facing message:

> **"AAWG is working on a practical two-tier LAWS framework: clear prohibitions for unacceptable autonomous uses, strict regulation for permissible systems, practical human judgement/control, lifecycle assurance, accountability and implementable verification."**

Then ask:

> **"Which part would you change?"**

This is better than:

> "Join our bloc."

---

# 29. HOW TO BRING IN A BRIDGE STATE

### Australia / Japan / India / China / UK / Canada / South Korea

Say:

> **"You do not have to settle the final legal form with us today. Help us design the safeguards you would actually implement."**

Then ask them to choose:

- testing;
- target limits;
- geography;
- duration;
- scale;
- logging;
- deactivation;
- material-change review;
- legal review.

Once they help draft a safeguard, they have already contributed to the bloc.

---

# 30. HOW TO RESPOND TO A STRONG TREATY STATE

If Germany/Norway/Netherlands/Pakistan/etc. says:

> "We need a binding instrument."

Do not just say:

> "Same."

Ask:

> **"Which obligations do you want the instrument to contain?"**

That shifts the conversation from political declaration to actual drafting.

---

# 31. HOW TO HANDLE A MEMBER WHO WANTS A STRONGER BAN

Do not immediately reject them.

Ask:

> "What exact technical/legal test would trigger the ban?"

Then:

> "Can that test be operationalized and verified?"

If yes, consider the language.

If not:

> "Can we state the principle strongly now and refine the operational threshold in the technical annex/review mechanism?"

This keeps the bloc together.

---

# 32. HOW TO HANDLE A MEMBER WHO WANTS A SOFTER FRAMEWORK

Ask:

> "What safeguards would still be acceptable under existing IHL?"

Then capture them.

The bloc can often preserve:

- testing;
- legal review;
- operating limits;
- traceability;
- lifecycle assurance;

even when legal-form disagreement remains.

---

# 33. AAWG RED-FLAG PHRASES

When someone proposes wording like:

> "Human control shall be ensured."

Ask:

> **"How?"**

When someone proposes:

> "States shall conduct testing."

Ask:

> **"What conditions?"**

When someone proposes:

> "International verification."

Ask:

> **"What evidence, who sees it, and what remains confidential?"**

When someone proposes:

> "Ban autonomous weapons."

Ask:

> **"What is the functional definition and prohibition test?"**

When someone proposes:

> "AI targeting systems are LAWS."

Ask:

> **"Which autonomous weapon function makes it fall within the scope?"**

# 34. THE AAWG CLAUSE TEST

Every proposed operative clause should answer:

### WHO?
Who implements it?

### WHAT?
What exactly must they do?

### WHEN?
At what lifecycle stage?

### WHERE?
What systems/uses are covered?

### HOW?
What mechanism makes it real?

### EVIDENCE?
How could compliance be demonstrated?

### CONFIDENTIALITY?
What information can remain protected?

If a clause cannot answer these, it probably needs more drafting.

# 36. AAWG SPONSOR LOGIC

A sponsor should be someone who:

- worked on the actual text;
- understands the clauses;
- can defend them;
- can negotiate amendments;
- represents genuine cross-regional participation where possible.

Do not pick sponsors because:

- they joined the WhatsApp group;
- they are famous delegates;
- they have the most MUNs;
- they are friends;
- they were first to join.

The principle:

> **Contribution + defendability + representation**

# 37. THE AAWG DOCUMENT STATUS SYSTEM

Use four labels:

All participating members accept.

If you have only 30 seconds:

> **AAWG supports a practical two-tier framework for LAWS. First, clearly unacceptable autonomous weapon uses/systems should be prohibited where lawful, predictable, bounded and accountable use of force cannot be ensured. # 42. WHAT EVERY MEMBER SHOULD BE ABLE TO DEFEND

Before calling something a common position, ask whether a member could answer:

> “Why?”

For every principle, the answer should have:

**problem → rule → mechanism**

Example:

### Problem
Autonomous operation may behave unpredictably outside the conditions in which it was tested.

### Rule
Covered systems should operate only within defined and tested conditions.

### Mechanism
A documented operating envelope with review after material changes.

That is a defendable bloc principle.

# 43. THE COMMON PRINCIPLE TEST

A proposal qualifies as genuine common ground when:

1. multiple members explicitly support it;
2. it can be stated without relying on one country's unique policy;
3. it can be translated into operative language;
4. it does not require every member to settle unrelated legal-form questions;
5. it can survive a skeptical POI.

If it fails one of these tests, mark it:

**YELLOW — BROAD SUPPORT**

rather than pretending it is unanimously agreed.

# 44. THE FIVE LEVELS OF AGREEMENT

Do not treat agreement as binary.

## LEVEL 1 — SAME PROBLEM

Two countries agree there is a problem.

Example:

> “Autonomous systems create accountability concerns.”

Useful, but weak.

## LEVEL 2 — SAME PRINCIPLE

They agree on the response.

> “Human judgement must remain meaningful.”

Better.

## LEVEL 3 — SAME SAFEGUARD

They agree on an actual mechanism.

> “Systems should operate within predefined geographic and temporal limits.”

Strong.

## LEVEL 4 — SAME CLAUSE

They can defend basically the same operative wording.

Very strong.

## LEVEL 5 — SAME NEGOTIATING POSITION

They are willing to defend the same package publicly.

That is the level to aim for before calling something a bloc position.

---

# 45. THE “DO NOT CALL IT AGREED YET” RULE

Do not write:

> “The bloc agrees…”

until the group actually agrees.

Use:

> “Current common ground appears to be…”

or:

> “There is broad support for…”

or:

> “Most members support…”

This protects the bloc from internal disputes later.

---

# 46. THE FIVE COMMON QUESTIONS THE BLOC MUST ANSWER

Before the serious drafting phase, the bloc should have working answers to:

### 1. WHAT IS COVERED?

What functional/capability features bring a system into scope?

### 2. WHAT IS PROHIBITED?

Which systems or uses cross the unacceptable threshold?

### 3. WHAT IS REGULATED?

What remains permissible only under strict conditions?

### 4. WHAT MUST HUMAN BEINGS DO?

What judgement, authority and control must remain effective?

### 5. HOW DO WE PROVE IT?

What legal, technical, operational and accountability evidence demonstrates compliance?

These five questions should organize the entire bloc document.

---

# 47. SCOPE — THE BLOC'S STARTING POSITION

The bloc should support a **function-based / capability-based scope**.

The focus should be autonomous weapon functions rather than a blanket category covering every military AI application.

Important distinctions:

### AI

A technology category.

### Automation

A process that may perform a task automatically.

### Autonomy

A system's ability to perform functions with less continuous human intervention.

### LAWS

A specific category of weapon-related autonomous functions involving the targeting/selection/engagement problem under the agenda.

Therefore:

> **Not every AI system is a LAWS.**

And:

> **Not every automated military system requires identical international regulation.**

This protects the bloc from overbreadth.

---

# 48. SCOPE — WHAT SHOULD STILL BE CAPTURED

A system should not escape meaningful discussion merely because its autonomy is packaged differently.

Look at the function.

Ask:

- Can the system identify relevant objects?
- Can it classify them?
- Can it select among targets?
- Can it initiate engagement?
- Can it adapt its behaviour?
- Can it continue operating without further human approval?
- Can its operation affect civilians or military objectives under the applicable legal framework?

The bloc should avoid platform-only definitions that can be bypassed by changing the technical label.

---

# 49. THE TARGETING-CHAIN TEST

When members disagree about whether something falls inside scope, ask where autonomy enters the chain:

**Detect → Identify → Classify → Select → Engage → Reassess**

Not every autonomous function has the same legal significance.

The key question is:

> **Where does the system materially affect the use of force?**

This gives the bloc a technically serious way to distinguish:

- intelligence support;
- decision support;
- navigation;
- defensive response;
- target selection;
- autonomous engagement.

---

# 50. PROHIBITION — THE SHARED ARCHITECTURE

The bloc should support a category of autonomous systems or uses that are not acceptable.

The precise legal test remains open until the members settle it.

Possible criteria include:

- inability to comply with applicable IHL;
- inability to sufficiently distinguish relevant targets/effects;
- inability to constrain the effects of force appropriately;
- unacceptable unpredictability;
- lack of effective human judgement/control;
- autonomous use of lethal force against humans where the group concludes human judgement cannot have practical effect;
- inability to maintain safe operation within the required conditions.

Do not make every criterion automatically cumulative.

The drafting question is:

> **Which characteristics, alone or together, should trigger prohibition?**

---

# 51. PROHIBITION — THE THREE-QUESTION TEST

For any proposed prohibited category, ask:

### QUESTION A
Can it comply with applicable IHL?

### QUESTION B
Can its effects be sufficiently bounded and predictable?

### QUESTION C
Can human judgement and responsibility have practical effect?

If the answer is consistently “no,” the case for prohibition becomes much stronger.

---

# 52. PROHIBITION — WHAT NOT TO SAY

Avoid:

> “Every autonomous weapon should be banned.”

That collapses the agenda too early.

Avoid:

> “All AI targeting is illegal.”

That is overbroad.

Avoid:

> “Any system with autonomy is a LAWS.”

That ignores the functional distinctions the agenda requires.

Use:

> **“The bloc supports clear prohibitions for specified autonomous systems or uses that cannot meet agreed conditions for lawful, predictable, bounded and accountable use.”**

---

# 53. REGULATION — THE SECOND TIER

The bloc's most important principle:

> **Not prohibited does not mean unrestricted.**

For covered systems that remain permissible, the framework should consider:

- target categories;
- operating environment;
- geographic area;
- duration;
- scale;
- anticipated effects;
- human judgement/control arrangements;
- legal review;
- testing;
- operator training;
- communications assumptions;
- cyber resilience;
- traceability;
- incident response;
- material-change review.

This is what makes the bloc more than a prohibition discussion.

---

# 54. THE OPERATING ENVELOPE — THE BLOC'S CORE TOOL

Every regulated autonomous system should be associated with an appropriate **operating envelope**.

It should define the conditions under which the system has been assessed and authorized.

Potential dimensions:

| Dimension | Example question |
|---|---|
| Target | What target categories are permitted? |
| Geography | Where may the system operate? |
| Time | For how long may autonomy continue? |
| Scale | How many objects/engagements may be handled? |
| Environment | Under what assumptions does the system remain reliable? |
| Communications | What happens if the link is lost? |
| Effects | What limits apply to expected effects? |
| Intervention | When and how can humans intervene? |

This is proposed bloc architecture, not existing treaty law.

---

# 55. OPERATING ENVELOPE — WHY IT MATTERS

A system may be predictable in:

- open terrain;

but not necessarily in:

- dense urban environments.

It may work under:

- stable communications;

but not necessarily under:

- severe interference.

It may be acceptable for:

- a tightly bounded target set;

but not necessarily for:

- an expanding target set.

Therefore:

> **“Is the system autonomous?” is not enough.**

The more useful question is:

> **“Under what conditions is this system authorized to exercise autonomy?”**

---

# 56. HUMAN JUDGEMENT — THE BLOC'S CENTRAL IDEA

Do not make the discussion depend entirely on one phrase.

The bloc should focus on what humans must actually retain.

### UNDERSTAND

Humans understand relevant capabilities, limitations and conditions.

### ASSESS

Humans can evaluate whether deployment is appropriate in context.

### CONSTRAIN

Humans can set meaningful boundaries.

### AUTHORIZE

An accountable authority approves the use.

### MONITOR

Relevant personnel receive information necessary for supervision.

### INTERVENE

Intervention or deactivation is available when technically and operationally feasible and appropriate.

### REASSESS

Humans can reassess when conditions materially change.

### ACCOUNT

Responsibility remains attributable to human institutions.

That is more useful than arguing over a slogan.

---

# 57. HUMAN CONTROL — DIRECT VS INDIRECT

Some systems may require direct human involvement.

Others may rely on indirect control through:

- programming;
- mission parameters;
- operating envelopes;
- target restrictions;
- doctrine;
- supervision;
- pre-set constraints.

The bloc should not require identical control architecture for every operational context.

The common principle is:

> **The human decision-making framework must have practical effect on the system's use of force.**

---

# 58. HUMAN CONTROL — THE SIX-PART TEST

When testing whether control is real, ask:

1. **Information** — Does the human have enough relevant information?
2. **Time** — Is there enough time to make the relevant decision?
3. **Authority** — Does the person actually have authority?
4. **Understanding** — Do they understand the system's capabilities and limits?
5. **Constraint** — Can they meaningfully bound the system?
6. **Intervention** — Can they intervene when intervention is feasible and required?

A person who merely presses “activate” is not automatically exercising meaningful control.

---

# 59. ACCOUNTABILITY — THE BASIC RULE

The bloc should reject the idea that the machine becomes the bearer of responsibility simply because an algorithm made the final operational selection.

The framework should preserve identifiable responsibility across:

- political authorization;
- military command;
- operational personnel;
- legal review;
- procurement;
- development/supplier processes;
- investigation.

Do not claim that every actor automatically has the same legal responsibility.

The important point is:

> **The responsibility chain must remain attributable and reconstructable.**

---

# 60. THE ACCOUNTABILITY RECORD

For a serious autonomous-system incident, a State should be able to reconstruct:

- who authorized the use;
- what system was used;
- what model/software version was active;
- what operating envelope applied;
- what target category was authorized;
- what geographic and temporal restrictions applied;
- what warnings or anomalies appeared;
- what interventions occurred;
- what changed before the incident;
- what happened after the incident.

This is why traceability matters.

---

# 61. LIFECYCLE ASSURANCE

The bloc should think of autonomy as a lifecycle:

**Development**
→ **Legal review**
→ **Testing**
→ **Procurement**
→ **Deployment authorization**
→ **Operation**
→ **Monitoring**
→ **Modification**
→ **Incident review**
→ **Retirement / transfer**

A system that was acceptable at one moment may become different later.

Therefore:

> **Assurance must continue.**

---

# 62. LEGAL REVIEW

The bloc should support legal review under applicable international law.

For States Parties to Additional Protocol I, Article 36 is a central treaty obligation.

Do not turn that into:

> “Article 36 applies identically to every State.”

Instead:

> “States should maintain appropriate legal review procedures under their applicable international obligations, with Article 36 remaining central for States to which it applies.”

This is legally safer and diplomatically easier.

---

# 63. TESTING — THE BLOC POSITION

The bloc should support testing that is:

- realistic;
- repeated where needed;
- appropriate to intended conditions;
- sensitive to edge cases;
- updated after material changes.

Testing should not be:

> “It worked once in the lab.”

It should examine the conditions in which the system is actually expected to operate.

---

# 64. TESTING MATRIX

A serious assurance process should consider, where relevant:

### NORMAL
Expected conditions.

### EDGE
Rare but plausible conditions.

### DEGRADED
Sensor, communication or environmental degradation.

### HOSTILE
Cyber/electronic/adversarial conditions.

### CIVILIAN-COMPLEX
Unexpected civilian presence and ambiguous environments.

### INTERACTION
Interaction with other systems.

### CHANGE
Material software/model/configuration changes.

### FAILURE
Failure-response behavior.

This gives the bloc concrete language when another delegate asks:

> “What do you actually mean by testing?”

---

# 65. MATERIAL CHANGE

The bloc should support reassessment after a **material change**.

Examples:

- new target-recognition capability;
- altered engagement logic;
- major software/model update;
- broader operating environment;
- new autonomous function;
- expanded target category;
- substantially changed expected effects.

Not every software patch must automatically trigger a full legal review.

The drafting challenge is defining what counts as **material**.

---

# 66. ADAPTIVE SYSTEMS

Adaptive AI creates a specific assurance problem.

The question is:

> “Can the State reasonably rely on the evidence from the original assessment after the system has changed?”

Potential controls:

- version identification;
- update logs;
- approved update channels;
- change thresholds;
- rollback capability where feasible;
- post-update testing;
- revalidation.

The bloc does not have to ban adaptation.

It needs to control **material adaptation**.

---

# 67. COMMUNICATIONS LOSS

The bloc should require a predefined and tested response to critical loss of communication or control where relevant.

Possible responses:

- abort;
- deactivate;
- safe return;
- restricted operation;
- loiter;
- transition to another predefined mode.

Do not force one universal outcome on every system.

The common requirement is:

> **Do not allow communication failure to silently create uncontrolled additional authority.**

---

# 68. CYBERSECURITY / ADVERSARIAL CONDITIONS

A system can be perfectly reliable under normal conditions and unreliable after:

- spoofing;
- jamming;
- sensor manipulation;
- adversarial input;
- cyber intrusion;
- corrupted data;
- contradictory signals.

The common bloc line:

> **Testing must challenge the assumptions on which autonomous operation depends.**

---

# 69. AUTOMATION BIAS

Human control can fail even when the person technically has authority.

Example:

A system gives a confident recommendation.

The operator assumes the machine is probably right.

The human technically approves it.

But the human's judgement is no longer meaningfully independent.

Therefore safeguards may include:

- training;
- confidence/uncertainty indicators;
- clear authority to reject recommendations;
- interface design;
- workload management;
- appropriate independent review.

The goal is not to ban decision-support.

The goal is to protect human judgement from becoming symbolic.

---

# 70. AI DECISION-SUPPORT BOUNDARY

The bloc should distinguish:

### AI SUPPORT

The system recommends, analyzes or assists.

### AUTONOMOUS WEAPON FUNCTION

The system itself performs a covered weapon function with reduced or absent human intervention.

A support system does not automatically become a LAWS merely because it is sophisticated.

However, high-risk decision-support may require safeguards when it materially shapes lethal targeting.

This distinction protects the framework from both under-regulation and over-regulation.

---

# 71. DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS

The bloc should not create a blanket exemption:

> “Defensive = safe.”

Instead examine:

- what is targeted;
- how the system identifies the target;
- what operating environment exists;
- how bounded the engagement is;
- how predictable the effects are;
- what human constraints exist;
- what happens during communications loss;
- whether the system can be interrupted;
- what evidence supports its use.

This allows urgent defensive contexts to be treated realistically without creating a loophole.

---

# 72. SWARMS

A swarm can create system-level risks that are not obvious from studying one platform.

Questions:

- What is the aggregate target set?
- Can the system expand engagements?
- What happens if communications are lost?
- What happens when many units interact?
- Can one malfunction propagate?
- What limits exist on scale and geography?
- Can humans still understand and control aggregate effects?

Potential safeguards:

- scale limits;
- geographic containment;
- target restrictions;
- system-level testing;
- communications-loss rules;
- system-wide logs;
- authorization before target-set expansion.

Do not automatically write:

> “All swarms are prohibited.”

Write the risk test first.

---

# 73. VERIFICATION — THE SHARED PRINCIPLE

The bloc should avoid both extremes.

### Extreme 1

> “Trust every State's self-assessment.”

### Extreme 2

> “Every State must reveal all sensitive technical details.”

The common position should be:

> **Verification must create credible evidence of compliance while protecting legitimate security and confidentiality concerns.**

---

# 74. VERIFICATION LADDER

A useful common architecture:

### LEVEL 1 — NATIONAL IMPLEMENTATION
National review and records.

### LEVEL 2 — GENERAL REPORTING
Description of laws, procedures and safeguards.

### LEVEL 3 — INCIDENT REPORTING
Serious incidents and lessons learned.

### LEVEL 4 — EXPERT EXCHANGE
Peer learning and technical/legal discussions.

### LEVEL 5 — RECORD-BASED ASSURANCE
Retention of auditable evidence.

### LEVEL 6 — PROTECTED TECHNICAL REVIEW
Experts review sensitive information under confidentiality arrangements where appropriate.

The exact institutional form remains negotiable.

---

# 75. CONFIDENTIALITY

The bloc should explicitly recognize that compliance mechanisms may involve sensitive information.

Possible protected information:

- source code;
- model architecture;
- military doctrine;
- detailed vulnerabilities;
- exact deployment configurations;
- sensitive operational data.

The answer is not:

> “No verification.”

The answer is:

> **“Design verification that reveals what must be demonstrated without unnecessarily exposing what need not be public.”**

---

# 76. SERIOUS INCIDENTS

A bloc framework should consider serious-incident review where there is:

- civilian harm;
- unexpected autonomous engagement;
- major loss of control;
- unauthorized activation;
- serious software malfunction;
- cyber compromise affecting autonomous functions.

The objective is:

**accountability + learning + corrective action.**

Not merely punishment.

---

# 77. INCIDENT → REVIEW LOOP

A serious incident should feed back into assurance.

**Incident**
→ **preserve records**
→ **investigate**
→ **identify failure**
→ **assess whether assumptions changed**
→ **retest**
→ **re-review if needed**
→ **correct system/procedure**
→ **document lesson**

This is a much stronger governance model than incident reporting alone.

---

# 78. PROCUREMENT

The bloc should treat procurement as a control point.

Before acquiring a system, States can require evidence of:

- legal review;
- testing;
- operating limits;
- documentation;
- update/change procedures;
- supplier responsibilities;
- cybersecurity;
- logging;
- training;
- support for incident investigation.

This prevents a State from discovering governance problems only after deployment.

---

# 79. SUPPLIER RESPONSIBILITY

The framework should consider requiring suppliers or developers, as appropriate, to support:

- system documentation;
- version tracking;
- change notifications;
- testing evidence;
- known limitations;
- cybersecurity information;
- incident cooperation;
- secure update processes.

Do not turn suppliers into substitutes for State responsibility.

The State remains responsible for deciding what it acquires and deploys.

---

# 80. TRANSFERS AND DIVERSION

The bloc should consider:

- transfer of prohibited systems;
- illicit procurement;
- theft/capture;
- unauthorized software access;
- modification of civilian platforms;
- diversion to non-State actors.

Possible mechanisms:

- end-use controls;
- transfer assurances;
- secure update mechanisms;
- loss/theft reporting;
- diversion cooperation;
- restrictions on transfer of prohibited systems.

This should remain connected to the LAWS problem rather than becoming a generic arms-control chapter.

---

# 81. NON-STATE ACTORS

The bloc should recognize that technology can move through:

- illicit procurement;
- captured equipment;
- stolen software;
- commercial components;
- modified civilian systems;
- intermediary supply chains.

But avoid pretending that every commercial AI component should automatically be regulated as a weapon.

Focus on:

**capability + intended use + transfer risk + safeguards.**

---

# 82. CAPACITY-BUILDING

Universal obligations require realistic implementation.

The bloc should explore:

- legal-review assistance;
- testing capacity;
- cybersecurity support;
- technical training;
- regional expert centres;
- procurement guidance;
- operator education;
- expert exchanges.

The principle:

> **Same core safety objective, different levels of technical assistance needed to reach it.**

---

# 83. REGIONAL CAPACITY HUBS

A useful proposal for lower-resource States could be regional or cross-regional expert hubs supporting:

- legal review;
- testing methodologies;
- assurance documentation;
- cybersecurity;
- operator training;
- technical evaluation;
- incident-learning exchanges.

This is particularly useful because a treaty with expensive implementation requirements can become unevenly applied in practice.

---

# 84. THE LEGAL-FORM ISSUE — HOW THE BLOC SHOULD HANDLE IT

This must remain disciplined.

### Brazil

> “We support a legally binding international instrument.”

### Some members may say

> “We support negotiations but want to keep the final form open.”

### Some bridge states may say

> “We prefer implementation of existing IHL and non-binding measures.”

The bloc should not collapse because of that.

Instead:

> **Agree on substance first.**

Then negotiate:

- mandate;
- timing;
- instrument type;
- protocol vs convention;
- political measures;
- review process.

---

# 85. THE LEGAL-FORM BRIDGE WORDING

For a common document, a safe interim formulation is:

> **“The group supports advancing from discussion toward a structured negotiating process based on the existing GGE elements, with the objective of durable international rules governing unacceptable autonomous weapon functions and safeguards for systems that remain permissible.”**

This creates common ground without forcing every member to sign off on one final legal form immediately.

---

# 86. BRAZIL'S NATIONAL INSERT

When the final document needs Brazil-specific language:

> **“Brazil supports the negotiation of a legally binding international instrument, building on the GGE's substantive work and combining clear prohibitions with strict regulation, effective human judgement and control, lifecycle assurance and accountability.”**

This is Brazil's sentence.

Do not present it as the bloc's sentence until the members explicitly adopt it.

---

# 87. RED LINES — WHAT BRAZIL SHOULD PROTECT

Within the bloc, Brazil should strongly protect:

### 1
There must be an actual prohibited category.

### 2
Permissible systems must still face strict safeguards.

### 3
Human judgement/control must be practically effective.

### 4
Responsibility cannot disappear into the machine.

### 5
Material changes need renewed assurance.

### 6
Serious incidents need investigation.

### 7
Verification must produce evidence.

### 8
Implementation must include capacity-building.

These are Brazil's core architecture.

---

# 88. WHAT BRAZIL CAN NEGOTIATE

Brazil can be flexible on:

- exact definition;
- exact phrase for human control;
- direct vs indirect control;
- precise Tier 1 threshold;
- reporting frequency;
- confidentiality;
- verification sequencing;
- technical expert structure;
- capacity-building mechanism;
- transfer language;
- timing and sequencing of negotiations.

This makes Brazil strong rather than rigid.

---

# 89. DO NOT TRADE THE WRONG THING

Bad trade:

> “We will remove human judgement requirements if you support a treaty.”

Why bad?

You just traded away substantive architecture to gain a legal-form label.

Better trade:

> “We can modify the wording of the control obligation if we preserve practical human judgement and accountability.”

Trade:

**form and implementation**

before trading:

**core safety architecture.**

---

# 90. THE CLAUSE NEGOTIATION FORMULA

Whenever a member objects to a clause:

### Step 1 — Ask why

> “What concern does this wording create?”

### Step 2 — Identify the objective

> “Do we still agree on the underlying objective?”

### Step 3 — Offer a narrower formulation

> “Would this wording solve your concern?”

### Step 4 — Protect the mechanism

> “Does the revised language still make the safeguard operational?”

### Step 5 — Record both versions

If unresolved:

**[A] / [B]**

Move on.

---

# 91. THE THREE DRAFTING STATES

Every clause should have one status.

### GREEN

Agreed wording.

### YELLOW

Substance agreed; wording still open.

### RED

Substance disputed.

Do not spend twenty minutes polishing a RED clause.

Draft GREEN clauses first.

Then YELLOW.

Return to RED after the rest of the architecture is visible.

---

# 92. HOW TO RUN THE FIRST REAL BLOC MEETING

### 0–5 MINUTES

Every delegation states:

> “Our two non-negotiables.”

Record them.

### 5–15 MINUTES

Identify:

- five points everyone broadly agrees on;
- five points where disagreement exists.

### 15–30 MINUTES

Convert broad agreement into clauses.

### 30–40 MINUTES

Assign clause owners.

### 40–50 MINUTES

Discuss outreach:

- who needs to be approached;
- what they can add;
- who will approach them.

### 50–60 MINUTES

Set:

- next meeting;
- document deadline;
- unresolved questions;
- responsible consolidator.

The group should leave with text, not just opinions.

---

# 93. EXACT FIRST-MEETING QUESTIONS

Ask in this order:

> “What is your one red line?”

> “What is one thing you absolutely want in the resolution?”

> “What is one thing you absolutely do not want?”

> “Which parts of human control do you think are essential?”

> “What should trigger prohibition?”

> “What should trigger re-review?”

> “How much verification would you accept?”

> “What implementation support is needed?”

Then:

> “Which clause do you want to draft?”

---

# 94. HOW TO HANDLE SIX DIFFERENT DEFINITIONS

Do not panic.

Create:

### Definition A

Functional.

### Definition B

Capability-based.

### Definition C

Outcome/effect-based.

Then ask:

> “Which functional elements appear in all three?”

Those shared elements become the basis of the working definition.

Definition disagreements are often easier to solve at the **element** level than at the sentence level.

---

# 95. HOW TO HANDLE HUMAN-CONTROL WORDING FIGHTS

Suppose:

Country A:

> “Meaningful human control.”

Country B:

> “Human judgement and control.”

Country C:

> “Appropriate human involvement.”

Do not spend the meeting deciding which phrase sounds best.

Ask:

> “What conditions do all three phrases actually require?”

Then extract:

- understanding;
- authority;
- constraints;
- time;
- information;
- intervention;
- accountability.

Draft those conditions.

The label can be solved later.

---

# 96. HOW TO HANDLE THE “IHL IS ENOUGH” ARGUMENT INSIDE THE BLOC

Do not say:

> “IHL is not enough.”

Say:

> “IHL remains the baseline. The question is which specific mechanisms help States apply and demonstrate compliance when autonomy changes how force is selected, constrained and monitored.”

Then use:

- operating envelope;
- testing;
- legal review;
- logging;
- material-change review;
- incident investigation.

That is a much more defensible argument.

---

# 97. HOW TO HANDLE “VERIFICATION WILL REVEAL SECRETS”

Answer:

> “The proposal does not require automatic public disclosure of source code or sensitive operational data. It creates a graduated system of evidence, reporting and protected technical review.”

Then ask:

> “What information would you consider sufficient to demonstrate compliance without compromising legitimate security?”

Turn the objection into a design requirement.

---

# 98. HOW TO HANDLE “THIS WILL BE TOO EXPENSIVE”

Answer:

> “That is exactly why the framework should include phased implementation and capacity-building rather than assume every State already has identical technical capacity.”

Then propose:

- regional testing hubs;
- expert exchanges;
- legal-review assistance;
- technical training;
- procurement guidance.

---

# 99. HOW TO HANDLE “THIS WILL STOP TECHNOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENT”

Answer:

> “The framework is focused on weapon functions and conditions of use, not on banning military AI research generally.”

Then distinguish:

- research;
- decision support;
- autonomous weapon functions;
- prohibited uses;
- regulated uses.

The bloc should regulate risk, not freeze all technology.

---

# 100. THE PUBLIC-FACING BLOC MESSAGE

When another delegate asks:

> “What is your bloc trying to do?”

Answer:

> **“We’re working on a practical LAWS framework. We want clear limits on unacceptable autonomous uses, strict safeguards for systems that remain permissible, effective human judgement and control, lifecycle review, accountability and realistic implementation.”**

Then:

> **“We’re still discussing some of the exact wording, so we’re interested in hearing what your delegation would change.”**

That second sentence is important.

It invites negotiation.

---

# 101. THE PRIVATE INTERNAL BLOC MESSAGE

When speaking to bloc members:

> **“We do not need identical national positions. We need a common architecture we can all defend.”**

Then:

> **“Let’s settle substance first and legal-form language second.”**

---

# 102. THE OUTREACH MESSAGE

When bringing in another country:

> **“Hey! We’re putting together a bloc for DISEC on the LAWS agenda. We’re working around clear restrictions on unacceptable autonomy, strong human judgement/control, testing and lifecycle safeguards. Would you like to join us?”**

If they say yes:

> **“Perfect. Which part of that would you be most interested in working on?”**

Do not send the entire policy paper in the first message.

---

# 103. THE “GIVE THEM A JOB” PRINCIPLE

Every serious new member should receive a concrete entry point.

Examples:

### Japan
Testing / predictability / technical safeguards.

### Australia
Operational safeguards / legal-review implementation.

### South Africa
Capacity-building / Global South implementation.

### Mexico
Human control / Latin American coordination.

### Switzerland
Verification / confidentiality.

### Netherlands
Legal architecture.

### Pakistan
Operational restrictions.

### Ireland
Accountability / human judgement.

### France
Two-tier threshold.

### Germany
Tier 1 / Tier 2 drafting.

This is a working-role map, not a fixed assignment.

---

# 104. THE “OWN YOUR CLAUSE” RULE

When a member helps draft a clause, ask them to be able to explain:

- why it is there;
- who implements it;
- how it works;
- what evidence proves compliance;
- what happens if it fails.

A delegate who can defend a clause is a better drafting partner than a delegate who merely joined the bloc.

---

A bloc should have:

### Main group

For:
- decisions;
- coordination;
- outreach;
- deadlines.

### Drafting document

For:
- clauses;
- comments;
- versions.

### Optional technical subgroup

For:
- definitions;
- testing;
- verification;
- technical language.

Do not bury the actual resolution inside 500 WhatsApp messages.

# 106. THE VERSION-CONTROL RULE

Every shared draft should have:

**V0.1 — structure**

**V0.2 — common principles**

**V0.3 — clauses**

**V0.4 — external feedback**

**V0.5 — negotiated wording**

**FINAL — sponsor-ready**

Never let three delegates circulate three contradictory “final” drafts.

# 107. THE “SOURCE BEFORE CLAIM” RULE

When a member says:

> “Country X definitely supports this.”

Ask:

> “Do we have the current source?”

Then distinguish:

- national statement;
- joint statement;
- group statement;
- UNGA vote;
- historical statement.

A UNGA YES vote alone is not proof of support for every clause in the bloc text.

---

# 108. WHAT COUNTS AS STRONG EVIDENCE IN THE BLOC

Useful evidence hierarchy:

### STRONGEST

Current official national statement / current CCW-GGE statement.

### STRONG

Official working paper.

### USEFUL

Current joint statement.

### CONTEXT

UNGA vote.

### BACKGROUND

Older national statement.

The evidence should determine how confidently the bloc describes a country's position.

# 109. THE “CURRENT VS HISTORICAL” LABEL

Whenever a country position appears in the internal document, mark it:

**CURRENT**

or

**HISTORICAL**

or

**GROUP-BASED**

or

**NEEDS VERIFICATION**

This prevents accidental overclaiming during committee.

# 112. THE “WHY DOES THIS EXIST?” TEST

For each clause, finish the sentence:

> “This clause exists because…”

If the answer is vague:

> “International cooperation is important…”

the clause is probably weak.

Better:

> “This clause exists because material software changes can invalidate earlier assumptions about system behavior.”

Then the mechanism:

> “Material changes affecting critical functions trigger renewed testing and legal review.”

That is useful drafting.

---

# 113. THE “WHO DOES WHAT?” TEST

Every serious clause should identify:

### STATE

What States must do.

### OPERATOR / AUTHORITY

What people within the national chain must do.

### SUPPLIER

What developers/manufacturers must provide where appropriate.

### INTERNATIONAL BODY

What the international mechanism does.

### EXPERTS

What technical/legal reviewers do.

This prevents vague institutional language.

---

# 114. THE “WHAT HAPPENS IF IT FAILS?” TEST

For every safeguard ask:

> “What happens if the safeguard fails?”

Examples:

### Testing failure
→ deployment not authorized until resolved.

### Material-change failure
→ system returns for reassessment.

### Communication failure
→ predefined tested response.

### Serious incident
→ investigation/review.

### Documentation failure
→ inability to demonstrate compliance.

This is where a resolution starts becoming operational.

# 115. THE BLOC'S NEGOTIATION MANTRA

Repeat:

> **“Protect the objective, negotiate the wording.”**

Not:

> “Protect our exact sentence.”

This is the easiest way to keep the bloc together.

---

# 116. THE BRAZIL ROLE INSIDE THE BLOC

Brazil should try to become the person who:

- summarizes;
- organizes;
- writes;
- integrates;
- asks for evidence;
- converts ideas into clauses;
- keeps deadlines;
- bridges countries.

Not simply the person who talks most.

The strongest practical signal of leadership is:

> **When the group gets stuck, the group knows you can structure the problem.**

---

# 117. WHEN BRAZIL SHOULD SPEAK FIRST

Speak first when:

- the group lacks structure;
- everyone is repeating the same principle;
- no one has converted it into text;
- there is confusion over what exactly is being decided.

Then say:

> “Let’s separate this into three questions.”

That instantly creates structure.

---

# 118. WHEN BRAZIL SHOULD NOT SPEAK FIRST

Do not jump in first when:

- a country is explaining a real concern;
- a bridge state is revealing what it can support;
- a member has a strong technical proposal;
- disagreement is useful for understanding the final clause.

Listen.

Then summarize:

> “So the concern is X, but we still agree on Y. Can we draft around Y?”

That is better bloc leadership.

---

# 119. THE “QUIET DELEGATE” RULE

Not everyone will speak in the group chat.

Ask directly:

> “Do you agree with this clause?”

or:

> “Would your delegation be comfortable defending this?”

This prevents the loudest delegates from accidentally becoming the whole bloc.

# 123. IF THE BLOC STARTS FALLING APART

Return to:

### QUESTION 1
What problem are we solving?

### QUESTION 2
What outcome do we all want?

### QUESTION 3
What minimum safeguard gets us there?

### QUESTION 4
What wording preserves everyone’s concern?

### QUESTION 5
Can we bracket the rest?

Do not restart the entire debate.

---

# 124. IF TWO DELEGATES START ARGUING ABOUT WORDS

Say:

> “Let's separate substance from wording. What does each side actually need the clause to achieve?”

Then write the shared objective.

Only after that return to wording.

---

# 125. IF A DELEGATE DEMANDS THEIR WORDING

Ask:

> “What legal or operational concern does that wording protect?”

If another formulation protects the same concern:

> “Can we preserve that protection without making the clause too narrow?”

This is how you negotiate without embarrassing anyone.

---

# 126. IF A DELEGATE WANTS TO PUT EVERYTHING IN THE RESOLUTION

Ask:

> “What problem does this solve?”

Then:

> “Does the committee need this as an operative obligation, or can it be handled through implementation guidance?”

This keeps the resolution focused.

---

# 127. IF A DELEGATE WANTS EVERYTHING TO BE A PRINCIPLE

Ask:

> “How does a State implement this tomorrow?”

If there is no answer, the bloc needs a mechanism.

---

# 128. IF A DELEGATE WANTS EVERYTHING TO BE TECHNICAL

Ask:

> “What legal or policy objective does this technical requirement serve?”

Technology should serve the governance objective.

# 129. THE FINAL BLOC QUALITY CHECK

Before treating the document as ready, ask:

### SCOPE
Can a delegate explain what is covered?

### PROHIBITION
Can they explain what is unacceptable and why?

### REGULATION
Can they explain what happens to everything else?

### CONTROL
Can they explain what humans must actually do?

### REVIEW
Can they explain when assurance happens?

### ACCOUNTABILITY
Can they explain who is responsible and what records exist?

### VERIFICATION
Can they explain how compliance can be demonstrated?

### IMPLEMENTATION
Can they explain how less-resourced States participate?

### LEGAL FORM
Can they distinguish common bloc substance from each country's national preference?

If yes, the bloc is ready to negotiate seriously.
