# LAWS TOPIC MASTER RESEARCH — FACTUAL KNOWLEDGE BASE
> Topic research only. This file deliberately does not choose MUN MODs or write speeches.

## 1. What is being regulated?

The CCW discussion concerns emerging technologies in the area of lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) and, in particular, how autonomy interacts with identification, selection and engagement of targets.

The 2026 GGE Chair's work uses a function-based approach: a weapon system can identify, select and engage targets without further human intervention after activation. The exact characterization and scope remain subjects of state debate.

Primary sources:
- https://meetings.unoda.org/meeting/79329
- https://meetings.unoda.org/meeting/79329/documents

## 2. Automation is not the same as autonomy

Research must distinguish:
- automated defensive reactions;
- decision-support systems;
- systems requiring human authorization;
- supervisory/on-the-loop systems;
- systems able to select and engage targets without further human intervention.

Do not collapse these categories into one label.

## 3. Human judgment and control

Relevant concepts include:
- human-in-the-loop;
- human-on-the-loop;
- human-out-of-the-loop;
- situational awareness;
- predictability;
- reliability;
- traceability;
- explainability;
- supervision;
- intervention;
- abort/deactivation;
- geographic and temporal constraints;
- mission-parameter restrictions.

Different states use different language. “Meaningful human control” and “context-appropriate human judgment and control” should not automatically be treated as legally identical.

## 4. International humanitarian law

Core IHL principles relevant to the debate include:
- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack;
- military necessity;
- protection of persons hors de combat;
- accountability.

The major legal-policy disagreement is not whether IHL applies to technologically new weapons. States generally accept that existing international law applies. The dispute concerns whether existing law is sufficient on its own and whether additional rules are required to address autonomy specifically.

## 5. Article 36 weapons reviews

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a State Party to determine whether development, acquisition or adoption of a new weapon, means or method of warfare would in some or all circumstances be prohibited by applicable international law.

Important distinctions:
- Article 36 is a national legal-review obligation for states bound by it;
- it does not itself create an international inspection regime;
- states differ over transparency, common criteria and peer review.

Primary:
https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36

## 6. Martens Clause

The Martens Clause is invoked in the debate concerning humanity and public conscience. It should not be paraphrased as a simple standalone “LAWS ban clause.” Its legal operation depends on the treaty context and the legal argument being made.

## 7. Prohibition and regulation

A recurring architecture is:
1. prohibit systems or uses considered inherently unacceptable or incapable of reliable compliance with IHL/human-control requirements;
2. impose positive obligations on other systems.

Possible regulatory obligations include:
- target restrictions;
- time/geographic limits;
- human judgment/control;
- predictability/reliability;
- supervision;
- intervention/deactivation;
- lifecycle controls;
- weapons reviews;
- accountability;
- information exchange.

## 8. Two-tier approaches

“Two-tier” generally means prohibitions plus regulations. The precise criteria differ by state.

Brazil has a documented prohibitions-plus-regulations position. France, Germany and other states have also described variants of this structure.

The UN Secretary-General and ICRC have called for specific prohibitions and restrictions rather than a blanket ban on every autonomous function.

Primary/current source:
https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/press-release/2026/08/renewed-call-united-nations-secretary-general-and-president

## 9. Direct autonomous targeting of humans

The 25 August 2026 joint UN Secretary-General/ICRC appeal identifies autonomous targeting of humans as a central concern and calls for new international rules with clear prohibitions and restrictions.

Primary:
https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/press-release/2026/08/renewed-call-united-nations-secretary-general-and-president

The ICRC's August 2026 working paper recommends a prohibition on autonomous weapon systems designed or used to target humans directly.

ICRC:
https://www.icrc.org/en/article/icrc-working-paper-regulate-autonomous-weapon-systems

## 10. Accountability

The policy problem becomes sharper when:
- system behaviour is difficult to predict;
- operators lack relevant information;
- parameters can change;
- responsibility is distributed across developers, commanders, operators and institutions.

Technology does not erase human legal responsibility. The detailed issue is how rules should structure control, documentation, attribution and accountability.

## 11. Verification and technical standards

Possible verification mechanisms include:
- testing;
- event logging;
- audit trails;
- traceability;
- explainability;
- certification;
- mission-parameter checks;
- geographic/time limits;
- representative-condition testing;
- incident reporting;
- review procedures;
- information exchange.

Brazil has proposed IEEE STD 7007-2021 as a possible technical reference. That is a **Brazilian proposal**, not an internationally adopted LAWS standard.

## 12. Lifecycle governance

Rules may address:
- design;
- development;
- acquisition;
- testing;
- training;
- deployment;
- activation;
- use;
- post-use review;
- modification or updating.

Updates and learning systems are particularly relevant to predictability and legal review.

## 13. Transfer and proliferation

Research questions:
- should prohibited systems be subject to transfer bans?
- what controls should apply to components?
- how can autonomous capabilities reach non-state armed groups?
- should end-use controls apply?
- how can developing states participate in verification and capacity-building?
- could regulation unintentionally entrench technological advantages?

UNGA Resolution 80/57 explicitly recognizes meaningful participation and capacity-building for developing countries.

## 14. CCW / GGE process

For 2026:
- first session: 2–6 March;
- second session: 31 August–4 September;
- Seventh Review Conference: 16–20 November 2026.

The GGE mandate is to formulate, **by consensus**, elements of an instrument **without prejudging its legal nature**, plus other possible measures.

Official:
https://meetings.unoda.org/meeting/79329

## 15. Current September 2026 state of play

This MUN occurs after the second 2026 GGE session and before the November Review Conference.

The 25 August 2026 UN Secretary-General/ICRC appeal says the accumulated GGE work should form the basis for negotiations on a legally binding instrument with clear prohibitions and restrictions.

UN Geneva:
https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/press-release/2026/08/renewed-call-united-nations-secretary-general-and-president

UN briefing on the same appeal:
https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/press-briefing/2026/08/un-information-service-geneva-press-briefing-2

The UN briefing also stresses that States remain the decision-makers and that negotiations on the legally binding instrument had not yet begun.

## 16. Current terminology traps

Avoid:
- “AI weapons are illegal” as a blanket claim.
- “IHL does not apply.”
- “Human-in-the-loop always means lawful.”
- “Any autonomous function is LAWS.”
- “Article 36 alone solves the problem.”
- “A YES vote means treaty support.”
- “The CCW already has a LAWS treaty.”
- “The GGE has negotiated a final treaty.”

Prefer:
- “Brazil's position is…”
- “the 2026 GGE elements…”
- “the current rolling text…”
- “some states argue…”
- “the ICRC recommends…”
- “the UNGA vote demonstrates support for the resolution, not necessarily for every proposed legal mechanism.”
