
# 01 — Master Agenda Map

Agenda: Establishing an International Regulatory Framework for the Development and Deployment of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems.

The agenda is not simply "ban killer robots." It is a multi-variable regulatory-design problem.

## 1. Scope
The first question is what capability is regulated.

Possible scope questions:
- autonomy in navigation only;
- autonomy in sensing or tracking;
- autonomy in identifying/classifying targets;
- autonomy in target selection;
- autonomy in engaging/applying force;
- swarms and systems of systems;
- defensive autonomous functions;
- AI decision-support.

The international debate generally focuses on autonomy in critical functions connected to the use of force.

## 2. Legal baseline
IHL already applies to the conduct of hostilities and weapons use. Core rules include distinction, proportionality, precautions in attack, the prohibition of indiscriminate attacks and restrictions on unnecessary suffering/superfluous injury.

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires States Parties to assess the legality of new weapons, means and methods of warfare at study, development, acquisition or adoption.

The existence of these rules does not by itself answer whether States should create additional LAWS-specific rules.

## 3. Human role
The main questions are:
- what must humans decide;
- how much information must they have;
- when must judgement occur;
- whether direct or indirect control is acceptable;
- whether constant real-time supervision is required;
- what happens when circumstances change;
- whether intervention/deactivation must be possible.

## 4. Prohibitions
Common proposals include prohibiting:
- systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or controlled;
- systems incapable by design of complying with IHL;
- autonomous systems designed or used to apply force directly against humans;
- systems operating outside an adequate human command/control structure.

These are proposals, not one universally accepted list.

## 5. Regulation of permitted systems
Possible controls include:
- target-type restrictions;
- military-object-only limits;
- geographic restrictions;
- time/duration limits;
- engagement-number or scale limits;
- limits on autonomous modification of targeting parameters;
- human oversight and intervention;
- testing and legal review;
- operator training;
- cybersecurity;
- audit trails;
- incident reporting;
- lifecycle re-review.

## 6. Accountability
A machine cannot itself be the bearer of legal responsibility. The regulatory challenge is to keep responsibility identifiable among commanders, operators, States and other actors across the lifecycle.

## 7. Verification
A realistic system may combine:
- national legal review;
- national implementation measures;
- transparency reports;
- audit records;
- incident reporting;
- expert exchanges;
- capacity-building;
- and, where politically feasible, international verification.

## 8. Institutional choice
Possible models include:
- CCW Protocol;
- standalone treaty;
- political declaration plus later treaty;
- layered regime combining binding prohibitions with nonbinding technical standards.

The major legal-form question is separate from the substantive question of what rules should exist.

## Core analytical frame

Scope → legal compliance → human judgement/control → prohibited category → regulated category → lifecycle → accountability → transparency/verification → international institution.
