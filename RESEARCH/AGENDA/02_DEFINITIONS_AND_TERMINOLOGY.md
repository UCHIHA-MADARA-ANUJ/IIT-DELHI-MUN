
# 02 — Definitions and Terminology

## 1. No single universal LAWS definition
International discussions have not produced one universally accepted definition covering every system.

The ICRC generally describes autonomous weapons as systems that, after activation, select and apply force to targets without human intervention.

The UN/CCW literature often focuses on autonomy in critical functions such as target selection and engagement.

## 2. Automation versus autonomy

Automation means a machine automatically performs a defined function.

Autonomy means a system can carry out a function after activation without a person directing each individual action, using information from the environment.

A weapons system can therefore be:
- automated but not autonomous in target selection;
- autonomous in navigation but human-controlled in engagement;
- autonomous in target selection and engagement.

## 3. AI is not synonymous with LAWS
AI can support:
- intelligence analysis;
- logistics;
- planning;
- image recognition;
- decision support;
- cyber defence.

Those applications do not automatically make a system a LAWS.

Conversely, autonomy can exist in systems that do not use contemporary machine learning.

## 4. Human-in/on/out-of-the-loop
These are useful analytical labels:
- in-the-loop: human participates in each critical engagement decision;
- on-the-loop: system can act within parameters while humans monitor and can intervene;
- out-of-the-loop: system performs critical functions without real-time human intervention.

These labels are not themselves universally accepted legal tests.

## 5. Meaningful human control
Meaningful human control is a major policy concept but should not be described as a universally codified treaty term or freestanding rule of customary international law.

The operational question is what makes human control meaningful:
- sufficient understanding;
- predictable effects;
- appropriate context;
- defined target/time/space limits;
- responsible command;
- intervention/deactivation where feasible;
- ability to respond to changed circumstances.

## 6. Lethality
"Lethal" can be ambiguous. A system can be designed to deliver potentially lethal force even if a specific engagement has a non-lethal outcome.

Pakistan's 2024 CCW proposal used a functional definition focused on weapons capable by design of applying lethal force rather than the actual result of a particular attack.

India's 2025 paper treated lethality as a function of system characteristics and effects and supported a narrower approach to scope.

## 7. Functional definitions
A functional definition regulates what the system can do rather than whether it is:
- a drone;
- a missile;
- a vehicle;
- a turret;
- a robot.

Functional language is more adaptable to technological change.

The Chair's September 2026 GGE explanation described the emerging characterization as capability-based and not dependent on a system's purpose or primary function.

## 8. AI decision support
An AI system that recommends a target to a human is not automatically a LAWS.

It can still matter because:
- automation bias may reduce genuine human judgement;
- time pressure can shrink deliberation;
- machine recommendations may become practically determinative;
- decision-support may feed autonomous systems.

A future framework may need to regulate interfaces with military AI without equating all military AI with LAWS.

## 9. Definition traps for MUN
Avoid:
- "The UN legally defines LAWS as..."
- "Meaningful human control is already customary IHL."
- "Every AI weapon is a LAWS."
- "Every drone is autonomous."
- "Every autonomous weapon is fully independent."

Prefer precise language:
- autonomy in critical functions;
- capability-based scope;
- context-dependent human judgement/control;
- existing IHL plus possible specific rules.
