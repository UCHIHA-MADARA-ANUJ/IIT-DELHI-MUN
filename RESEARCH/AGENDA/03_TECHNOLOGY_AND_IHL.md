
# 03 — Technology and the International-Law Baseline

## A. Technical functions that matter

Autonomy can appear in:
1. perception/detection;
2. classification;
3. tracking;
4. target selection;
5. engagement;
6. navigation;
7. battle-damage assessment;
8. re-planning.

The most sensitive functions for LAWS governance are those tied directly to selecting and applying force.

## B. Predictability
Predictability can be affected by:
- environment;
- sensor limitations;
- software updates;
- machine-learning behaviour;
- adversarial interference;
- communications loss;
- unexpected target patterns;
- interaction with other autonomous systems.

The legal issue is not whether technology is perfect. It is whether the system can be used within the rules applicable to the attack in the circumstances where it is employed.

## C. Core IHL principles

### Distinction
Attacks must be directed only against lawful military objectives.

### Proportionality
Expected incidental civilian harm must not be excessive relative to anticipated concrete and direct military advantage.

### Precautions
Those who plan or decide on attacks must take feasible precautions to avoid or minimize civilian harm.

### Indiscriminate attacks
Weapons/methods that cannot be directed at a specific military objective or whose effects cannot be limited as required by IHL can raise fundamental legality problems.

### Unnecessary suffering / superfluous injury
Applicable IHL rules also restrict means and methods causing unnecessary suffering or superfluous injury.

## D. Article 36
Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a State Party, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would be prohibited by the Protocol or another applicable rule of international law.

For MUN, say:
"States Parties to Additional Protocol I have an Article 36 treaty obligation."

Do not say every UN member has exactly the same Article 36 treaty obligation.

## E. Why autonomy pressures existing law
The central practical problem is that IHL rules assume a chain of human legal judgement. Autonomy changes:
- who performs classification;
- how quickly targets are selected;
- how many engagements can happen;
- how much information humans have;
- how easily humans can intervene;
- how predictably the system behaves.

This is why States can agree that IHL applies while still debating the need for LAWS-specific rules.

## F. Human responsibility
The ICRC and the 2026 GGE work both stress that responsibility cannot be transferred to a machine.

The relevant question becomes:
How do we keep responsibility attributable and traceable when software performs critical functions?

## G. Martens Clause and public conscience
The autonomous-weapons debate also invokes humanity and the dictates of public conscience. This is important to ethical/legal argument, but do not confuse it with a simple standalone ban.

## H. Important legal distinction
Three separate claims must not be collapsed:
1. existing IHL applies;
2. certain systems may be incompatible with IHL in particular circumstances;
3. States may choose to create additional treaty prohibitions or restrictions even where IHL already governs.
