
# 04 — Human Judgement and Human Control

## 1. Why this is the centre of the debate
"Human control" can refer to very different things:
- deciding to procure a system;
- setting mission parameters;
- authorizing activation;
- monitoring operation;
- assessing changing circumstances;
- retaining intervention or deactivation capability.

The international debate is about which of these are required and when.

## 2. Four-layer model

Strategic control:
Human authorities define whether and why the capability may be used.

Mission control:
Humans define task, target category, geography, duration and engagement limits.

Operational control:
Humans monitor and reassess use as circumstances change.

Technical control:
The system permits constraints, interruption, safe failure and appropriate deactivation.

A treaty could require several layers without requiring constant direct control over every machine action.

## 3. Direct versus indirect control
The 2026 GGE debated language allowing human control to be exercised directly or indirectly.

Arguments for flexibility:
- some defensive systems operate faster than humans can react;
- communications may fail;
- continuous live supervision is not always feasible;
- autonomy can be bounded through pre-use restrictions.

Arguments for stronger human involvement:
- a prior authorization may not be enough if circumstances change;
- human judgement must have practical effect on use;
- operational restrictions should not become mere formalities.

## 4. Predictability
Human judgement is meaningful only if likely effects can be reasonably understood.

Important variables:
- target type;
- environment;
- time;
- geographic area;
- duration;
- number of engagements;
- system reliability;
- ability to change or stop the mission.

## 5. Automation bias
A human operator may technically remain "in the loop" while practically deferring to the machine because:
- time is compressed;
- the system appears more accurate;
- the operator lacks confidence to override;
- institutional incentives favour machine outputs.

Therefore training, interface design and doctrine matter, not just the existence of a human button.

## 6. Deactivation
"Kill switch" language is too simplistic.

The policy question is:
- who has authority;
- what conditions trigger intervention;
- how reliably the command reaches the system;
- what happens after communications failure;
- whether deactivation is possible in the relevant environment.

## 7. Practical regulatory package
A robust control framework can be built from:
Human judgement + predictable effects + bounded target/time/space parameters + responsible chain of command + intervention/deactivation + lifecycle testing.

## 8. 2026 GGE fault line
Some delegations argued that human control should not become a new standalone legal standard. Others wanted language strong enough to ensure human judgement cannot become merely nominal or remote.

That disagreement is important for MUN because it lets you distinguish:
- control as a policy requirement;
- control as a legal standard;
- control as a set of concrete operational obligations.
