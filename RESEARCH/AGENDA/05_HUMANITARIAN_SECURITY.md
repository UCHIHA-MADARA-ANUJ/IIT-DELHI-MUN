
# 05 — Humanitarian, Ethical and Security Dimensions

## A. Civilian protection
Concerns include:
- misclassification of civilians;
- inability to recognize surrender or hors de combat status;
- failure to adapt when civilians enter an operating area;
- unpredictable or excessive incidental effects;
- large numbers of simultaneous engagements.

These are risk pathways, not proof that every autonomous system causes unlawful harm.

## B. Direct autonomous targeting of humans
The ICRC recommends prohibiting autonomous weapons designed or used to apply force directly against persons.

The argument is humanitarian and ethical as well as legal:
- human status can change;
- context can be complex;
- machines cannot bear legal/moral responsibility;
- autonomous human-targeting raises concerns about dignity and dehumanization.

Some States converge on some form of restriction; the exact legal formulation remains contested.

## C. Security and strategic stability
Pakistan has connected autonomy with arms-race, escalation and strategic-stability risks. China has similarly stressed avoiding miscalculation and arms races and keeping AI applications secure, reliable and controllable.

Potential destabilizing mechanisms:
- compressed decision time;
- automated retaliation/response;
- more simultaneous engagements;
- lower perceived human cost;
- rapid deployment by multiple actors;
- cyber or electronic interference.

These are plausible risk mechanisms, not predictions.

## D. Lower threshold for force
The UN/ICRC have warned that perceived reductions in risk to military personnel may make the use of force easier to contemplate and can contribute to escalation.

## E. Autonomous-on-autonomous interaction
If opposing systems interact rapidly, problems can include:
- false identification;
- cascading responses;
- spoofing;
- loss of attribution;
- uncertain escalation.

## F. Ethical dimension
A central ethical question is whether there are decisions in warfare that should never be delegated away from humans.

This is not a substitute for IHL analysis; it is a separate normative question.

## G. Potential military benefits
States may also see benefits:
- reduced risk to personnel;
- faster reaction;
- improved protection;
- precision in constrained environments;
- reduced human fatigue/error.

A serious regulatory framework has to address both risk and legitimate military utility.

## H. Non-State actors
Proliferation of commercial robotics, software and sensors can reduce barriers to acquiring or adapting autonomous capabilities.

That creates questions about:
- illicit transfers;
- commercial diversion;
- secure supply chains;
- preventing unauthorized access;
- capacity of States to regulate private actors.
