
# 07 — Prohibition, Restriction and Regulatory Models

## Model 1: Nonbinding responsibility framework
Could include:
- common principles;
- legal reviews;
- testing;
- transparency;
- training;
- lifecycle safeguards.

Strength: easier to adopt politically.  
Limitation: does not create new binding prohibitions.

## Model 2: Binding CCW protocol
Could create:
- legal definitions;
- prohibited categories;
- mandatory restrictions;
- national implementation;
- reporting;
- review mechanism.

Strength: builds on an existing IHL/disarmament institution.  
Limitation: negotiations and consensus can be difficult.

## Model 3: Standalone treaty
Would create a dedicated regime.

Strength:
- treaty can be written specifically for autonomous weapons.

Challenges:
- relationship with CCW;
- institutional duplication;
- universalization;
- verification design.

## Model 4: Layered regime
Potentially combines:
1. binding prohibitions;
2. binding positive obligations;
3. national legal reviews;
4. procurement and testing standards;
5. transparency;
6. capacity-building;
7. periodic review.

This addresses the reality that a treaty alone cannot test or operate a weapon.

## Two-tier substantive model

A recurring approach is:
Tier 1: prohibit unacceptable systems/uses.
Tier 2: strictly regulate other autonomous weapons.

Common candidate prohibition categories:
- direct autonomous targeting of humans;
- systems whose effects cannot be adequately predicted/controlled;
- systems incapable by design of complying with IHL;
- systems without adequate human responsibility/control.

Common candidate restrictions:
- target type;
- military objectives;
- geography;
- time/duration;
- scale/number of engagements;
- human oversight/intervention;
- no autonomous changes to critical targeting parameters;
- legal review;
- testing;
- operator training.

## Design variable: regulate by design, use, or both

Design-based rules can catch inherently unsafe systems before deployment.

Use-based rules can account for context.

A combined framework can be more comprehensive but more complicated.

## Design variable: direct versus indirect control

A strict direct-control rule is clearer but may exclude systems with very short reaction times or communications constraints.

A contextual indirect-control rule is more flexible but can become too vague unless paired with measurable safeguards.

## Design variable: classified information

A framework can require:
- national record retention;
- confidential disclosure to competent national authorities;
- aggregate public reporting;
- protected international expert exchange.

This is more realistic than assuming military source code should become public.

## Design variable: capacity-building

Developing States may need:
- legal-review assistance;
- testing capacity;
- technical experts;
- procurement guidance;
- cybersecurity support.

Without capacity-building, formally equal obligations can become unequal in practice.
