
# 09 — 2026 Status and MUN Context

## What happened before the MUN

2–6 March 2026:
First GGE session.

31 August–4 September 2026:
Second and final GGE session.

4 September 2026:
The GGE concluded its mandate with consensus adoption of its final report.

7–9 September 2026:
PrepCom for the Seventh CCW Review Conference.

16–20 November 2026:
Scheduled Seventh CCW Review Conference.

## Why the MUN date matters

The IIT Delhi MUN is on 26–27 September 2026.

Therefore delegates are speaking:
- after the final GGE session;
- after the PrepCom;
- before the Review Conference.

The practical question is no longer just "should the UN study LAWS?"

The debate is closer to:
- what should be done with the GGE outcome;
- whether States should launch negotiations;
- what substantive elements should be carried forward;
- what institution and mandate should be used;
- what interim measures can be implemented nationally.

## Current diplomatic pressure

On 25 August 2026, the UN Secretary-General and ICRC President renewed their call for urgent international rules, including clear prohibitions and restrictions.

On 28 August 2026, the ICRC published a working paper arguing that the GGE elements should become a basis for negotiations and specifically recommended retaining or adding prohibitions on autonomous targeting of humans and restrictions to military objectives by nature.

On 14 September 2026, Sri Lanka called for an explicit negotiating mandate for a legally binding instrument and highlighted human judgement/control across the lifecycle.

The diplomatic environment is therefore moving from general discussion toward a decision about the next negotiating phase.

## What the GGE mandate means

The GGE mandate was to formulate elements of an instrument without prejudging its legal nature.

Therefore:
- the GGE text is not a treaty;
- consensus on the report does not mean all States prefer every clause;
- the Review Conference still matters for the legal and institutional next step.

## High-value MUN questions

1. What exact systems are in scope?
2. Which uses are prohibited?
3. What is the standard of human judgement/control?
4. Can indirect control be enough?
5. What operating limits are mandatory?
6. What legal review is required?
7. What testing must occur before deployment?
8. What updates trigger renewed review?
9. What incident data must be retained or reported?
10. How can developing States implement the framework?
11. How are transfers and non-State actors handled?
12. What should the November 2026 Review Conference decide?

## Strategic note for the Brazil delegation

Do not choose a bloc from broad slogans before completing the 86-country issue comparison.

First find:
- countries agreeing on prohibited categories;
- countries agreeing on human-control language;
- countries agreeing on CCW/Protocol architecture;
- countries agreeing on lifecycle/testing;
- countries agreeing on capacity-building and implementation.

Then bloc architecture can be built from concrete overlaps.
