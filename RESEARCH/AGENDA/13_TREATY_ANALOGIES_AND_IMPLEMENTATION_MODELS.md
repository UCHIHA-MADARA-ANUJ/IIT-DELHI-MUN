
# 13 — Treaty Analogies and Implementation Models

A LAWS framework should be designed with knowledge of how existing arms-control and humanitarian instruments implement rules.

## 1. CCW Protocol V — reporting model

Protocol V on Explosive Remnants of War uses recurring national implementation reports and a centralized database.

The model demonstrates that a CCW protocol can combine:
- treaty obligations;
- national implementation;
- standardized reporting;
- recurring updates;
- an international information repository.

### Transferable lesson for LAWS

A future LAWS instrument could ask States to report on:
- national legal-review procedures;
- responsible authorities;
- testing/evaluation frameworks;
- training requirements;
- national implementation measures;
- aggregate categories of systems covered.

It should avoid requiring open publication of sensitive operational data.

## 2. Amended Protocol II — conditional restrictions

The CCW's mine/booby-trap regime shows how a treaty can combine:
- prohibition of certain methods;
- design requirements;
- precautions;
- restrictions tied to environment or use;
- lifecycle-related controls.

### Transferable lesson

LAWS regulation does not have to be binary. A treaty can prohibit a defined class while imposing operational limits on other classes.

## 3. Anti-Personnel Mine Ban Convention

The international mine-ban process illustrates what happens when States conclude that restrictions are insufficient for a category they regard as unacceptable.

Implementation features include:
- prohibition;
- destruction obligations;
- reporting;
- international cooperation;
- assistance.

### Transferable lesson

Where a prohibited category can be clearly characterized, treaty design can move from use restrictions to full lifecycle controls.

## 4. Convention on Cluster Munitions

The cluster-munitions model combines:
- prohibitions;
- destruction;
- clearance;
- victim assistance;
- national implementation;
- reporting;
- international cooperation.

### Transferable lesson

A weapons treaty can address humanitarian consequences beyond the moment of use.

For LAWS, that suggests considering:
- incident investigation;
- assistance/capacity-building;
- technical cooperation;
- lifecycle control.

## 5. Arms Trade Treaty as a transfer analogue

The Arms Trade Treaty shows a model in which transfer controls are separated from the substantive characteristics of the weapon itself.

### LAWS relevance

A future framework could potentially distinguish:
- prohibited autonomous weapons and components;
- regulated systems;
- ordinary AI technology with civilian or non-weapon uses.

This is important for avoiding a definition that inadvertently creates blanket restrictions on civilian AI.

## 6. ICRC's 2025 analysis of existing weapon regulation

The ICRC's 2025/2026 position paper explicitly draws lessons from:
- anti-personnel mines;
- booby traps;
- explosive remnants;
- other regulated weapons.

It notes that regulation historically developed iteratively: initial restrictions can be strengthened if experience shows they are insufficient.

### Key design implication

The international community does not have to choose between:
"do nothing" and "ban everything."

It can build:
- prohibitions for clearly unacceptable categories;
- conditional restrictions for others;
- implementation measures;
- review clauses for technological change.

## 7. Review mechanisms

Existing CCW and humanitarian treaties rely heavily on recurring conferences, expert meetings and national reporting.

A LAWS framework should anticipate technological change by including:
- periodic review;
- technical expert exchange;
- updating of guidance;
- opportunities to strengthen rules;
- treatment of material technological developments.

## 8. Verification realism

The hardest verification problem is that software is not a stable physical object.

A realistic regime can emphasize **verification of processes and assurance** rather than attempting universal inspection of source code.

Possible evidence:
- legal-review records;
- test protocols/results;
- configuration histories;
- change-management records;
- incident logs;
- national certification decisions;
- aggregate implementation reports.

## 9. Confidentiality

A credible treaty should distinguish:
- public transparency;
- confidential national reporting;
- protected expert exchanges;
- classified operational information.

The goal is accountability without forcing States to disclose tactical vulnerabilities.

## 10. Capacity-building

Existing humanitarian treaties use cooperation and assistance mechanisms.

For LAWS, capacity-building could cover:
- Article 36/legal-review assistance;
- technical testing;
- cyber assurance;
- procurement guidance;
- operator training;
- expert exchanges.

This is especially relevant for developing States.
