
# 18 — Deep Dive: Prohibitions

## The central question

A regulatory framework becomes concrete when it identifies what States should never develop, acquire, transfer, deploy or use.

International debate has repeatedly returned to a two-tier architecture:

**Tier 1:** prohibit specified unacceptable autonomous weapons or uses.  
**Tier 2:** impose strict controls on other autonomous systems.

The disagreement is over the exact threshold.

## Candidate prohibition 1 — Autonomous force against humans

The ICRC has consistently advocated prohibiting autonomous weapons that are designed or used to apply force directly against persons.

The argument combines:
- civilian protection;
- the difficulty of predicting human behaviour/status;
- dignity and humanity concerns;
- responsibility and accountability.

This is a policy proposal, not a universally accepted treaty rule.

## Candidate prohibition 2 — Systems that cannot comply with IHL

A system could be prohibited where it is incapable by design of being used consistently with:
- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions;
- other applicable IHL constraints.

This formulation is attractive because it anchors the prohibition in existing law, but it raises an operational question: who determines capability, on what testing evidence, and at what level of certainty?

## Candidate prohibition 3 — Unpredictable/uncontrollable effects

Possible threshold:
Prohibit systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or controlled in the intended operational context.

This can capture unsafe systems regardless of platform or algorithm type.

It is technically harder to implement because predictability is context-dependent.

## Candidate prohibition 4 — No meaningful human control

A system could be prohibited if no human can exercise an adequate level of judgement and control over critical use-of-force functions.

This formulation requires a precise operational test to avoid a purely rhetorical standard.

## Candidate prohibition 5 — Explicitly autonomous changes in critical parameters

A future instrument could forbid a weapon from autonomously changing:
- target categories;
- geographic restrictions;
- mission duration;
- engagement permissions;
where such changes would materially alter the legal basis for use.

This may be more practical as a restriction than as a universal prohibition.

## Design questions

### Design vs use
Should the framework prohibit a system because of inherent characteristics, or only prohibit specified uses?

### Development vs possession
Should prohibited categories be prohibited from:
- development;
- production;
- acquisition;
- stockpiling;
- transfer;
- deployment;
- use?

### Direct human targeting
Should the prohibition be tied to "persons" specifically, or to circumstances in which IHL requires context-sensitive human judgement?

### Military objectives
For permitted systems, should autonomous targeting be restricted to specified categories of military objectives?

## Two-tier architecture

A coherent system could say:

### Tier 1
Prohibited:
- autonomous direct application of force against humans;
- systems incapable of IHL compliance;
- systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently bounded/predicted;
- systems lacking an effective human command/control mechanism.

### Tier 2
Permitted only under:
- target restrictions;
- geographic limits;
- time limits;
- scale limits;
- pre-use legal review;
- rigorous testing;
- human judgement/control;
- intervention/deactivation where feasible;
- traceability;
- incident investigation.

The exact list is a negotiating package, not existing treaty law.

## Important State variations

China's 2022 proposal used a different route: distinguish unacceptable and acceptable autonomous weapons, with criteria including human intervention/control, impossibility of termination, indiscriminate killing and certain forms of autonomous evolution.

India's 2025 position supports prohibiting fully autonomous systems that cannot comply with IHL, while regulating other LAWS.

Pakistan's 2024 proposal combines prohibitions with contextual restrictions and a CCW Protocol approach.

These differences show that "ban vs no ban" is too crude; the real negotiations concern the threshold and drafting.
