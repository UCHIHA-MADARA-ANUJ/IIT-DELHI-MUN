
# 19 — Deep Dive: Human Control

## The most contested practical concept

The international debate increasingly treats human judgement/control as something that must be operationalized rather than repeated as a slogan.

The 2026 GGE Chair's summary explicitly placed "context-appropriate human judgement and control" in the LAWS-specific box of the rolling text, explaining that it was treated as a notion required for compliance rather than as an existing IHL obligation in those exact words.

That distinction is extremely important.

## What human control could mean

### 1. Pre-use judgement
A human decides:
- mission;
- target class;
- geography;
- duration;
- acceptable effects;
- engagement limits.

### 2. Operational judgement
Humans reassess whether the system remains appropriate as circumstances change.

### 3. Intervention
A human can:
- modify parameters;
- halt the mission;
- deactivate the system;
where technically and operationally feasible.

### 4. Responsibility
An identifiable person remains responsible for authorizing and supervising the use of force.

## Direct vs indirect control

### Direct model
Human authorizes each engagement or target.

Advantages:
- clear human role;
- easier to describe legally.

Problems:
- may be impossible in high-speed defensive contexts;
- communications can fail;
- may remove legitimate uses of bounded autonomy.

### Indirect/contextual model
Humans establish constraints and supervise within a bounded operational environment.

Advantages:
- accommodates reaction-time constraints;
- compatible with some defensive systems;
- easier to integrate with autonomy.

Risks:
- "human control" can become nominal;
- broad mission authorization may effectively delegate target selection.

## Conditions that make indirect control meaningful

A contextual model becomes stronger when combined with:
- predictable effects;
- constrained target set;
- limited geography;
- limited duration;
- limited scale;
- appropriate human training;
- reliable intervention/deactivation;
- system logs;
- legal review;
- reassessment when conditions change.

## Automation bias

A formal human operator can still fail to exercise genuine judgement if:
- the system's recommendation is treated as presumptively correct;
- time pressure is extreme;
- the operator cannot understand limitations;
- institutional incentives punish overrides;
- the interface hides uncertainty.

Therefore human-control regulation should include training, testing and interface/doctrine considerations, not merely a requirement for "one human in the chain."

## Human-control ladder

A useful analytical ladder is:

1. Human decision to possess/use.
2. Human mission authorization.
3. Human definition of target/context limits.
4. Human monitoring and reassessment.
5. Human intervention capability.
6. Human post-use review/accountability.

The more critical the autonomy function and the more complex the environment, the more of these safeguards may be needed.

## Key negotiation questions

- Must a human choose every specific target?
- Can human target categories be enough?
- Can indirect control satisfy the framework?
- When is constant real-time supervision impossible?
- What happens when communication is lost?
- What counts as meaningful intervention?
- How quickly must deactivation work?
- What evidence proves that humans actually had control?

## Research conclusion

The strongest drafting approach is to regulate **conditions of control** rather than debate the abstract humanity of the machine.
