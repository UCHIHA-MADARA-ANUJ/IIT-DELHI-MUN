
# 20 — Deep Dive: Regulation, Lifecycle, Verification

## The agenda after the ban

A credible regime must explain what happens to systems that are not prohibited.

## 1. Lifecycle

### Research
Identify prohibited capabilities and foreseeable risks.

### Development
Document:
- intended functions;
- limitations;
- failure modes;
- autonomy parameters;
- cybersecurity controls.

### Legal review
Assess compliance with applicable international law.

### Procurement
Contracts can require:
- documentation;
- testing evidence;
- update controls;
- cybersecurity;
- auditability.

### Testing
Test:
- expected conditions;
- edge cases;
- degraded sensors;
- communications loss;
- cyber/electronic interference;
- unexpected civilian presence;
- interaction with other autonomous systems.

### Deployment
Set:
- mission;
- target category;
- area;
- duration;
- scale;
- human-control arrangements.

### Operation
Monitor performance and preserve evidence.

### Modification
Material modifications affecting critical functions should trigger renewed review.

### Retirement/transfer
Prevent unauthorized reuse and uncontrolled transfer.

## 2. Article 36 and national review

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I is an important legal-review model but its treaty basis does not bind all States identically.

A future framework can:
- reinforce national Article 36 procedures;
- encourage equivalent legal-review processes for non-AP I States;
- establish minimum elements for autonomous-systems review;
- share review methodology.

## 3. Testing as evidence

Testing should not be a one-time certificate.

Evidence may include:
- test protocols;
- results;
- failure-mode analysis;
- operating envelopes;
- software/model version;
- change history;
- operator training.

## 4. Traceability

Useful records:
- who authorized the mission;
- what parameters were active;
- software/model version;
- system warnings;
- interventions;
- relevant sensor information;
- incident records.

Not all records need public release.

## 5. Verification

### Level A — National processes
Legal reviews, certification and command procedures.

### Level B — Transparency
Reports on national frameworks.

### Level C — Peer exchange
Experts compare methods.

### Level D — Incident reporting
Serious incidents are preserved and potentially reported.

### Level E — Confidential audit
Protected review of records/testing methodology.

### Level F — International inspection
Potentially possible for narrow questions, but sovereignty/security concerns are highest.

## 6. Procurement as implementation

A treaty that ignores procurement can become difficult to enforce.

Potential rules:
- suppliers document system limitations;
- material updates are disclosed to authorities;
- contracts preserve audit rights;
- cybersecurity controls are maintained;
- records survive personnel turnover.

## 7. Industry

Private companies may supply:
- models;
- software;
- sensors;
- robotics;
- communications;
- data.

The State remains the key international-law actor, but supplier duties can support State compliance.

## 8. Transfers and non-State actors

Potential tools:
- export controls;
- end-user controls;
- secure software/update procedures;
- transfer prohibitions for prohibited systems;
- international cooperation against diversion.

## 9. Capacity-building

A framework should consider assistance for:
- legal-review institutions;
- technical testing;
- cybersecurity;
- training;
- procurement;
- expert exchanges.

## 10. Confidentiality

Transparency must not equal disclosure of operational vulnerabilities.

A realistic regime can separate:
- public aggregate information;
- confidential national reporting;
- protected expert review;
- classified information.

## Research conclusion

Verification should focus first on **demonstrable processes and assurance evidence**, not on the unrealistic assumption that States will publish complete source code.
