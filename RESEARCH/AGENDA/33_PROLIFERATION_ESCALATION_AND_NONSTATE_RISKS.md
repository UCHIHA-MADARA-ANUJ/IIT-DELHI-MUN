# 33 — Proliferation, Escalation and Non-State Risks

## 1. Proliferation

Armed UAVs and autonomous/AI-integrated systems are becoming more versatile and more accessible.

SIPRI's 2026 assessment describes rapid proliferation of armed UAVs and movement toward autonomous, AI-integrated and swarm-capable systems, while existing export-control arrangements cover only parts of the threat spectrum. citeturn534274search4turn534274search66

## 2. Non-State actors

Regulation should consider:
- commercial drone availability;
- open-source software;
- off-the-shelf sensors;
- illicit procurement;
- stolen/captured systems;
- transfer of military autonomy software;
- modification of civilian platforms.

Possible measures:
- secure software distribution;
- end-use controls;
- transfer restrictions for prohibited systems;
- international cooperation against diversion;
- reporting of material loss/theft;
- standards for high-risk autonomous components.

## 3. Escalation

Autonomy can compress the time available for:
- interpretation;
- communication;
- human intervention;
- diplomatic de-escalation.

Swarms intensify this because multiple units may interact at once.

## 4. Flash-war problem

A useful regulatory response is not necessarily "ban all swarms."

Instead consider:
- geographic containment;
- target restrictions;
- communication-loss behavior;
- maximum engagement rate;
- automatic halt conditions;
- human authorization for expansion of target sets;
- deconfliction mechanisms between autonomous systems.

## 5. Nuclear and strategic stability

A separate risk exists where conventional autonomous systems interact with:
- early-warning systems;
- dual-capable missile environments;
- nuclear command-and-control;
- high-alert postures.

A LAWS framework could therefore include a confidence-building provision discouraging autonomous systems from taking actions that could be interpreted as strategic/nuclear attack triggers.

## 6. Cyber and electronic warfare

The system should be tested against:
- spoofing;
- jamming;
- adversarial inputs;
- communications loss;
- sensor degradation;
- GPS/GNSS denial.

## 7. Emergency states

A framework should predefine what happens when:
- command link is lost;
- localization fails;
- sensors disagree;
- target classification becomes uncertain;
- system leaves its operational envelope.

Possible defaults:
- abort;
- safe return;
- loiter;
- deactivate.

The appropriate default will vary by weapon/environment, so the treaty may need a context-based standard rather than one universal mode.

## 8. Current technology trend

Recent 2026 reporting shows military planners investing heavily in drone autonomy and swarm concepts. SIPRI also reports a trend toward versatile AI-integrated platforms and swarm operations. citeturn534274search4turn534274search66

The policy implication is not that every autonomous drone is a LAWS. It is that the framework needs technology-neutral rules capable of covering increasing levels of autonomy without treating every unmanned system identically.
