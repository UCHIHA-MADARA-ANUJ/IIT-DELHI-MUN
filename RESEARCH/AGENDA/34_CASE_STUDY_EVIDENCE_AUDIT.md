# 34 — Case-Study Evidence Audit

## Why this exists

The Background Guide uses real conflicts to make the topic concrete. Those examples must be handled carefully because claims about autonomy can be disputed.

## Libya / Kargu-2

The UN Panel of Experts on Libya is the primary institutional source normally cited for the 2020 Kargu-2 discussion.

For MUN:
- use the UN report for what was documented;
- distinguish the report's description from independent confirmation of full autonomous operation;
- do not treat the case as proof that every loitering munition is autonomous.

## Nagorno-Karabakh

Harop and other loitering systems illustrate:
- varying levels of human involvement;
- sensor-based autonomy;
- target-selection issues;
- the need for a functional rather than platform-based definition.

Do not call every loitering munition LAWS.

## Israel / AI-enabled targeting

This case is particularly sensitive.

The IDF officially states that Lavender and Habsora are intelligence-processing tools and denies that they autonomously select attack targets or generate attack orders. citeturn534274search2

A UN Commission report discussed allegations that Lavender generated human-target recommendations and emphasized the need for diligent assessment, validation and approval procedures. citeturn534274search67

The correct MUN use is therefore:
**illustrate the boundary between AI decision-support and autonomous weapons, and the problem of automation bias**, rather than claim undisputed LAWS deployment.

## Defensive systems

Phalanx and Aegis are useful for discussing:
- reaction-time constraints;
- constrained operating environments;
- defensive use;
- risk differentiation.

They should not be presented as proof that all defensive autonomy is safe or that all offensive autonomy is unsafe.

The regulatory question is contextual:
- what target?
- where?
- how long?
- what effects?
- what predictability?
- what human constraints?

## Why these case studies matter

Good case studies should establish:
1. an actual capability;
2. a real governance problem;
3. a drafting lesson.

They should not be used as rhetorical shock examples detached from evidence.
