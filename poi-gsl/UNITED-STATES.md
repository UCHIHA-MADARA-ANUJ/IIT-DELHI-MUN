# United States — Brazil POI Arsenal

## Agenda
**Establishing an International Regulatory Framework for the Development and Deployment of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)**

## Why the U.S. is a target
The U.S. position is NOT that human judgment, IHL, or responsibility are unnecessary. The U.S. says human judgment is required to assess IHL compliance, and that control must have a practical effect on system operation. Its sharper disagreement with Brazil is that human judgment/control should not themselves become standalone legal standards, and that the required level is context-dependent. The U.S. also prefers non-binding guidance over a new legally binding LAWS treaty. That is the pressure point. citeturn0search0turn0news8

# 1. BEST POI — “WHO DEFINES APPROPRIATE?”

> **“The United States calls for ‘appropriate levels of human judgment.’ Who defines what is ‘appropriate’ — an internationally binding standard, or the State deploying the weapon?”**

### Simplified
> **“Who decides what ‘appropriate’ human judgment means — the international community or the country using the weapon?”**

### Why this is strong
The U.S. itself uses the word **“appropriate.”** It also says the level depends on operational context. Brazil's attack is therefore not “America doesn't want human control.” It is: **if the standard is context-dependent but not internationally binding, what stops States from setting their own threshold?** citeturn0search0

---

# 2. DEPLOYMENT THRESHOLD — DIRECTLY ON THE AGENDA

> **“If the United States rejects a binding international human-control standard, what common minimum threshold does it support before a LAWS may be deployed in a new operational environment?”**

### Simplified
> **“If the standard isn't binding, what minimum level must every country meet before deploying a LAWS?”**

### Follow-up
If they say “it depends on context”:

> **“Brazil agrees that context matters. What binding minimum applies regardless of context?”**

---

# 3. THE SELF-DEFINED STANDARD ATTACK

> **“If each State determines what level of human judgment is appropriate, what prevents two States from declaring completely different levels of human involvement sufficient for the same autonomous targeting function?”**

### Simplified
> **“If every country sets its own standard, what stops two countries from calling completely different levels of control ‘enough’?”**

### Why it hurts
It forces the U.S. to defend State discretion **without letting it hide behind the general statement that IHL applies**.

---

# 4. THE “HUMAN CAN INTERVENE” TRAP

> **“If a human can technically intervene but cannot understand or predict the system's behaviour quickly enough to prevent an unlawful effect, does the United States consider that genuine human control?”**

### Simplified
> **“If the human cannot understand or stop the system in time, is that really human control?”**

### Why it hurts
The U.S. itself told the 2026 GGE that control must have a **practical effect** on the system's operation and that necessary technical and operational measures must actually be implemented. Use their own standard against them. citeturn0search0

---

# 5. THE CHANGING-BATTLEFIELD POI

> **“If the operational environment materially changes after authorization, does the United States support mandatory reassessment before the system continues autonomous engagement?”**

### Simplified
> **“If the battlefield changes after approval, must the system be reassessed before it keeps operating?”**

### Follow-up if they say “yes”
> **“Then why should reassessment remain a national policy choice rather than a binding international requirement?”**

### Why it works
This takes the debate away from vague “human-in-the-loop” language and into **deployment conditions**, which is exactly the agenda. Current CCW discussions are dealing with context, operational restrictions, and the practical implementation of human judgment and control. citeturn0search12turn0search0

# 6. THE OPERATIONAL-PARAMETERS POI

> **“If the United States accepts restrictions on target type, scale, time and geographical scope, why should those safeguards remain non-binding rather than becoming minimum international requirements for deployment?”**

### Simplified
> **“If you accept limits on targets, time, place and scale, why shouldn't those limits be internationally binding?”**

The U.S. itself listed operational restrictions on scale, target type, time and space as measures relevant to control. citeturn0search0

# 7. THE “EXISTING IHL IS ENOUGH” POI

> **“If existing IHL is sufficient to regulate autonomous weapons, what LAWS-specific international mechanism does the United States support to ensure those rules are implemented through technical and operational safeguards before deployment?”**

### Simplified
> **“If existing law is enough, what specific international rule makes countries put these safeguards in place before deployment?”**

### If they answer “national legal reviews”
> **“Then Brazil asks: why should compliance depend on the deploying State's own assessment rather than a common international minimum?”**

The current 2026 debate shows the U.S. and Russia arguing that existing IHL provides the framework, while Brazil and other states advocate a legally binding instrument. citeturn0news13turn0news8

---

# 8. THE RESPONSIBILITY POI

> **“The United States correctly maintains that responsibility remains human. But what concrete international obligation ensures that the responsible human actually has sufficient control over an autonomous system to prevent an unlawful effect?”**

### Simplified
> **“If humans remain responsible, what binding rule guarantees they actually have enough control to prevent an unlawful attack?”**

---

# 9. THE VERIFICATION POI

> **“If the United States prefers non-binding guidance, what international verification mechanism does it support for determining whether a State's autonomous weapon actually complies with the safeguards it claims to have implemented?”**

### Simplified
> **“If the rules are voluntary, who checks whether countries actually follow them?”**

This is particularly useful if the U.S. argues that national safeguards are sufficient.

---

# 10. KILLER SHORT POI

> **“Who defines ‘appropriate’ — international law, or the State using the weapon?”**

### Even simpler
> **“Who sets the threshold: the UN or the country deploying the weapon?”**

# FOLLOW-UP TREE

### They say: “IHL already applies.”
> **“Brazil agrees. What binding LAWS-specific obligation ensures IHL is implemented through operational safeguards before deployment?”**

### They say: “The commander remains responsible.”
> **“Then what minimum control must that commander have over target identification and engagement?”**

### They say: “Human judgment is always required.”
> **“At which critical functions — development, authorization, target identification, selection, engagement, or all of them?”**

### They say: “Context determines the level.”
> **“Then what common international minimum prevents context from becoming a lower standard?”**

### They say: “The U.S. has strict safeguards already.”
> **“National safeguards are not an international minimum. Does the United States support making those minimum safeguards binding on all States?”**

### They say: “A treaty would be premature.”
> **“Then what measurable condition must be met before the United States would accept binding international regulation?”**

---

# BRAZIL'S STRATEGIC RULE

**Do NOT say:** “The U.S. does not believe in human control.”

That is inaccurate.

**DO say:**

> The U.S. accepts human judgment/control as part of implementing IHL, but resists making it a standalone international legal standard and prefers flexibility/context-dependence. Brazil's challenge is therefore: **where is the common, enforceable minimum that determines when a LAWS is predictable and controllable enough to deploy?** citeturn0search0

That is much harder for the U.S. delegate to dismiss.

## Current political context
Resolution 80/57 on LAWS was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 1 December 2025 by **164 in favour, 6 against and 7 abstentions**; the United States voted against. Current 2026 reporting describes the U.S. and Russia as opposing a new legally binding instrument while Brazil is among the states pressing for binding regulation. citeturn0search3turn0news8
