# 01 — Accountability & Attribution

Brazil's 2024 CCW submission makes a specific point that goes beyond simply requiring “human control”: the use of an autonomous weapon must not prevent attribution of State or individual legal responsibility for its consequences. Brazil links this directly to whether the system's functions and effects can be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained.

## 1. What Brazil is actually saying

Brazil's position is not that every autonomous weapon is automatically unlawful merely because it is autonomous. Brazil's 2024 submission takes a **context-dependent approach**: the legality of an AWS depends significantly on how it is used and how it functions in the particular operational context.

That assessment remains governed by the core rules of IHL, especially:

- distinction;
- proportionality;
- precautions in attack; and
- the requirement of constant care to minimize harm to civilians.

Brazil states that the **absence of meaningful human control would render a system inherently incompatible with IHL**. Meaningful human control is described as the mechanism through which the human operator retains ultimate decision-making power over the critical functions of the system, particularly targeting decisions and the use of force.

This matters for accountability because the legal question is not simply whether a human was somewhere in the chain of command. The relevant question is whether the human decision-maker retained enough control, understanding and responsibility for the use of force for legal accountability to remain meaningful.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 2. Attribution is a separate problem from “human involvement”

A human being involved in the deployment of a system does not, by itself, solve the accountability problem.

There is a major difference between:

**Human involvement**

and

**meaningful human responsibility for the use and effects of force.**

For example, a person may approve a mission in advance while the system subsequently identifies, selects and engages targets without further human intervention. The existence of the initial authorization does not automatically establish that the human could meaningfully control or anticipate the later use of force.

Brazil's position therefore focuses on whether the relevant human decision-maker has sufficient control over the system's critical functions and sufficient knowledge of the operational context to remain legally accountable for the resulting use of force.

The CCW discussion has similarly connected meaningful human control with the ability of those authorizing force to **explain and predict its effects**, alongside mechanisms for internal oversight and investigation.

Source: CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.4.

## 3. The attribution problem created by autonomous target selection

The most important accountability issue arises when the system itself performs critical functions such as:

**identify → select → engage**

rather than merely executing a decision already made by a human.

The 2025 CCW GGE chair's rolling text characterized LAWS around the capacity, once activated, to identify, select and engage a target without human intervention in the execution of those tasks. It also clarified that a human setting the target profile or parameters does not, by itself, remove the system from that characterization.

This distinction matters because setting a broad target profile is not necessarily the same as making the individual targeting decision.

A human may define the parameters while the system determines which particular object or person falls within those parameters.

That raises a substantive accountability question:

**How much of the legally relevant targeting judgment has actually remained with the human?**

If the answer is “very little,” simply pointing to the existence of a human authorization does not resolve the issue of meaningful human control.

Source: CCW/GGE.1/2025/WP.9.

## 4. Brazil's threshold for compatibility with IHL

Brazil's 2024 submission identifies several circumstances in which an AWS would be incompatible with IHL.

An AWS would be incompatible if it cannot be controlled by humans and therefore cannot be used in compliance with IHL, including where its use:

- cannot be directed at a specific military objective;
- may cause superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering;
- has effects that cannot be sufficiently limited as required by IHL;
- has functions that cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained; or
- may preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility for the consequences of its use.

These are important because Brazil is not treating accountability as an abstract ethical preference. It is connected to the practical legal conditions required for lawful use.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 5. Understanding, predictability and accountability are connected

Brazil's position creates a direct relationship between three things:

**human control → predictability/understanding → legal accountability**

If the responsible human cannot sufficiently understand how the system will operate in the relevant circumstances, it becomes harder to establish that the person meaningfully controlled the use of force.

If the system's relevant effects cannot be sufficiently predicted, it becomes harder to establish that the human decision-maker could make the required legal judgments concerning the use of force.

If the decision-maker could not meaningfully understand, control or anticipate the relevant effects, attributing the consequences of those effects to that person becomes substantially more difficult.

This is why Brazil's 2024 submission places attribution alongside understanding, predictability and control rather than treating them as completely separate issues.

The underlying concern is the preservation of the connection between **human intent and the consequences of the use of force**.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1; CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.4.

## 6. The responsibility chain must remain identifiable

For an autonomous weapon to be deployed consistently with Brazil's approach, the relevant chain of responsibility should remain identifiable.

At minimum, there should be clarity concerning:

### Authorization
Who authorized the system's use?

### Operational context
Who determined that the system was appropriate for the particular environment and mission?

### Critical functions
Who retained decision-making authority over target selection and the application of force?

### Limits
Who established the geographic, temporal, target and other operational constraints?

### Monitoring
Who was responsible for supervising whether the system continued to operate within those constraints?

### Intervention
Who had the authority and practical ability to intervene, modify, suspend or terminate the system's operation when necessary?

### Legal responsibility
Who remains responsible for the consequences of the system's use?

The purpose is not to create a bureaucratic checklist. The purpose is to ensure that autonomous operation does not dissolve the connection between the use of force and the human/State responsibility required by international law.

## 7. Authorization is not the same as continuing responsibility

A particularly important distinction for Brazil is between **initial authorization** and **continuing control**.

A system may be lawful under a particular operational context at the moment of authorization but encounter conditions that differ materially from those originally assessed.

Relevant changes could include:

- a change in the physical environment;
- changes in the civilian presence around the target area;
- changes in the target profile;
- changes in the mission objective;
- changes in the duration of the operation;
- changes in the system's operating parameters; or
- circumstances affecting the ability to apply distinction, proportionality or precautions.

Where such changes affect the assumptions underlying the original legal judgment, the existence of an earlier authorization cannot automatically resolve the new legal question.

Brazil's approach to meaningful human control therefore requires the operator to be able to take necessary precautions during operations and to ensure that the system cannot change mission parameters without human validation.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 8. Mission-parameter changes are particularly important

Brazil's 2024 submission specifically states that the human operator must be able to take necessary precautions during the conduct of operations to ensure that the weapons system is **not able to change mission parameters without human validation**.

This is a significant safeguard because mission parameters determine the conditions within which the autonomous system is expected to operate.

If the system can autonomously alter parameters that materially affect:

- which targets may be engaged;
- where force may be applied;
- when engagement may occur;
- how long the system may operate; or
- other critical conditions of use,

then the original human authorization may no longer correspond to the actual use of force.

Human validation of critical changes therefore protects both IHL compliance and accountability.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 9. Spatial and temporal limits

Brazil explicitly identifies **adequate environmental limits**, including spatial and temporal limits, as part of meaningful human control.

These limits matter for accountability as well as civilian protection.

A human decision-maker can assess a defined operational context more meaningfully when the system's authorized area and duration are known.

By contrast, an authorization that allows an autonomous system to operate across an undefined or expanding area, or for an undefined period, weakens the connection between:

**the circumstances assessed by the human → the circumstances in which force is actually used.**

Spatial and temporal restrictions therefore help preserve the factual basis on which the original human decision was made.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 10. Situational awareness and accountability

Brazil also requires the human operator to approve the operational context through a **sufficient level of situational awareness**.

This is important because legal responsibility requires more than technical authority.

A person cannot meaningfully exercise judgment over the use of force if they lack sufficient information about the circumstances in which that force will be applied.

Situational awareness is therefore part of the chain connecting:

**information → judgment → authorization → use of force → responsibility.**

If the human decision is made without sufficient awareness of the relevant operational conditions, the formal existence of a human decision does not automatically establish meaningful control.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 11. Accountability cannot be transferred to the algorithm

The legal responsibility for the use of force remains with human beings and States. The autonomous system itself cannot become the bearer of legal responsibility.

This creates a basic principle:

**Delegating a function to a machine cannot mean delegating the legal consequences of that function to the machine.**

If an autonomous system is incapable of being sufficiently controlled, understood, predicted or limited, the fact that the system acted autonomously cannot be used as a reason why responsibility becomes unclear.

Instead, the legal framework must prevent deployment in circumstances where autonomous operation would make meaningful accountability impossible.

This is the logic behind Brazil's position that an AWS may be incompatible with IHL if its use may preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 12. State responsibility and individual responsibility

Brazil's wording is significant because it refers to both **State and individual legal responsibility**.

These should not be collapsed into one concept.

### State responsibility
The State remains responsible for conduct attributable to it under international law and for complying with its international obligations, including applicable IHL obligations.

### Individual responsibility
Individuals involved in the authorization, planning, command, operation or other relevant conduct may also have legal responsibilities under applicable international law.

An autonomous system cannot be treated as a third category that sits between the two and absorbs responsibility because its behaviour was generated by an algorithm.

The introduction of autonomy therefore cannot create a legal “responsibility gap” between State responsibility and individual responsibility.

Brazil's 2024 submission expressly identifies the possibility of such an attribution problem as a reason why an AWS could be incompatible with IHL.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 13. Accountability must exist before deployment

The strongest regulatory implication is that accountability should not be treated solely as an **after-the-fact investigation issue**.

A framework that waits until civilian harm occurs to ask who was responsible is incomplete if the system's design or authorized mode of operation already made attribution impossible or seriously uncertain.

The accountability question therefore belongs in the deployment assessment itself:

**Before deployment, can the State identify the responsible decision-makers and establish a meaningful chain of human responsibility for the system's critical functions and foreseeable use?**

If not, the State should not treat post-incident investigation as a substitute for meaningful accountability built into the conditions of use.

This follows from Brazil's position that an AWS may be incompatible with IHL where its use may preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility.

## 14. Why “the commander is responsible” is not enough by itself

A common position is that the commander remains responsible because the commander authorized deployment.

Brazil's substantive response is not to deny command responsibility. It is to ask what the commander actually knew, controlled and authorized.

For accountability to be meaningful, the commander should have sufficient information about:

- the system's capabilities and limitations;
- the intended operational context;
- the target profile;
- the geographic and temporal boundaries;
- the foreseeable circumstances of use;
- the system's reliability and predictability in those circumstances;
- the safeguards and intervention mechanisms available; and
- the conditions requiring suspension, modification or termination.

If the system operates in circumstances materially different from those the commander assessed, the original authorization becomes a weaker basis for attributing the resulting conduct to that decision-maker.

The issue is therefore not whether a commander signed an authorization. It is whether the authorization represented a meaningful human judgment over the actual use of force.

## 15. Why “the operator can press the stop button” is not enough

A nominal override does not necessarily establish meaningful control.

The relevant questions include:

- Does the operator know when intervention is required?
- Does the operator have enough situational awareness to identify an unlawful or dangerous development?
- Is there enough time to intervene before the system produces the relevant effect?
- Does the intervention mechanism actually work under the operational conditions?
- Does the operator understand the system's behaviour sufficiently to know what intervention will accomplish?

A theoretical ability to intervene is therefore different from **effective control in practice**.

This is consistent with the broader CCW discussion of meaningful human control, which emphasizes that those authorizing the use of force should be able to explain and predict its effects and that tailored mechanisms should exist for oversight and investigation.

Source: CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.4.

## 16. Testing and accountability

Testing is relevant not only to technical reliability but also to legal accountability.

If a system has not been adequately tested in the circumstances in which it will be used, the human decision-maker may lack a sufficient basis for understanding and predicting its behaviour.

That matters because Brazil's position requires confidence in the system's reliability and predictability for the identification, selection and engagement of targets.

The relevant question is therefore not simply:

**“Did the system pass a test?”**

It is:

**“Was the system sufficiently tested and understood for the particular operational context in which the human is authorizing its use?”**

Testing must therefore be connected to the conditions and limits of deployment.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 17. Explainability and post-incident investigation

The ability to explain the relevant operation also matters after an incident.

Where a system's functions or effects cannot be sufficiently understood or explained, determining whether the use complied with IHL and determining responsibility may become significantly harder.

A credible regulatory framework therefore needs mechanisms for:

- recording relevant system activity;
- preserving operational data;
- documenting authorization and mission parameters;
- identifying changes to critical parameters;
- investigating incidents; and
- determining responsibility where an unlawful outcome is alleged.

These mechanisms do not replace meaningful human control. They support accountability by ensuring that the legal and factual record of the use of force is not lost inside an autonomous system.

The 2024 CCW discussion on the human element similarly emphasized specific mechanisms for internal oversight and investigations into the development and deployment of AWS.

Source: CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.4.

## 18. Relationship with distinction, proportionality and precautions

Accountability cannot be separated from the substantive IHL rules that govern attacks.

### Distinction
The system must be capable of being used in a manner that distinguishes lawful military objectives from civilians and civilian objects.

### Proportionality
The human decision-maker must be able to make the required assessment concerning expected incidental civilian harm in relation to the anticipated military advantage.

### Precautions in attack
The attacker must take feasible precautions to avoid or minimize civilian harm.

If the autonomous system's operation makes these judgments impossible to make meaningfully, or makes its effects insufficiently controllable or predictable, the accountability problem is also an IHL compliance problem.

Brazil therefore treats meaningful human control as a practical mechanism supporting compliance with these principles, rather than as an isolated technological preference.

Source: Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

## 19. What this means for a future legally binding instrument

A legally binding LAWS instrument should preserve a clear connection between:

**human judgment → authorization → controlled autonomous operation → consequences → legal responsibility.**

Relevant obligations could include requirements for:

- meaningful human control over critical functions;
- defined operational contexts;
- spatial and temporal restrictions;
- sufficient situational awareness before authorization;
- demonstrated reliability and predictability in the intended context;
- human validation of changes to critical mission parameters;
- effective intervention, suspension and termination mechanisms;
- documentation of authorization and operating conditions;
- appropriate oversight and investigation mechanisms; and
- preservation of State and individual legal responsibility.

These requirements would not make the machine legally responsible. They would ensure that the human and State responsibility that already exists under international law remains practically identifiable when autonomous functions are introduced.

## 20. 2025 CCW development

The 2025 CCW GGE chair's rolling text stated that **context-appropriate human judgment and control is needed to ensure the use and effects of LAWS comply with international law, particularly IHL, including distinction, proportionality and precautions in attack**.

The 2025 discussion also showed that the phrase “context-appropriate human judgment and control” was an area of significant discussion. Some delegations supported it, while others raised concerns that the concepts of judgment and control could be interpreted differently or conflated, and several delegations called for the meaning to be clearly defined.

For Brazil, this supports the need to move from a general reference to human control toward **concrete, legally meaningful requirements** that can be assessed against actual operational circumstances.

Source: CCW/GGE.1/2025/WP.9.

## 21. The central substantive distinction

There are three different questions that should not be confused:

### 1. Was a human involved?
This is the weakest question.

### 2. Did a human authorize deployment?
This is stronger, but still does not necessarily establish meaningful control over autonomous target selection and engagement.

### 3. Did a human retain meaningful judgment and control over the critical use of force, with responsibility remaining attributable?
This is the substantive standard relevant to Brazil's position.

The third question is the one that matters for a genuine accountability framework.

## 22. Bottom line

Brazil's accountability position can be reduced to a legal chain:

**If humans cannot meaningfully control the critical use of force → lawful use becomes impossible or seriously compromised.**

**If the relevant effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained → meaningful human judgment is undermined.**

**If responsibility cannot be attributed to the State or relevant individuals → the autonomous system has created an accountability problem that cannot be solved by simply pointing to the machine.**

Brazil therefore treats accountability and attribution as part of the conditions governing lawful deployment of LAWS, not as an issue that can be postponed until after an incident.
