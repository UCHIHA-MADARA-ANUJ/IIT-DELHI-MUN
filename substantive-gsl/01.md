# 01 — Accountability & Attribution

Brazil's 2024 CCW submission makes a specific point that goes beyond simply requiring “human control”: the use of an autonomous weapon must not prevent attribution of State or individual legal responsibility for its consequences. Brazil links this directly to whether the system's functions and effects can be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained.

## 1. Brazil's position — the exact legal problem

Brazil's position is not that every autonomous weapon is automatically unlawful merely because it contains autonomous functions. Brazil's 2024 submission takes a contextual approach: legality depends significantly on the system's functioning and its mode of use in the particular circumstances of employment.

Brazil nevertheless sets a hard principle: **absence of meaningful human control is incompatible with IHL.** For Brazil, meaningful human control is the mechanism by which a human operator retains ultimate decision-making power over critical functions, especially decisions concerning targeting and the application of force.

Brazil identifies several situations in which an AWS would be incompatible with IHL, including where the system cannot be directed at a specific military objective, where its effects cannot be sufficiently limited, where its functions cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained, or where its use may preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility.

This makes accountability a substantive legal requirement, not merely an ethical aspiration.

**Primary source:** Brazil, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1.

---

## 2. Why “a human was involved” is legally insufficient

There are three very different propositions:

1. A human was somewhere in the chain.
2. A human authorized the system before activation.
3. A human retained meaningful judgment and control over the critical use of force, with responsibility remaining attributable.

Brazil's position is concerned with the third.

A person pressing “activate” does not necessarily mean that person selected the target, understood the circumstances of engagement, controlled the system's critical functions, or could intervene effectively when the situation changed.

The accountability problem becomes sharper where the system independently performs the functions of identifying, selecting and engaging a target. The human may have established a target profile or mission parameters, but the system can then determine which particular object or person falls within those parameters.

The legal issue is therefore not simply **whether a human touched the system**. It is whether the relevant human decision-maker retained sufficient judgment and control over the use of force to discharge the applicable legal obligations.

The ICRC reaches a similar distinction: IHL obligations concerning attacks are obligations of humans who plan, decide upon and carry out attacks; those obligations and accountability for them cannot be transferred to a machine or weapon system. citeturn0search2

---

## 3. The critical functions: identify → select → engage

The current CCW work has increasingly focused on the functions most directly connected with lethal force.

The 2025 GGE work specifically examined the meaning of critical functions, including **identification, selection and engagement**. The Chair's background paper explains that these functions are central because they sit directly within the chain leading to the application of force. citeturn1search10

The December 2025 rolling text characterized a LAWS, for the purposes of the GGE's work, as a functionally integrated combination capable of identifying, selecting and engaging a target without human intervention in the execution of those tasks. This was still part of a text under negotiation, not a universally binding legal definition. citeturn2search10

For Brazil, the importance of these functions is straightforward:

**If the system itself makes the targeting decision and applies lethal force, the human role cannot be reduced to a distant authorization.**

The closer autonomy comes to the actual decision over whom or what will be attacked, the more important meaningful human judgment and control become.

Brazil's 2024 submission specifically identifies target selection and target engagement as the functions around which human control should be maintained. citeturn2search9

---

## 4. Accountability is connected to IHL compliance

Brazil does not treat accountability as an isolated question.

The chain is:

**human control → ability to apply IHL → lawful use of force → attributable responsibility**

The relevant IHL principles include:

### Distinction
The system must be capable of being used in a manner consistent with the obligation to distinguish between civilians/civilian objects and military objectives.

### Proportionality
The relevant human decision-makers must be able to make the legally required assessment concerning expected incidental civilian harm in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage.

### Precautions in attack
Feasible precautions must be taken to avoid or minimize civilian harm.

Brazil's 2024 submission expressly places distinction, proportionality and precaution at the centre of the assessment of AWS and emphasizes the requirement of constant care to minimize civilian harm. citeturn0search10

Therefore, if a system's autonomous operation makes the relevant judgments impossible to exercise meaningfully, the problem is simultaneously an **IHL compliance problem and an accountability problem**.

---

## 5. “Predictability” is not the same as accuracy

This is important because the agenda is about predictability, but Brazil's accountability argument gives predictability a more precise legal function.

Brazil says the human operator must have confidence in the system's **reliability and predictability in the identification, selection and engagement of targets**. citeturn0search10

A single percentage cannot answer all of those questions.

For example, “95% accuracy” does not establish:

- what the system was tested against;
- what operational environment was used;
- what target categories were involved;
- whether civilians were present;
- whether the system's behaviour changes in a different environment;
- whether the system's effects remain within the authorized parameters;
- whether a human can recognize when the system is failing; or
- whether the resulting conduct can be reconstructed and explained.

The ICRC similarly identifies predictability and reliability as closely connected to human control and notes that increasing mobility, adaptability and interaction among systems can increase unpredictability. citeturn0search1

For Brazil, predictability matters because **a person cannot meaningfully take legal responsibility for a use of force that they could not reasonably understand or anticipate in the relevant circumstances.**

---

## 6. Understandability and explainability

Brazil's wording is unusually useful here: the system's functions and effects must be sufficiently **understood, predicted and explained**.

That has two dimensions.

### Before use
The responsible human needs sufficient understanding of what the system can do, where it can operate, what it can identify, how it may behave in the intended environment, and what its limitations are.

### After use
If an incident occurs, the State and relevant authorities need enough information to reconstruct what happened and determine whether the operation complied with international law.

This means explainability is not merely a technical preference. It supports both **ex ante legal judgment** and **ex post accountability**.

The ICRC's work similarly identifies difficulties where machine-learning systems make it difficult to understand, predict or explain how an autonomous weapon reaches a particular output. citeturn1search1

---

## 7. The “black box” issue

A system can produce a technically functioning output without giving the human operator a legally adequate basis for understanding that output.

That distinction matters.

The question is not whether every algorithm must be mathematically transparent to every soldier. The relevant question is whether the humans responsible for authorizing and supervising the use of force have sufficient knowledge of the system's behaviour, limitations and expected effects to make the required legal judgments.

If a system is so opaque or adaptive that the responsible human cannot reasonably determine why it is behaving as it is, or cannot reliably anticipate its behaviour in the intended context, meaningful control becomes harder to establish.

Brazil's 2024 submission specifically treats insufficient understanding, predictability and explanation as potential grounds for incompatibility with IHL. citeturn0search10

---

## 8. Machine learning and adaptive behaviour

Brazil's 2024 material recognizes that autonomy exists on a spectrum and that some systems may have machine-learning capabilities allowing them to adapt their actions based on new data acquired during operations.

That raises a specific accountability concern: **if behaviour can change during the operation, what exactly did the human authorize?**

A human can authorize a defined function under defined conditions. If the system subsequently adapts in ways that materially alter its behaviour, the factual basis of the original authorization may no longer be identical to the system's later operation.

Brazil therefore emphasizes that the operator must be able to ensure that a weapons system cannot change mission parameters without human validation. citeturn2search9

This is particularly important for systems with adaptive or machine-learning components, because the relevant issue is not only whether the system worked during initial testing but whether its behaviour remains within the authorized and legally assessed parameters during actual use.

---

## 9. Initial authorization vs continuing responsibility

A major accountability distinction is between **authorization at time zero** and **responsibility throughout the operation**.

Suppose a human approves a system for:

- a defined mission;
- a defined geographic area;
- a defined duration;
- a defined target profile; and
- a defined operational environment.

If those circumstances materially change, the original authorization cannot automatically answer the new legal question.

Possible changes include:

- civilian presence increasing or changing;
- the physical environment changing;
- the target profile changing;
- the mission objective changing;
- the system operating for longer than initially assessed;
- the system moving outside the tested geographic area;
- critical parameters changing; or
- the system behaving differently from what was reasonably expected.

Brazil's approach therefore treats operational limits and continuing human control as essential safeguards rather than assuming that a single pre-deployment authorization is enough. citeturn0search10

---

## 10. Spatial and temporal limits

Brazil specifically identifies adequate environmental limits, including **spatial and temporal limits**, as part of meaningful human control. citeturn0search10

These restrictions are important for accountability because they preserve the relationship between the circumstances that were assessed by a human and the circumstances in which the system actually applies force.

A tightly defined authorization might say, in substance:

**this system → this mission → this target category → this area → this time period → these conditions.**

The more open-ended the authorization becomes, the harder it is to establish that the original human judgment remained applicable to every subsequent autonomous engagement.

The ICRC has similarly identified restrictions concerning task, target, operational environment, geographical space and time as practical elements of human control. citeturn0search1

---

## 11. Situational awareness is part of accountability

Brazil requires the human operator to approve the operational context with a **sufficient level of situational awareness**. citeturn0search10

That matters because a person cannot meaningfully exercise legal judgment over an operation if the person lacks sufficient information about the circumstances in which force will be applied.

The relevant chain is:

**information → human judgment → authorization → operation → consequence → responsibility**

If the information available to the human is materially insufficient, the formal existence of human authorization does not automatically establish meaningful control.

Situational awareness therefore belongs inside the accountability framework, not outside it.

---

## 12. Intervention must be practically meaningful

A nominal “override” does not necessarily solve the accountability problem.

The relevant questions are:

- Does the operator know when intervention is required?
- Does the operator have enough information to recognize a problem?
- Is there sufficient time to intervene before the relevant effect occurs?
- Does the intervention mechanism actually work under the operational conditions?
- Can the operator understand what will happen after intervention?
- Can the system continue to apply force despite an attempted intervention?

The ICRC identifies human supervision, ability to intervene and timely deactivation as relevant elements of human control. citeturn1search1

Thus:

**theoretical ability to intervene ≠ effective control.**

---

## 13. Mission-parameter changes

One of the most concrete Brazilian safeguards is that the human operator must be able to take necessary precautions during operations and ensure that the system **cannot change mission parameters without human validation**. citeturn0search10

This is important because mission parameters can determine:

- which targets are eligible;
- where force may be applied;
- how long the system may operate;
- what circumstances trigger engagement; and
- what other constraints apply.

If a system can autonomously make a material change to such parameters, the system may move beyond the conditions that the human actually assessed and authorized.

Human validation of critical changes therefore preserves both the legal basis of the operation and the responsibility chain.

---

## 14. “The commander is responsible” — what that actually requires

A State may argue that there is no accountability gap because the commander remains responsible for the deployment.

Brazil's position does not require denying command responsibility. It requires examining what the commander actually knew, authorized and controlled.

A meaningful authorization should rest on adequate knowledge of:

- system capabilities and limitations;
- intended operational context;
- target categories;
- geographical and temporal boundaries;
- expected system behaviour;
- reliability and predictability in the relevant circumstances;
- intervention and deactivation mechanisms; and
- circumstances requiring suspension or termination.

The important point is that **responsibility cannot be preserved merely by attaching a human name to the authorization document**.

The human must have had a meaningful basis for the judgment that led to the use of force.

---

## 15. State responsibility and individual responsibility are distinct

Brazil deliberately refers to **State and individual legal responsibility**.

These concepts should not be merged.

### State responsibility
The State remains bound by its international obligations and may bear responsibility for internationally wrongful conduct attributable to it.

### Individual responsibility
Individuals can bear responsibility under applicable rules of international law and domestic law for conduct for which they are legally responsible.

The autonomous weapon itself cannot become a third category that absorbs responsibility simply because an algorithm generated an output.

The introduction of autonomy therefore cannot create a legal zone in which:

**State responsibility → machine → no individual responsibility**

becomes a substitute for ordinary legal attribution.

Brazil's 2024 position specifically identifies possible prevention of attribution of State and individual responsibility as an IHL incompatibility. citeturn0search10

---

## 16. Do not overstate individual liability

This is an important legal nuance for Brazil.

Saying **“the commander is automatically guilty because the machine killed a civilian”** would be legally unsound.

Brazil's actual position is more precise: autonomy must not prevent attribution of legal responsibility where responsibility exists.

Whether a particular individual is legally responsible depends on the applicable law and the facts, including what that person knew, intended, authorized, controlled, or failed to do where a relevant legal duty existed.

The point is therefore not automatic punishment of commanders, programmers, operators or manufacturers.

The point is that **autonomy must never itself become a shield against established principles of legal responsibility.**

This distinction also appeared in 2026 CCW discussion, where Bangladesh stressed that commanders, programmers, operators and manufacturers do not incur automatic liability merely because they are in the chain, while insisting that autonomy cannot become a legal shield. citeturn2search3

---

## 17. Why responsibility cannot be transferred to the machine

IHL rules governing attacks are addressed to humans who plan, decide upon and carry out attacks.

The ICRC has stated that the legal obligations and accountability attached to those rules cannot be transferred to a machine, computer program or weapon system. citeturn0search2

Therefore, if a system independently selects and engages a target, the legal responsibility does not migrate into the algorithm.

The human and State actors remain the relevant legal subjects.

This creates a practical requirement for regulation:

**the more autonomous the system becomes in the application of force, the more important it becomes that the conditions of use preserve a clear and legally meaningful chain of human responsibility.**

---

## 18. Accountability must exist before deployment, not only after an incident

A weak framework would wait until an unlawful outcome occurs and then ask who was responsible.

Brazil's position points to a stronger approach: accountability must be built into the **conditions of lawful use**.

Before deployment, the State should be able to establish:

- who authorized the system;
- what exactly was authorized;
- in what operational context;
- under what spatial and temporal limits;
- what target categories were contemplated;
- what system behaviour was expected;
- what safeguards were required;
- who could intervene;
- what changes required human validation; and
- how responsibility would remain traceable.

If the system is deployed in a way that makes those questions impossible to answer, the problem existed before the incident occurred.

This is why Brazil's accountability position is fundamentally **preventive as well as remedial**.

---

## 19. Accountability requires traceability

The 2025 rolling-text discussions included the idea that States should ensure accountability across the life cycle of LAWS through a **traceable and responsible chain of human command and control**. The 2025 Chair's summary records proposed elements stating that humans must remain responsible and accountable for decisions regarding LAWS and that accountability cannot be transferred to machines. citeturn1search12

Traceability matters because a post-incident investigation should be able to reconstruct the relevant chain:

**system version → testing/validation → legal review → authorization → mission parameters → operational use → interventions/changes → outcome → investigation.**

A traceable record does not itself guarantee legality. It makes meaningful oversight and accountability possible.

---

## 20. Testing and evaluation are part of the accountability chain

The 2025 GGE discussions included proposed obligations for States to conduct **rigorous testing and evaluation in realistic simulated operational environments** so that a human user can have a reliable expectation of how the weapon will perform in the anticipated circumstances of use. citeturn1search12

That matters directly to Brazil's position.

If the system has not been sufficiently tested in circumstances resembling its intended use, the human authorizing deployment may not have a sufficient factual basis for predicting its behaviour.

Testing therefore supports:

**knowledge → predictability → judgment → authorization → accountability.**

The test should not simply be whether the system technically works. It should assess whether it behaves reliably enough **in the circumstances in which it is actually intended to be used**.

---

## 21. Bias and automation bias

The 2025 rolling-text discussions also considered measures concerning harmful or unintended bias in data and AI capabilities, as well as **automation bias**. citeturn1search12

This is relevant to accountability because a human can technically remain “in the loop” while relying excessively on an automated output.

If the system's recommendation is treated as presumptively correct, the human may retain formal authority while exercising little substantive independent judgment.

That is another reason why human control cannot be defined simply as:

**“a human had the ability to approve the system's output.”**

The quality of the human judgment matters.

Training, guidance, system understanding and operational procedures can therefore be relevant to whether human control is genuine rather than nominal.

The 2025 GGE work specifically considered appropriate guidance, training and instructions for human users. citeturn1search12

---

## 22. Legal review — important but not sufficient

States have existing obligations concerning the legal review of new weapons under applicable international law. Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires States Parties to determine whether the employment of a new weapon, means or method of warfare would, in some or all circumstances, be prohibited by applicable international law.

A legal review is therefore an important part of the framework.

But a review performed before deployment cannot substitute for operational controls where legality depends on the circumstances of use.

Brazil's 2024 submission explicitly takes the position that legality depends significantly on the mode of use and functioning of the system within a specific context. citeturn0search10

So the framework needs both:

**pre-deployment legal review**

and

**operational controls that preserve the conditions under which the legal assessment was made.**

The ICRC has also noted practical challenges in legal reviews of autonomous weapons, including assessing whether a system will perform as anticipated in the intended or expected circumstances of use. citeturn0search1

---

## 23. Lifecycle accountability

Accountability should not begin only when the weapon reaches the battlefield.

The 2025 CCW rolling-text work treats accountability as relevant across the **entire life cycle** of LAWS. Proposed elements include human responsibility for decisions concerning LAWS and a traceable, responsible chain of human command and control. citeturn1search12

A serious framework therefore needs accountability at multiple stages:

### Development
Understand system capabilities, limitations and intended functions.

### Testing
Test in realistic environments and identify failure modes.

### Legal review
Assess compatibility with applicable international law.

### Procurement / acquisition
Ensure the procured system corresponds to the legally assessed configuration and intended use.

### Training
Ensure users understand the system, its limitations and the conditions of authorized employment.

### Deployment
Apply defined operational limits and human-control requirements.

### Operation
Maintain oversight, precautions and intervention capability.

### Modification / updating
Reassess where material changes affect relevant capabilities or assumptions.

### Incident investigation
Preserve records and determine what happened and who was responsible.

### Decommissioning
Ensure accountability and traceability are not lost when the system is withdrawn.

The important point is that accountability can be weakened by decisions made **before** the weapon is ever used.

---

## 24. The 2025 rolling text — why it matters to Brazil

The 2025 Meeting of High Contracting Parties described the latest rolling text as following a widely supported **two-tier approach**, with prohibitions on some types of LAWS and restrictions on the design and use of others. It also highlighted human responsibility and accountability and the operationalization of context-appropriate human judgment and control. citeturn1search11

Brazil, together with Ireland, Norway and Switzerland, supported a joint statement backing the rolling text and the need to move towards negotiations within the CCW; the statement received support from more than 40 High Contracting Parties and one observer State. citeturn1search11

This is important for Brazil because the accountability argument is not separate from the broader treaty architecture. It sits inside the emerging framework:

**prohibition where lawful human control/accountability cannot be maintained + regulation where controlled use remains possible.**

---

## 25. 2026 — the issue has moved closer to a decision point

The CCW GGE entered its final year under its current mandate in 2026. The first 2026 session was held from 2–6 March, and the second from 31 August–4 September. The Chair's 2026 summary notes that the Group continued substantive work on the rolling text and that the mandate is to formulate elements of an instrument without prejudging its nature, while narrowing remaining divergences. citeturn2search0turn2search7

The 2026 process is therefore important because accountability is no longer being discussed only as a theoretical question. It is part of the text being negotiated as a possible element of an international instrument.

The UN Secretary-General has continued to call for legally binding international rules, including human control, human accountability and safeguards across the military AI lifecycle. In September 2025 he called for a legally binding instrument and stressed that human accountability and strong safeguards should govern military AI. citeturn1search4

In August 2026, the Secretary-General and ICRC President renewed their call for legally binding rules, warning that autonomous targeting of humans by machines is approaching a fundamental humanitarian boundary. citeturn1search3

---

## 26. ICRC's current legal position — useful independent support

The ICRC's 2026 position paper is useful because it distinguishes two propositions:

1. **Existing IHL applies to autonomous weapons.**
2. **Existing IHL does not answer every specific legal, humanitarian and ethical question raised by increasing autonomy.**

The ICRC therefore supports new legally binding rules that clarify and specify how IHL applies to AWS and establish additional prohibitions and restrictions. citeturn1search2turn1search6

This supports Brazil's argument that saying “IHL already applies” does not by itself settle what operational standards should govern autonomous weapons.

The relevant debate is not whether IHL disappears because a weapon is autonomous. It is how States ensure that autonomy does not undermine the ability of humans to comply with IHL and remain accountable for the use of force.

---

## 27. A useful distinction: accountability gap vs accountability uncertainty

Do not overstate the argument by saying every autonomous weapon automatically creates an “accountability gap.”

A more precise distinction is:

### Accountability preserved
The responsible humans understand the system, control its critical functions, operate within defined limits, can intervene, and responsibility remains traceable.

### Accountability weakened
The system behaves in ways that are difficult to understand or predict, human supervision is weak, operational conditions are poorly bounded, or the chain of responsibility becomes unclear.

### Accountability potentially precluded
The autonomous operation may prevent attribution of State or individual legal responsibility for the consequences of use.

Brazil's 2024 language is strongest in the third category: **if use may preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility, that is itself a reason the AWS would be incompatible with IHL.** citeturn0search10

This distinction keeps the argument legally precise.

---

## 28. Accountability is not automatic liability

Another important legal distinction:

**Maintaining accountability does not mean making every person in the development chain automatically liable for every outcome.**

The relevant legal question remains whether responsibility is attributable under the applicable legal framework and facts.

A programmer does not automatically become legally responsible because software contains an error.

A manufacturer does not automatically become responsible because a military system malfunctions.

A commander does not automatically become criminally responsible merely because an autonomous system causes civilian harm.

But none of these qualifications justify allowing autonomy to make responsibility impossible to determine.

The correct principle is:

**autonomy must not create immunity; established rules of responsibility must remain applicable.**

---

## 29. Why this matters for investigations

If a civilian object is struck by an autonomous system, a meaningful investigation should be able to examine at least:

- what the system was designed and configured to do;
- what version/configuration was deployed;
- what target parameters were active;
- what geographic and temporal limits applied;
- what information the system received;
- what it identified and selected;
- what humans authorized;
- whether mission parameters changed;
- whether human intervention was possible and attempted;
- whether the system operated within its tested conditions;
- what safeguards were active; and
- which human/State decisions are legally relevant to responsibility.

Without adequate records, even a system that was intended to be accountable may become difficult to investigate after an incident.

This is why traceability, documentation, oversight and investigation belong inside the broader accountability framework.

---

## 30. What Brazil's framework rejects

Brazil's substantive position is difficult to reconcile with the following approaches:

### “A human approved it once, so everything afterward is covered.”
Not sufficient where the system's operation exceeds the circumstances actually assessed.

### “The system is 95% accurate, so it is predictable enough.”
A numerical accuracy rate does not by itself establish IHL compliance or meaningful human control.

### “There is an override button, therefore there is human control.”
A theoretical override is not the same as effective supervision and timely intervention.

### “Existing IHL is enough; no additional standards are needed.”
Brazil supports the application of IHL but also argues for positive regulatory obligations giving meaningful human control concrete content. citeturn0search10

### “The algorithm made the decision.”
The machine cannot inherit the legal responsibility that applies to human and State actors.

### “The commander is automatically responsible.”
Responsibility must still be determined under the applicable legal rules and facts; autonomy cannot create automatic liability either.

---

## 31. What a serious regulatory framework would need

For Brazil, accountability should be operationalized through requirements such as:

### A. Clear human authority
Humans retain ultimate decision-making power over critical functions involving target selection and application of potentially lethal force.

### B. Defined operational context
The system is authorized for a specific and sufficiently understood context rather than unrestricted autonomous operation.

### C. Spatial and temporal limits
Geographic area and duration are constrained so that the original human judgment remains relevant to actual use.

### D. Target constraints
The system's permissible target categories and functions are clearly bounded.

### E. Reliability and predictability
Testing provides a reliable basis for expectations concerning system behaviour in the intended circumstances.

### F. Situational awareness
The human authorizing deployment has sufficient information about the operational context.

### G. Human intervention
Intervention and deactivation mechanisms are practically effective and timely.

### H. Mission-parameter validation
Material changes to critical mission parameters require human validation.

### I. Traceability
A responsible chain of human command and control can be identified throughout the lifecycle.

### J. Investigation
Relevant system and operational information can be preserved and reviewed when incidents occur.

### K. Responsibility
State and individual legal responsibility remains attributable according to applicable law.

These are not eleven unrelated safeguards. Together they preserve the chain between **human judgment and the use of force**.

---

## 32. The deeper principle: autonomy cannot become a legal intermediary

A useful way to understand Brazil's position is that autonomy cannot become a new legal intermediary standing between a human decision and the consequence of force.

The chain cannot become:

**human decision → algorithm → consequence → responsibility unclear**

It needs to remain:

**human decision → controlled autonomous operation → consequence → legally attributable responsibility**

The machine can perform functions. It cannot become the legal subject that absorbs the consequences of those functions.

That is the central accountability principle running through Brazil's position from the 2019 human-element intervention, through the 2024 detailed response, and into the more operational 2025–2026 CCW work.

Brazil stated in 2019 that the human element is what provides a subject for accountability and argued that critical decisions, particularly target designation and use of force, must remain human decisions. citeturn1search13

---

## 33. Brazil's evolution: 2019 → 2024 → 2025 → 2026

### 2019
Brazil emphasized the human element, effective situational awareness, understanding of the system, capacity to interrupt autonomous processes, and human decision-making for critical decisions such as target designation and use of force. Brazil linked this to accountability and supported a legally binding instrument. citeturn1search13

### 2024
Brazil developed the position in much greater detail: absence of meaningful human control is incompatible with IHL; the system's functions and effects must be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained; and use must not preclude attribution of State or individual legal responsibility. Brazil also identified operational requirements concerning spatial/temporal limits, situational awareness, reliability/predictability and human validation of mission-parameter changes. citeturn0search10

### 2025
The GGE work increasingly operationalized **context-appropriate human judgment and control**, critical functions, lifecycle obligations, testing, bias mitigation and human responsibility/accountability. Brazil supported the rolling text as a basis for negotiations. citeturn1search10turn1search11

### 2026
The GGE entered its final year under the current mandate and continued work on the rolling text, while the international debate moved toward the question of whether and how negotiations for a binding instrument should proceed. citeturn2search0turn2search7

So Brazil's current position is not a sudden policy invention. The core idea has remained consistent while becoming progressively more concrete.

## 34. Important current-status caution

The CCW **rolling text is not yet a binding treaty**.

Likewise, formulations described as “provisionally found consensus” within the GGE are not equivalent to universally binding law.

This distinction is important in conference debate.

Brazil can accurately say that these formulations represent substantial progress in the negotiations and that Brazil supports using the rolling text as a basis for negotiations. It should not describe every element of the rolling text as an already-existing legal obligation.

The 2025 Meeting of High Contracting Parties itself described the rolling text as a basis for negotiations and noted that the two-tier approach and accountability elements were gaining broad support. citeturn1search11

## 35. UN General Assembly context

Resolution A/RES/80/57 was adopted by the General Assembly on **1 December 2025 by 164 votes in favour, 6 against and 7 abstentions**. Brazil voted in favour. citeturn0search0turn0search9

The resolution stresses the importance of the role of humans in the use of force to ensure responsibility and accountability and for States to comply with international law. It also calls on CCW High Contracting Parties to continue work toward completing elements for an instrument with a view to future negotiations. It notes the Secretary-General's call for a legally binding instrument using a two-tier approach of prohibitions and regulations. citeturn0search11

This provides a broader multilateral basis for Brazil's position, while the resolution itself should not be misrepresented as creating a treaty obligation.

## 36. The most precise Brazilian formulation

The strongest substantive formulation of Brazil's position is not simply:

**“Humans must control AI.”**

It is:

**A lawful use of autonomous force requires a sufficiently meaningful human chain of judgment, control and responsibility connecting the authorization of force to its actual effects.**

That requires the human to have sufficient understanding of the system and operational context, sufficient ability to constrain and supervise its critical functions, sufficient predictability regarding its relevant effects, and sufficient authority to intervene when circumstances require it.

If autonomy prevents attribution of State or individual legal responsibility, Brazil's own 2024 position identifies that as a reason the use may be incompatible with IHL. citeturn0search10

## 37. Final substantive synthesis

The accountability issue can be reduced to one chain:

**1. HUMAN JUDGMENT**
A human assesses the operational context and makes the relevant legal judgment.

↓

**2. HUMAN CONTROL**
The human retains meaningful control over the critical functions of the system.

↓

**3. PREDICTABILITY**
The human has a reliable basis for expecting how the system will perform in the intended circumstances.

↓

**4. OPERATIONAL LIMITS**
The system is bounded by target, spatial, temporal and other relevant constraints.

↓

**5. CONTINUING OVERSIGHT**
The human can supervise, intervene, suspend or deactivate where necessary.

↓

**6. TRACEABILITY**
The authorization, parameters, operation and relevant changes can be reconstructed.

↓

**7. LEGAL ACCOUNTABILITY**
Responsibility remains attributable to the relevant State and individuals under applicable law.

Break the chain at any point and the legal problem becomes more serious.

For Brazil, the machine cannot be allowed to become the point at which the chain disappears.
