# SUBSTANTIVE II — MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL: FROM AUTHORIZATION TO OPERATIONAL REALITY

> **Brazil — Committee Research Dossier**
>
> This substantive develops a distinct line of analysis from Substantive I. The central question here is not merely who bears responsibility after an autonomous engagement, but what a human must actually understand, anticipate, constrain, supervise, and reassess for control over the use of force to remain meaningful.

---

## 01 — Defining Meaningful Human Control

Meaningful human control cannot be reduced to the existence of a human somewhere in the weapons chain. The relevant question is whether human judgment remains substantively connected to the critical functions through which force is ultimately applied. Brazil's 2024 CCW submission links meaningful human control to ultimate human decision-making over critical functions, particularly targeting and the use of force. This is important because activation and control are not synonymous: a person can authorize a system without personally determining the particular target, timing, or circumstances of an engagement.

The concept is therefore functional rather than ceremonial. It concerns the quality of information available to the human, the ability to understand the system's relevant limitations, the predictability of its behaviour within the authorized environment, and the practical ability to constrain or interrupt employment. The appropriate level of control can differ according to the weapon, mission, target, environment, duration, and scale of operation.

Brazil's position should consequently be framed as a continuing condition of lawful employment. Human involvement must remain meaningful at the point where autonomous functions materially affect the use of force, rather than existing only at procurement or authorization.

```text
HUMAN INVOLVEMENT
       ↓
UNDERSTAND → ANTICIPATE → CONSTRAIN → INTERVENE → REASSESS
       ↓
MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL
```

## 02 — Human Agency and the Decision to Employ Force

International humanitarian law imposes obligations on human decision-makers and States; it does not create a legal vacuum merely because a weapon contains autonomous functions. The ICRC has repeatedly emphasized that the use of autonomous weapons does not transfer legal obligations to the machine. The central issue is therefore whether the human decision to employ the system remains sufficiently connected to the actual circumstances in which force is applied.

That connection becomes weaker when a human approves a generalized mission but the system subsequently determines individual targets and the timing or circumstances of engagement. The distance between authorization and execution then becomes legally and operationally significant. This does not mean every autonomous function is automatically prohibited. It means that the more consequential the delegated function, the more carefully the State must establish how meaningful human judgment is preserved.

Brazil should distinguish between using autonomy to assist human judgment and allowing autonomy to replace judgment over critical decisions. A system that presents information to a human who independently decides whether to attack is materially different from one that independently identifies, selects, and engages a target.

## 03 — Authorization Is Not Control

Authorization answers a limited question: did a competent authority permit the system or mission to be employed? Meaningful control asks a broader question: can a human still govern the relevant use of force while the system is operating? Treating authorization as equivalent to control risks converting a single decision at the beginning of an operation into a permanent legal permission for subsequent autonomous decisions.

Consider a system authorized for a defined target set within a defined geographical area. If the environment changes, civilian presence increases, the target category becomes ambiguous, or the system encounters conditions outside its validation envelope, the original authorization does not itself resolve whether continued autonomous employment remains appropriate. Control must therefore persist through the operation.

Brazil can use this distinction to press delegations that rely heavily on pre-deployment authorization. The decisive issue is not whether a human once approved the mission, but whether that human decision remains sufficiently connected to the circumstances in which lethal force is ultimately applied.

```text
AUTHORIZATION → INITIAL CONDITIONS → AUTONOMOUS OPERATION
                                      ↓
                             MATERIAL CHANGE?
                              ↙          ↘
                            NO           YES
                            ↓             ↓
                         CONTINUE     REASSESS
```

## 04 — The Human–Machine Decision Nexus

The human-machine decision nexus describes the connection between human judgment, machine output, operational circumstances, and the eventual application of force. Speed, computational power, and technical sophistication do not themselves establish meaningful control. A machine can process information faster than a human while still operating outside the conditions the human reasonably understood.

The nexus becomes stronger when the human knows what the system can and cannot do, defines the relevant mission parameters, receives meaningful information during operation, and retains an effective ability to intervene. It becomes weaker when the system independently determines critical targeting decisions that the human cannot adequately inspect or challenge.

This framework also prevents an artificial binary between “manual” and “autonomous.” Different functions can be distributed between human and machine in different ways. Identification, tracking, classification, target selection and engagement should therefore be examined separately. Brazil's inquiry is fundamentally functional: **which decision has been delegated, under what circumstances, and what meaningful human judgment remains over the resulting use of force?**

## 05 — Critical Functions of LAWS

The phrase “critical functions” should not become a vague technical expression. In autonomous weapons discussions, functions such as detection, identification, tracking, selection and engagement can have different legal significance. A system that automatically tracks an already human-selected target presents a different control question from one that independently identifies and selects targets from a broader population.

Brazil's 2024 CCW submission places particular emphasis on ultimate human decision-making over critical functions, especially targeting decisions. The useful approach is therefore to identify exactly what the human decides and what the machine decides. The closer an autonomous function is to the final selection and application of lethal force, the more consequential the question of meaningful human control becomes.

A rigorous assessment should also consider combinations of functions. A human may technically select a target category while the system determines which individual objects fall inside that category. The legal analysis cannot stop at the label “human-selected.” It must examine the actual decision architecture and the information available to the human at the time.

```text
DETECT → IDENTIFY → TRACK → SELECT → ENGAGE
   │        │         │        │         │
   └──────────── HUMAN / MACHINE ALLOCATION ────────────┘
                     ↓
          WHERE DOES THE CRITICAL
             JUDGMENT ACTUALLY OCCUR?
```

## 06 — Human-in-the-Loop

“Human-in-the-loop” describes a structure in which a human is expected to approve or participate in an action before it occurs. The label is useful, but it does not by itself establish meaningful control. A human may be technically positioned in the decision loop while receiving inadequate information, having insufficient time to assess the situation, or being heavily dependent on an opaque machine recommendation.

The relevant test is substantive. Does the human possess the information, competence, time and authority necessary to exercise independent judgment? If a system presents a recommendation seconds before an engagement and the human can only approve or reject without understanding the basis of the recommendation, formal human authorization may not amount to meaningful deliberation.

The 2026 CCW GGE material discusses understandable human-machine interfaces, training, guidance and mitigation of automation bias. These elements demonstrate why terminology alone is insufficient. Brazil should therefore avoid accepting “human-in-the-loop” as a complete legal answer and instead ask what the human actually knows, decides, and can change.

## 07 — Human-on-the-Loop

Human-on-the-loop arrangements permit autonomous operation while humans monitor the system and retain intervention capabilities. Such arrangements can vary dramatically in practice. A supervisor monitoring a small number of predictable systems in a constrained environment has a different capacity from a supervisor monitoring numerous rapidly operating systems in a dynamic battlefield.

Meaningful supervision requires more than a screen showing system status. The operator must receive information in a form that can be understood, must know the system's relevant limitations, and must have enough time and authority to intervene before a critical engagement. If the system's cycle is faster than the human's ability to understand and respond, the formal intervention power may be largely theoretical.

Brazil should therefore assess on-the-loop systems according to circumstances of use rather than labels. Target type, geographic area, duration, system speed, number of simultaneous engagements, communications, and intervention time all matter. The question is always whether the human can exercise effective judgment where it matters.

## 08 — Human-out-of-the-Loop Systems

When a human cannot intervene in the critical selection or application of force, the system operates without contemporaneous human judgment over that decision. This is particularly consequential when the target's status depends on context, when the environment is dynamic, or when the system's behaviour is not fully predictable.

Brazil's position should remain precise. It need not claim that every autonomous function in every military system is unlawful. Rather, it should identify circumstances in which removing meaningful human control creates unacceptable legal risk or is incompatible with IHL obligations. The central concern is autonomy over critical decisions that materially determine who is subjected to force, when, and under what circumstances.

The absence of a human at the final engagement does not eliminate human responsibility, but it can make the human's earlier decision substantially less connected to the actual use of force. That distinction is central to Brazil's broader argument: accountability cannot be separated from the quality of control that existed before the engagement.

## 09 — Ceremonial Human Oversight

Ceremonial oversight occurs when a human remains formally present while substantive decision-making has shifted toward the autonomous system. This can result from automation bias, excessive system speed, poor interface design, information asymmetry, inadequate training, or institutional pressure to trust machine outputs.

The problem is not solved simply by requiring a human to press a button. If the human lacks meaningful opportunity to question the recommendation, cannot understand relevant limitations, or cannot realistically reject the system's output, the formal presence of a human may obscure rather than solve the control problem.

The 2026 CCW GGE discussion explicitly considers automation bias and the need for training and understandable interfaces. Brazil can therefore frame meaningful control as a quality of decision-making rather than a checkbox. A human should not be reduced to an administrative intermediary between an autonomous system and the use of force.

```text
FORMAL HUMAN PRESENCE
          ↓
   Is information adequate?
          ↓
   Is time adequate?
          ↓
   Is intervention realistic?
          ↓
   Is independent judgment possible?
          ↓
   YES → MEANINGFUL CONTROL
   NO  → CEREMONIAL OVERSIGHT RISK
```

## 10 — The Brazil Concept of Meaningful Control

Brazil can organize meaningful human control around five connected capacities: **understand, anticipate, constrain, intervene, and reassess**. This is not presented as a rigid technological checklist applicable identically to every system. Rather, it is a framework for asking whether human judgment remains substantively connected to autonomous force in the actual circumstances of use.

First, the responsible human must understand relevant capabilities and limitations. Second, behaviour must be sufficiently predictable within the authorized operational envelope. Third, the human must be able to constrain targets, geography, duration, scale and other parameters. Fourth, intervention must be technically and operationally possible when required. Fifth, material changes in circumstances must trigger reassessment rather than automatic continuation.

This framework allows Brazil to engage both prohibitionist and regulatory arguments without reducing the debate to slogans. It also connects directly to the deployment chain: control is created during design and testing, exercised during authorization and operation, and renewed when circumstances materially change.

```text
UNDERSTAND → ANTICIPATE → CONSTRAIN → INTERVENE → REASSESS
       ↖____________________________________________↙
                 CONTINUING HUMAN CONTROL
```

## 11 — Research, Development and Design

Meaningful control begins before deployment. Design choices determine what information the system receives, how objects are classified, what parameters can be imposed, what data are retained, and what intervention mechanisms are available. A later operator cannot fully compensate for a design that prevents meaningful understanding or timely intervention.

This means that weapons review cannot be isolated from system architecture. Developers and acquiring authorities should understand which functions are autonomous, what assumptions underlie system behaviour, and how those assumptions will be communicated to operators. Design decisions can either preserve or undermine later human judgment.

Brazil can therefore argue that human control is partly an engineering property and partly an operational/legal property. A system designed without adequate logging, constraint mechanisms or deactivation capabilities may impose an impossible burden on the operator. Control cannot be created at the final second of deployment if the architecture has already removed the necessary human capacities.

## 12 — Training Data and System Behaviour

Where machine-learning techniques are involved, training data and model construction can influence later behaviour. A model may perform well on representative test conditions yet behave differently when confronted with objects, environments or combinations of factors not adequately represented during development. This creates a gap between human intention and actual system performance.

The ICRC has highlighted difficulties associated with predictability, explainability and testing of learning systems. The concern is not that machine learning is inherently unlawful. Rather, the operator needs sufficient assurance about the conditions in which the system can be relied upon and the situations in which autonomous behaviour should be restricted.

For Brazil, training and validation should therefore be linked. Claims about accuracy should identify the relevant environment, target classes and testing conditions. A general statement that a system performs well is not enough to establish meaningful control in a specific operational setting.

## 13 — Testing and Validation

Testing should establish more than whether a system functions under ideal conditions. A meaningful validation programme should examine behaviour under the conditions in which the system is expected to operate, including environmental variation, target characteristics, sensor limitations, communications, failure modes, operator workload and intervention time.

Validation is particularly important because an autonomous system can be reliable within a defined operational envelope without being suitable outside that envelope. The human's ability to control the system depends partly on knowing where that envelope begins and ends.

Brazil should therefore treat testing as a bridge between technical performance and legal judgment. The question is not simply “Does the system work?” It is “Is its behaviour sufficiently understood and predictable for the proposed use, against the proposed targets, in the proposed environment and for the proposed duration?” That is a far more demanding and useful standard.

## 14 — Operational Authorization

Operational authorization should be based on a concrete understanding of capabilities, limitations and intended conditions of use. A broad approval of a weapon platform is not necessarily equivalent to authorization for every mission in every environment. Autonomous functions may require narrower parameters than the underlying platform itself.

Relevant restrictions can include target category, geographic area, duration, mission objective, environmental conditions, number of systems, and circumstances requiring human intervention. These restrictions narrow the range of situations that the human must anticipate and can therefore strengthen meaningful control.

The critical issue is whether authorization remains connected to reality. If the operation departs materially from the conditions considered at authorization, the responsible authority should have a mechanism to reconsider continued autonomous employment. Authorization should establish the envelope of lawful and controlled use, not create an unlimited presumption of safety.

## 15 — Rules of Engagement and Autonomous Systems

Rules of engagement translate legal and operational requirements into instructions governing the use of force. With autonomous systems, their role becomes particularly important because they can define when autonomous functions may operate, against what categories of targets, within what area, and for how long.

The 2026 CCW GGE discussion includes clear operational guidance and rules of engagement among relevant elements of responsible use. Yet written restrictions are effective only if the system and personnel can implement them. A rule that limits autonomous engagement to a specific target category has limited practical value if the system cannot reliably distinguish that category in the intended environment.

Brazil should therefore connect rules of engagement with technical validation and human supervision. Legal instructions, system capabilities and operational practice must align. If any one of these fails, the existence of a written rule should not be mistaken for meaningful control.

## 16 — Target Selection

Target selection is among the most consequential autonomous functions because it connects classification with the decision to apply force. A human choosing a specific target after considering current information is not analytically equivalent to a human authorizing a broad target profile from which a machine later selects individual objects.

The ICRC describes autonomous weapon systems as systems capable of selecting and applying force to targets without human intervention. The significance lies not simply in technical autonomy but in the fact that the human may not know the specific target, timing or location of the resulting engagement in advance.

Brazil should therefore insist on precision in discussions of “human authorization.” The committee must ask what was actually authorized: a particular target, a bounded target category, a geographic area, a mission, or merely the operation of an autonomous function. The narrower and more informed the human decision, the stronger the connection between human judgment and force.

## 17 — Engagement Decisions

The engagement decision is the point at which previous design, authorization, classification and targeting choices become the physical application of force. A system may have passed testing and received authorization, yet the circumstances of a particular engagement can still determine whether the attack complies with IHL.

This is why human control cannot be exhausted during procurement or activation. If the system independently decides when to fire based on changing circumstances, the State must establish how those circumstances remain within a predictable and controlled operational envelope.

Brazil should distinguish between autonomy that assists execution of a human decision and autonomy that creates the decisive engagement decision. The latter demands far greater scrutiny because the machine's output directly determines the use of force. Where human intervention cannot occur before the critical engagement, predictability and operational restrictions become especially important.

## 18 — Human Supervision During Operation

Effective supervision requires more than watching a status indicator. The supervisor must receive sufficiently informative data, understand what the system is doing, recognize when circumstances fall outside expected conditions, and possess a realistic opportunity to alter or terminate the operation.

The ICRC connects meaningful control with supervision and intervention, while the 2026 CCW discussions highlight training, guidance and understandable interfaces. These elements are mutually reinforcing. A highly trained operator cannot meaningfully supervise a system that provides inadequate information, and a transparent interface is of little use if the operator has no time to act.

Brazil should therefore treat supervision as an operational relationship rather than a staffing requirement. One human may supervise a system in one environment but be unable to supervise the same number of systems in another. Scale, speed and complexity must be included in the assessment.

## 19 — Intervention and Deactivation

Intervention is meaningful only when it is possible at the relevant moment. A system may contain a theoretical shutdown command, but if the operator receives notice after an engagement has already occurred, that command does not provide meaningful control over the engagement itself.

Intervention also requires authority. Operators need clear instructions about when autonomous employment must be interrupted, what circumstances trigger deactivation, and who can make the decision. The ICRC identifies timely intervention and deactivation as relevant elements of human control.

Brazil should therefore distinguish **technical possibility** from **operational effectiveness**. A button that exists is not enough. The operator must have sufficient warning, information, time and authority to use it. This is particularly important where systems operate at high speed or where multiple autonomous platforms act simultaneously.

```text
DETECT CHANGE → ALERT HUMAN → ASSESS → INTERVENE → VERIFY STOP
                     │
                     └── If time is insufficient → CONTROL FAILURE RISK
```

## 20 — Post-Operation Review and Traceability

Post-operation review cannot substitute for control during an engagement, but it is important for determining whether the assumptions behind deployment were correct. Logs can preserve information about commands, system outputs, warnings, human interventions and operational conditions, allowing investigators to reconstruct what occurred.

The 2026 CCW material discusses traceability mechanisms such as data logs and audit trails while recognizing that traceability can be technically complex. This matters because a system whose operation cannot be reconstructed can make both learning and accountability more difficult.

Brazil should therefore treat traceability as part of a broader control architecture. It does not make an otherwise uncontrolled weapon lawful, but it can support investigation, training, system improvement, legal review and identification of recurring failures. The stronger the autonomy, the more important reliable records can become.

## 21 — Predictability as a Condition of Control

Predictability is central to meaningful control because a human cannot meaningfully govern behaviour that is not sufficiently understood in the relevant circumstances. Predictability does not require mathematical certainty or perfect foresight. It requires a degree of confidence about expected behaviour and effects sufficient for the human decision-maker to make lawful judgments.

The ICRC has linked human control closely with predictability and reliability and has highlighted the challenge created by systems that can adapt, interact, or operate in complex environments. Predictability should therefore be assessed in relation to the intended task, target, environment, duration and scale.

Brazil should resist the argument that any measurable probability of error automatically creates an acceptable legal threshold. Technical performance metrics must be connected to the actual legal task. A system can have a high average accuracy while still behaving unpredictably in the particular circumstances in which a civilian or military object must be distinguished.

## 22 — Reliability Versus Predictability

Reliability and predictability are related but distinct concepts. Reliability generally concerns whether a system performs a function consistently. Predictability concerns whether humans can reasonably anticipate its behaviour and effects within the relevant circumstances. A system may be reliable at completing a defined task while remaining unsuitable when the environment changes.

This distinction matters because military systems are not evaluated only by average technical performance. Human decision-makers need to know the boundaries of that performance. If a system is dependable only under specific conditions, those conditions become part of the control framework.

Brazil can therefore ask delegations citing high accuracy or reliability to identify the operational envelope behind the statistic. What target set was tested? Under what environmental conditions? For how long? With what level of civilian complexity? What happens when the system leaves those conditions? The answer to those questions is more informative than a single performance percentage.

## 23 — Explainability and Legal Judgment

Explainability matters because human judgment may depend on understanding why a system produced a particular output. This does not necessarily require exposing every mathematical operation inside a model. It does require enough information for the responsible human to understand relevant capabilities, limitations, uncertainty and the basis for relying on the system in the intended circumstances.

The 2026 CCW discussions emphasize understandable human-machine interfaces. This is significant because an operator cannot effectively supervise a system through an interface that hides material warnings, limitations or changes in operational status.

Brazil should therefore distinguish technical explainability from operational intelligibility. The committee does not need every soldier to become a machine-learning engineer. It does need responsible personnel to understand what the system is authorized to do, what it cannot reliably do, when its outputs should be questioned, and when autonomous operation must be interrupted.

## 24 — The Black-Box Problem

Machine-learning systems can make their internal reasoning difficult to inspect. This becomes particularly consequential when a system's output contributes directly to target selection or engagement. If a human cannot determine why a system classified an object as a target, the ability to independently assess that classification can be weakened.

The ICRC has highlighted difficulties in predicting, understanding, explaining and testing certain learning systems. The concern is therefore not merely philosophical. It affects the practical capacity of a human operator to exercise judgment over a critical function.

Brazil should avoid claiming that every opaque algorithm is automatically unlawful. Instead, the argument should be contextual: the more consequential the autonomous function and the more dynamic the environment, the stronger the need for sufficient transparency, testing and human understanding. A black box may be tolerable for a low-consequence analytical aid while creating far greater concerns when it independently selects a target for lethal engagement.

## 25 — Uncertainty and Confidence Thresholds

A machine confidence score is not equivalent to a legal judgment. A system may report high confidence while operating outside the conditions for which it was validated. Conversely, uncertainty may be resolvable by a human who can obtain additional information or choose not to employ force.

Meaningful human control therefore requires a relationship between system uncertainty and human action. Operators need to know when uncertainty should trigger additional verification, rejection of a machine recommendation, narrowing of the mission, or termination of autonomous operation.

Brazil can challenge the simplistic idea that a numerical threshold solves the problem. The committee must ask what the number measures, how it was calibrated, whether it remains valid in the operational environment, and how it relates to the legal requirement to distinguish and take precautions. A statistical confidence score cannot substitute for human legal judgment.

## 26 — Distribution Shift and Changing Environments

A system may perform differently when operational conditions differ from the conditions represented during development and testing. Changes in terrain, lighting, weather, sensor quality, civilian patterns, object appearance or adversary behaviour can alter system performance. This is particularly relevant to machine-learning systems whose behaviour depends on patterns learned from data.

Brazil's 2024 submission emphasizes that the legality of autonomous weapon systems depends significantly on the mode and context of their use. The technical identity of a weapon therefore cannot answer every legal question in advance.

The practical implication is that deployment envelopes must be defined realistically. If a system is validated only under limited conditions, those limits should inform authorization. When conditions move beyond the validated envelope, continued autonomous operation should not be presumed safe merely because the system performed well during testing.

## 27 — Adaptive and Learning Systems

Adaptive systems create a distinctive control question because their behaviour may evolve after initial testing or authorization. If a model changes during operation, the assumptions on which the human relied may no longer describe the system accurately. This is particularly significant when changes affect classification, target selection, or engagement behaviour.

The ICRC has raised concerns about systems that learn during use and about the difficulty of maintaining certainty that attacks remain within legal limits when system behaviour changes. This does not mean all adaptation is inherently prohibited. It means adaptation must be included in validation, monitoring and reassessment.

Brazil should ask a simple question: **what exactly did the human authorize?** If the answer refers to a system whose relevant behaviour can materially change after authorization, the control framework must account for that change. A human cannot meaningfully supervise an evolving system while treating it as identical to the system that was originally tested.

## 28 — Multiple-System Interaction and Swarming

Autonomous systems operating in groups create a scale problem. An operator may be capable of supervising one system but unable to meaningfully supervise dozens or hundreds simultaneously, especially when systems interact and their collective behaviour produces effects not apparent from any individual platform.

The ICRC has identified increased interaction between systems, including swarms, as a factor that can reduce predictability. The concern therefore extends beyond the performance of individual machines. It includes the emergent complexity of the overall operation and the human capacity to monitor it.

Brazil should include scale as a control variable. The question is not simply whether each platform has an intervention mechanism, but whether the human has sufficient information and time to use those mechanisms across the operation. If simultaneous engagements exceed the human's practical capacity to assess and intervene, formal supervision can become nominal.

## 29 — Failure Modes and Unexpected Behaviour

No testing programme can guarantee that a system will never encounter an unexpected circumstance. The relevant question is whether foreseeable failure modes have been identified, whether the operational envelope is defined, and whether safe responses exist when the system behaves unexpectedly.

Unexpected behaviour is especially important when autonomous systems operate in environments containing novel objects, ambiguous targets, communications disruption, sensor degradation, or rapidly changing conditions. A system that simply continues operating despite uncertainty may create a control problem that cannot be solved after the fact.

Brazil should therefore connect failure-mode analysis to deployment restrictions. The State should know which failures trigger automatic termination, human review, reduced autonomy, or withdrawal from the mission. This is another reason meaningful human control is a continuing condition rather than a single authorization event.

## 30 — Predictability of Effects, Not Merely Accuracy

Accuracy is an incomplete measure of lawful autonomous employment. A system can classify an object accurately according to a technical model while the object is not lawfully targetable in the circumstances. Similarly, a system may identify a military objective correctly while the proportionality assessment changes because civilian circumstances have changed.

Predictability must therefore concern the relationship between system behaviour and the operational effects relevant to IHL. Technical accuracy can support this assessment, but it cannot replace it.

Brazil should insist on this distinction when delegations rely heavily on technical performance figures. The relevant question is not simply “How often does the model classify correctly?” It is “Can the responsible human reasonably anticipate the system's behaviour and resulting effects within the specific conditions in which force will be used?” That is the standard that connects technology to legal judgment.

## 31 — The Operating Environment as a Legal Variable

The same autonomous system can present different control problems in different environments. A system operating within a narrow geographic area against a clearly defined military target type for a short duration is not necessarily comparable to a system operating for extended periods across a complex environment containing civilians and ambiguous objects.

The ICRC identifies operating environment, target type, geographic area, duration and scope as relevant considerations for human control. Brazil's contextual approach reaches the same basic conclusion: legality and meaningful control cannot always be determined by looking at the weapon platform in isolation.

This is important for international regulation because a single technical label can conceal radically different modes of use. A useful framework should regulate the conditions under which autonomous functions may be employed rather than relying exclusively on broad categories of technology.

## 32 — Urban Warfare and Civilian Density

Urban environments can combine dense civilian populations, complex infrastructure, moving persons, ambiguous objects, restricted visibility and rapidly changing circumstances. These features can increase the difficulty of distinguishing lawful targets and assessing incidental civilian harm.

The issue is not that autonomy is automatically impossible in every urban environment. Rather, the burden of demonstrating meaningful control can become greater when the environment is highly dynamic and civilian presence is difficult to characterize in advance. Target restrictions, geographic limits, duration and intervention capabilities therefore become particularly important.

Brazil should ask whether the system was validated for the specific environment and whether the human can intervene when conditions depart from those assumptions. A control architecture suitable for a constrained environment cannot simply be assumed adequate in a densely populated city.

## 33 — Dynamic Battlefields

Battlefields change continuously. People move, objects change status, military units relocate, communications degrade, weather changes, and information becomes obsolete. A target that satisfied the relevant conditions at one moment may no longer satisfy them later.

Meaningful control must therefore accommodate temporal change. A human decision made at the beginning of a mission cannot necessarily govern every subsequent machine decision if the factual circumstances materially change. This is particularly important for systems capable of operating for extended periods without renewed human authorization.

Brazil can therefore argue that autonomy should be bounded by time as well as geography and target type. The longer a system operates without renewed human judgment, the greater the possibility that the conditions underlying the original decision will no longer exist.

## 34 — Geographic Restrictions

Geographic restrictions can narrow the range of circumstances in which autonomous functions operate. By defining where a system may act, the responsible authority can reduce uncertainty about the environment, target population and expected operational conditions.

The ICRC includes geographical scope among practical restrictions that can contribute to human control. Such restrictions can be especially important where civilian density, infrastructure, terrain or target characteristics differ sharply between locations.

Brazil should therefore treat geography as part of the control architecture rather than merely a planning detail. A system authorized within a defined area should have mechanisms that prevent or identify operation outside that area. If the system cannot reliably respect geographic boundaries, the human's authorization may not correspond to the system's actual behaviour.

## 35 — Temporal Restrictions

Time is a control variable because the factual basis of a decision can change. A short autonomous engagement may occur within conditions the human can realistically understand and supervise, while prolonged autonomous operation creates greater opportunities for environmental and target changes.

A time limit can therefore function as a practical mechanism for preserving the connection between human authorization and operational reality. The expiration of an authorization does not necessarily mean that every autonomous action must cease in exactly the same way, but it should trigger a clearly defined legal and operational condition for continuation.

Brazil should emphasize that duration must be considered alongside target, environment and system behaviour. A weapon that is predictable for a short bounded period may present a different control challenge if allowed to search and engage autonomously for hours without meaningful human reassessment.

## 36 — Target-Type Restrictions

Restricting the category of targets can reduce the range of judgments that an autonomous system must make. The narrower and more objectively identifiable the target set, the more feasible it may be to validate system behaviour and constrain autonomous employment.

However, a target category written into an authorization is only useful if the system can reliably distinguish that category in the actual environment. A broad or ambiguous target definition may simply transfer a complex legal judgment from the human to the machine.

Brazil should therefore evaluate target restrictions together with system capability. The committee should ask whether the target category is sufficiently specific, whether the system can recognize it under expected conditions, what happens when classification is uncertain, and whether a human can intervene before force is applied. Target restrictions can support meaningful control, but they do not replace it.

## 37 — Scale of Autonomous Employment

Scale changes the nature of supervision. Human oversight that is realistic for a single platform may become impossible when dozens of systems operate simultaneously. The problem is not only the number of machines but the number of simultaneous decisions, alerts, targets and possible interventions that reach the human operator.

The 2026 CCW discussion's attention to human-machine interaction and automation bias is relevant here. Excessive information can be as problematic as insufficient information if the operator cannot distinguish the warnings that require immediate action.

Brazil should therefore include operator workload in any meaningful-control assessment. A State should be able to demonstrate that the personnel responsible for supervision can actually monitor the system at the relevant tempo. Merely assigning a human supervisor to a large autonomous operation does not establish that the human retains meaningful control over it.

## 38 — Communications Failure and Degraded Conditions

Communications links can fail or become unreliable. Sensors can degrade, navigation can be disrupted, and operators can lose access to information required for intervention. A control framework that assumes perfect connectivity may therefore overstate the real level of human control.

Meaningful control requires predefined responses to degraded conditions. The system should have clearly understood behaviour when communication with the responsible human is lost, and the human should know what autonomy remains possible and what triggers termination or return to a safer state.

Brazil should treat loss of communication as an operational control question rather than merely a technical inconvenience. If the human's ability to understand, supervise or intervene is materially reduced, the State must have a basis for determining whether continued autonomous operation remains within the authorized envelope.

## 39 — Changing Mission Parameters

Mission parameters can change after deployment. The target set may expand, the geographic area may be altered, the duration may be extended, or the operational objective may shift. Each change can affect the assumptions underlying the original authorization and validation.

A meaningful-control framework should therefore distinguish minor administrative adjustments from material changes that alter the nature of autonomous employment. Material changes should trigger a deliberate reassessment rather than being absorbed automatically into an existing authorization.

Brazil's position is particularly strong here because reassessment preserves the connection between human judgment and changing operational circumstances. The human does not need to micromanage every technical action, but the human should retain authority over changes that materially alter what the autonomous system is being asked to do.

```text
      NO                                    YES
      ↓                                      ↓
Continue under controls                 REASSESS
                                             ↓
                                  Renew / restrict / terminate
```

## 40 — Reassessment During Deployment

Reassessment is the mechanism that prevents an old authorization from becoming detached from new circumstances. It can be triggered by material changes in the environment, target characteristics, system behaviour, communications, mission parameters, or civilian presence.

The purpose is not bureaucratic repetition. It is to renew human judgment when the factual assumptions that supported the earlier decision no longer hold. The longer or more adaptive the autonomous operation, the more important this becomes.

Brazil should therefore distinguish reassessment from routine monitoring. Monitoring observes; reassessment decides whether the legal and operational basis for continued autonomy remains sound. A system can be functioning exactly as designed and still require reassessment if the environment or mission has materially changed.

## 41 — Article 36 Weapons Reviews

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires States party to the Protocol, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would be prohibited in some or all circumstances by applicable international law. Weapons reviews therefore provide an established legal mechanism for examining new technologies before they enter operational use.

For autonomous systems, the review cannot sensibly examine only the physical platform. It should consider the weapon's autonomous functions, intended methods of employment, foreseeable operating environments, target types, duration, constraints and human interaction. The ICRC has emphasized the importance of rigorous weapons reviews in the context of emerging technologies.

Brazil should also emphasize that a review does not create permanent authorization for every future use. The conclusions depend on the weapon as studied and the conditions contemplated. Significant modifications or materially different modes of use can require renewed legal assessment.

## 42 — Distinction and Autonomous Targeting

The principle of distinction requires parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants and between civilian objects and military objectives, and to direct operations only against permissible targets. Autonomous targeting therefore raises a fundamental question about whether the system can perform its delegated function within the relevant circumstances.

Brazil should avoid reducing distinction to a simple image-recognition problem. Legal classification can depend on context, information, conduct and changing circumstances. A technical system may contribute to identification while a human retains the final legal judgment.

Where autonomous functions determine which objects will be engaged, the State must establish that the system's capabilities and operational constraints are compatible with the distinction requirement in the intended environment. If the system cannot reliably operate within those conditions, meaningful human control becomes correspondingly more difficult to establish.

## 43 — Proportionality and Contextual Judgment

The proportionality rule prohibits attacks expected to cause incidental civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. This is a contextual assessment, not merely a classification task. Information about civilian presence, military advantage, timing and expected effects can change during an operation.

Autonomous systems can assist with information, estimation and decision support, but the question becomes more difficult when a system independently selects and engages targets based on changing circumstances. A human must remain capable of making or appropriately supervising the relevant judgment where the law requires contextual assessment.

Brazil should therefore distinguish between autonomy that supports a human proportionality assessment and autonomy that effectively substitutes machine-generated calculations for the human judgment required by the circumstances. Technical estimates can inform a legal decision; they do not automatically constitute the legal decision itself.

## 44 — Precautions in Attack

The obligation to take feasible precautions in attack requires parties to do what is feasible to verify targets, choose means and methods that reduce civilian harm, and cancel or suspend attacks when required circumstances arise. Autonomous systems do not remove these obligations.

The control question is whether the system and its operators can implement precautionary requirements in the circumstances of use. This includes target verification, information gathering, appropriate choice of weapon and intervention when relevant information changes.

Brazil can therefore argue that meaningful human control and precautions are closely connected. A human who cannot understand the system's limitations, cannot receive updated information, or cannot interrupt the engagement may face serious difficulty fulfilling precautionary responsibilities. The existence of autonomy does not lower the legal standard; it changes how the State must demonstrate that the standard can be met.

## 45 — Unlawful or Unpredictable Effects

Unpredictable effects create a direct challenge to lawful employment because IHL obligations concern actual attacks and expected consequences, not merely the intentions of developers. If a State cannot reasonably anticipate relevant system behaviour in the circumstances of use, it may be unable to demonstrate that the weapon can be employed consistently with applicable rules.

This is why predictability must be connected to operational limits. A system may be acceptable within a narrow validated environment but unsuitable when transferred to a different environment or target set. The legal assessment must therefore identify where predictability is sufficient and where it breaks down.

Brazil should frame this as a question of evidence rather than rhetoric. What testing establishes the expected behaviour? What are the known limitations? What happens outside the validated envelope? What human intervention exists? What triggers termination? These questions transform a general claim of “reliability” into a concrete assessment of lawful employment.

## 46 — Existing IHL and New International Rules

Existing IHL applies to new weapons and technologies, including autonomous systems. The fact that a technology is novel does not suspend established obligations concerning distinction, proportionality, precautions and other applicable rules. At the same time, the existence of IHL does not necessarily resolve every policy, accountability or weapons-specific question created by increasing autonomy.

Brazil can therefore reject two extremes: the claim that autonomy exists in a legal vacuum, and the claim that existing IHL automatically resolves every concern without further international clarification. The CCW is specifically designed to address weapons-related humanitarian concerns and can develop additional rules without abandoning existing law.

The strongest position is therefore complementary: existing IHL remains binding, while international rules can clarify what meaningful human control, operational constraints, testing, review and responsible employment should require in the specific context of LAWS.

## 47 — Brazil's Two-Tier Approach

Brazil has supported a two-tier approach distinguishing weapons systems that should be prohibited from those that may be permissible subject to stringent regulation. For the purposes of meaningful human control, the distinction should be grounded in the functions and circumstances of the system rather than in broad claims about whether all autonomy is good or bad.

The prohibited category should address systems whose autonomous functions cannot be reconciled with applicable IHL or meaningful human control in the relevant circumstances. The regulated category should address systems whose employment can be constrained through meaningful human judgment, operational limitations, testing, review and safeguards.

Brazil's approach therefore connects prohibition and regulation to the actual control problem. The existence of technical sophistication should not be treated as sufficient evidence of legality. Conversely, a system should not be treated as identical to every other autonomous weapon merely because it contains an autonomous function. The central inquiry remains: what does it do, where, against whom, for how long, and under what human control?

## 48 — A Brazil Test for Lawful Meaningful Control

Brazil can consolidate its position into five capacities. **Understand:** responsible personnel must know relevant capabilities, limitations and operating conditions. **Anticipate:** behaviour must be sufficiently predictable within the authorized envelope. **Constrain:** the human must be able to set meaningful limits on target, geography, duration, scope and other critical parameters. **Intervene:** the human must have a realistic opportunity and authority to interrupt when required. **Reassess:** material changes must trigger renewed human judgment.

These capacities are interconnected. A human cannot effectively intervene without information and time. A human cannot anticipate behaviour without adequate testing. A human cannot constrain a system if the system lacks enforceable operational boundaries. Reassessment is necessary when the factual basis of the original judgment changes.

```text
          UNDERSTAND
              ↓
          ANTICIPATE
              ↓
          CONSTRAIN
              ↓
          INTERVENE
              ↓
          REASSESS
              ↺
     CONTINUING HUMAN CONTROL
```

This framework gives Brazil a concrete vocabulary for negotiations without reducing meaningful control to a single technological specification.

## 49 — Counterarguments and Rebuttals

One common argument is that existing IHL already provides sufficient rules and that adding a new control standard is unnecessary. Brazil can respond that existing IHL remains applicable, but operational uncertainty about increasingly autonomous systems can still justify clarifying how human control, testing, review and restrictions should be implemented.

Another argument is that a human remains responsible because a commander authorized the weapon. Brazil's response is that authorization and control are distinct. The question is whether the human decision remains sufficiently connected to the actual circumstances of force. A third argument is that technology will improve and therefore restrictions should not be too rigid. Brazil can accept technological development while maintaining that legal obligations apply to the system actually employed, not a hypothetical future system.

A further argument is that national discretion is necessary because States use different technologies and doctrines. Brazil can acknowledge operational diversity while insisting that international law cannot become so discretionary that the meaning of meaningful control changes entirely from one State to another. Flexibility in implementation should not eliminate substantive minimum standards.

## 50 — Brazil's International Framework

Brazil's framework should preserve a clear hierarchy: existing IHL remains binding; systems incompatible with those obligations or incapable of meaningful human control in their intended circumstances should not be employed; and systems that can be lawfully employed should be subject to rigorous operational constraints and continuing human oversight.

An international instrument should therefore address the entire deployment chain rather than focusing only on the moment of firing. It should cover design and weapons review, testing and validation, operator training, authorization, rules of engagement, target and geographic restrictions, duration, scale, intervention, traceability, investigation and reassessment following material changes.

The essential principle is straightforward. Human control should be **substantive, contextual and continuous**. A human should understand the system, anticipate its relevant behaviour, constrain its operation, intervene when necessary and reassess employment when circumstances materially change. This approach does not demand that every military system operate identically. It demands that autonomy never become a substitute for the human judgment required to keep the use of force within international law.

```text
DESIGN
  ↓
LEGAL REVIEW
  ↓
TESTING & VALIDATION
  ↓
TRAINING
  ↓
AUTHORIZATION + ROE
  ↓
DEPLOYMENT
  ↓
SUPERVISION / INTERVENTION
  ↓
MATERIAL CHANGE?
  ↙          ↘
NO           YES
↓             ↓
CONTINUE   REASSESS
  \          /
   POST-OPERATION REVIEW
          ↓
     TRACEABILITY
          ↓
    LESSONS / REVIEW
```

Brazil's objective is not to replace human judgment with a technological formula. It is to ensure that technological autonomy remains bounded by human judgment wherever the law requires human judgment. **Autonomy may alter the chain of execution; it must never erase the conditions that make human control meaningful.**
