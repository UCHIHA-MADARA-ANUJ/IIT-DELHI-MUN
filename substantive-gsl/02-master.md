# SUBSTANTIVE II — MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL: FROM AUTHORIZATION TO OPERATIONAL REALITY

## 01 — Defining Meaningful Human Control

Meaningful human control is not satisfied merely because a human appears somewhere in the deployment chain. Brazil's 2024 CCW submission describes it as a mechanism through which the human operator retains ultimate decision-making power over critical functions, particularly targeting decisions before the use of force. The distinction matters because an autonomous system may be activated by a human while the human does not select the specific target, timing or circumstances of the resulting engagement. Meaningful control therefore concerns the quality and location of human judgment, not simply the existence of a human signature on an authorization form.

The concept should be understood across the lifecycle and operational context of the system. A person cannot meaningfully control an outcome that they cannot adequately understand, anticipate or constrain. Brazil therefore treats meaningful control as a substantive legal and operational condition rather than a ceremonial procedural requirement.

```text
HUMAN INVOLVEMENT → UNDERSTAND → ANTICIPATE → CONSTRAIN → INTERVENE → REASSESS
                                      ↓
                         MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL
```

## 02 — Human Agency and the Decision to Employ Force

International humanitarian law regulates attacks through obligations borne by human decision-makers. The ICRC has emphasized that legal obligations and accountability cannot be transferred to a machine, computer programme or weapon system. The central question is whether the human decision to employ a system remains sufficiently connected to the eventual use of force. If the machine independently determines the particular target and circumstances of engagement, a formal human authorization may be too remote to constitute meaningful judgment by itself.

This does not mean every autonomous function is automatically unlawful. The significance depends on what the system does, where it operates, against what targets, for how long, and under what constraints. Brazil's approach is contextual: human agency must remain substantively connected to critical functions in the circumstances in which force is actually applied.

## 03 — Authorization Is Not Control

Authorization answers whether a person permitted a mission or system to be employed. Control asks whether a human can meaningfully govern relevant system behaviour while that employment occurs. Conflating the two creates a gap in which responsibility may formally exist while substantive decision-making has migrated into an autonomous process.

A commander could authorize an operation based on a defined target set and geographic area. If circumstances subsequently change, the original authorization does not make every later machine decision predictable or lawful. Continuing control must therefore be treated as a distinct requirement.

```text
AUTHORIZATION → INITIAL CONDITIONS → AUTONOMOUS OPERATION
```

## 04 — The Human–Machine Decision Nexus

The decisive issue is the nexus between human judgment and the application of force. A machine may process information faster than a human, but speed does not itself establish lawful or meaningful control. What matters is whether the responsible human understands relevant system behaviour, knows the circumstances in which it will operate, establishes appropriate limits and can intervene when necessary.

Brazil can distinguish decision-support from autonomous decision-making. An AI system that provides information to a human who independently decides whether and how to attack is not analytically identical to a system that independently selects and attacks a target. The degree and location of autonomy matter because they determine where the critical judgment occurs.

## 05 — Critical Functions of LAWS

The phrase “critical functions” should not become an empty technical label. Identification, tracking, target selection and engagement can each affect whether a human retains meaningful control over the use of force. A system may perform one function autonomously while leaving another to a human, producing materially different legal and operational circumstances.

Brazil's 2024 submission places particular emphasis on ultimate human decision-making over critical functions, especially targeting and the use of force. The appropriate inquiry is functional rather than merely technological: what exactly has been delegated to the machine, and what remains within human judgment at the moment force is applied?

## 06 — Human-in-the-Loop

“Human-in-the-loop” is useful terminology, but the label alone proves very little. A human may technically approve each engagement while receiving insufficient information, facing an unrealistic response time, or relying almost entirely on an opaque machine recommendation. Formal intervention can therefore exist without meaningful deliberation.

The substantive question is whether the human had the information, competence, time and authority necessary to make an independent judgment. The 2026 CCW GGE discussion identifies understandable human-machine interfaces, guidance, training and mitigation of automation bias as relevant measures.

## 07 — Human-on-the-Loop

Human-on-the-loop arrangements allow systems to operate with greater autonomy while humans monitor performance and retain intervention capabilities. Their adequacy depends on operational circumstances. Monitoring a system is not meaningful if the operator cannot understand its outputs or cannot intervene before the critical engagement.

Human supervision and the ability to intervene are important elements of control, alongside predictability and operational restrictions. Brazil should therefore avoid treating “on-the-loop” as automatically sufficient or automatically unlawful. Its value depends on the actual system, mission, environment, duration and time available for intervention.

## 08 — Human-out-of-the-Loop Systems

When no human can intervene in the critical decision to apply force, the system operates without meaningful contemporaneous human judgment over that decision. This is particularly consequential where the system encounters dynamic circumstances or targets whose status depends on context.

Brazil's position is not that every autonomous function in warfare must be prohibited. Rather, the further autonomy extends into critical selection and application of force, the greater the burden on the State to establish that resulting behaviour remains within lawful and acceptable limits. Brazil's 2024 submission states that absence of meaningful human control renders a system incompatible with IHL.

## 09 — Ceremonial Human Oversight

A central risk is ceremonial oversight: a human remains nominally responsible while the practical decision is determined by a system the human cannot realistically challenge. This can occur through automation bias, excessive system speed, information asymmetry, insufficient training or an interface that presents machine recommendations as authoritative.

The 2026 CCW discussion notes automation-bias concerns and the importance of training and understandable interfaces. These are not merely technical conveniences. They determine whether oversight remains a genuine source of judgment or becomes a formal layer placed above autonomous decision-making.

## 10 — The Brazil Concept of Meaningful Control

Brazil can articulate meaningful control through five connected capacities: **understand, anticipate, constrain, intervene and reassess**. The framework does not claim every weapon must have identical controls. The required degree depends on system functions and circumstances of use.

A human must understand relevant capabilities and limitations; anticipate behaviour sufficiently in the authorized environment; constrain targets, geography, duration and other parameters; intervene when necessary; and reassess continued use when material circumstances change.

## 11 — Research, Development and Design

Meaningful control begins before deployment. Design choices determine what information a system receives, how it classifies objects, what parameters can be imposed, what logs are created and what intervention mechanisms are available. A later operator cannot fully repair a design that prevents meaningful understanding or intervention.

CCW work on the human role has stressed that development, testing and training affect what kind of human involvement may be acceptable during use. The development stage is therefore part of the control architecture rather than an isolated technical phase.

## 12 — Training Data and System Behaviour

Where machine-learning systems are used, training data and model construction can influence later behaviour. This creates a distinction between human intention behind a system and actual performance in an unfamiliar environment. A model may perform strongly in tested conditions while producing less predictable outputs when conditions differ materially.

The ICRC notes that machine-learning systems can be difficult to predict, understand, explain and test, while adaptive systems raise additional questions where the model changes during use. This makes the relationship between design, validation and operational control central to meaningful human judgment.

## 13 — Testing and Validation

Testing should establish more than whether a system works in ideal conditions. The relevant question is whether behaviour is sufficiently understood and predictable in the circumstances in which it will actually be used. Validation should consider environmental conditions, target characteristics, failure modes, intervention time and operational constraints.

The ICRC has identified practical difficulties in assessing predictability and reliability and has linked human control to strict operational constraints. A system validated in one environment cannot automatically be assumed to behave equivalently in another.

## 14 — Operational Authorization

Operational authorization should be based on an identifiable understanding of system capabilities, limitations and intended employment conditions. Authorization should define the circumstances under which autonomous functions may operate rather than merely approving a weapon in the abstract.

Target categories, geographic boundaries, duration, mission objectives and environmental conditions can restrict the range of situations in which autonomous behaviour is permitted. Such constraints are repeatedly identified in international discussions of practical human control.

## 15 — Rules of Engagement and Autonomous Systems

Rules of engagement translate legal and operational requirements into instructions for personnel. With autonomous systems, their importance extends to defining how, where and against what targets autonomous functions may be employed. The 2026 CCW discussion highlighted clear operational guidance, including rules of engagement, alongside training and understandable interfaces.

Written restrictions cannot compensate for a system that cannot reliably remain within them. A rule limiting a system to a target category has limited value if the system cannot reliably distinguish that category in the environment of use.

## 16 — Target Selection

Target selection is among the most consequential autonomous functions because it connects classification with the decision to apply force. Human control is materially different when the human chooses the specific target from when the human only authorizes a generalized target profile.

The ICRC describes autonomous systems as capable of selecting and applying force to targets without human intervention and emphasizes that users may not know the specific target, timing or location of resulting applications of force. Brazil's 2024 submission consequently places particular importance on human decision-making in targeting.

## 17 — Engagement Decisions

The engagement decision is where the consequences of preceding stages become physical. A system may have been lawfully designed and authorized, yet the circumstances of a particular engagement can still determine whether its use complies with IHL.

Human control must therefore remain connected to the critical use of force rather than being exhausted during procurement or activation. Where a system independently chooses when and where to apply force, predictability, operational restrictions and intervention become especially important.

## 18 — Human Supervision During Operation

Supervision requires more than observing a status screen. Effective supervision depends on information quality, operator competence, system transparency, time available for response and the ability to alter or terminate the operation. A supervisor who receives insufficient information or cannot act before engagement does not necessarily exercise meaningful control.

The ICRC expressly connects human control with supervision and intervention. The 2026 CCW discussion similarly identifies understandable interfaces, guidance and training as relevant measures. These elements should be assessed together.

## 19 — Intervention and Deactivation

Intervention is meaningful only when it is technically possible, legally authorized and operationally timely. A theoretical ability to deactivate a system after an engagement has occurred does not preserve meaningful control over that engagement.

The ICRC identifies timely intervention and deactivation as important elements of human-machine interaction. Brazil should therefore ask whether the human can intervene **before** the critical use of force when circumstances require it, rather than merely whether a shutdown function exists somewhere in the system.

## 20 — Post-Operation Review

Post-operation review cannot substitute for control during an engagement, but it remains important for identifying failures, improving training and determining whether authorization assumptions remain valid. Logs and audit trails can help reconstruct instructions, system outputs and human interventions.

The 2026 CCW discussion records proposals for traceability mechanisms such as data logs and audit trails, while noting that traceability needs careful treatment because not all systems are inherently traceable. Review is therefore part of a wider accountability architecture, not a replacement for control.

## 21 — Predictability as a Condition of Control

Predictability is central because human control cannot be meaningful if the human cannot reasonably anticipate relevant effects in authorized circumstances. The ICRC has linked effective control closely to predictability and reliability and warned that increasing mobility, adaptability and interaction between systems can reduce predictability.

Predictability does not mean mathematical certainty. It means sufficient understanding of expected behaviour and effects for human decision-makers to make lawful judgments. The necessary level depends on the system's task, target, environment, duration and scope.

## 22 — Reliability Versus Predictability

Reliability and predictability are related but distinct. A system might consistently perform a defined task while still producing behaviour that is difficult for an operator to anticipate in a changing environment. Conversely, a system may be predictable within a narrow envelope even if it is not highly capable outside that envelope.

A high technical success rate therefore does not automatically prove meaningful human control. The relevant question is whether performance characteristics are sufficiently understood and stable for lawful judgment in the intended circumstances.

## 23 — Explainability and Legal Judgment

Explainability matters because legal decision-making may require humans to understand why a system produced a particular output. If an operator cannot interpret the relevant basis of a classification or determine whether the system remains within validated parameters, supervision becomes substantially weaker.

The 2026 CCW material emphasizes understandable human-machine interfaces. Explainability should not mean that every algorithm must be intelligible in ordinary language; the relevant question is whether humans possess enough information to exercise responsible control.

## 24 — The Black-Box Problem

Machine-learning systems can make their internal reasoning difficult to inspect. The ICRC has noted that some models can be difficult for humans to predict, understand, explain and test, particularly where learning processes influence system behaviour.

For LAWS, opacity becomes especially consequential where the system controls functions connected to target selection and force application. The issue is whether the responsible human can obtain sufficient assurance about behaviour and limitations to make the relevant legal and operational judgments.

## 25 — Uncertainty and Confidence Thresholds

A machine confidence score is not itself a legal judgment. A system may report high confidence while operating outside the conditions for which it was validated, while low confidence may arise in circumstances where a human can resolve uncertainty through additional information.

Meaningful human control requires an informed relationship between system uncertainty and human action. Operators need guidance on when uncertainty requires rejection, additional verification, restriction of autonomy or reassessment. A numerical confidence value cannot by itself answer the legal question.

## 26 — Distribution Shift and Changing Environments

A system's performance can change when the operational environment differs from the conditions represented during development and testing. Differences in terrain, weather, lighting, objects, behaviour or sensor conditions can alter classification and tracking performance.

Brazil's 2024 submission expressly states that AWS legality significantly depends on mode of use and functioning within specific contexts. The technical identity of a weapon is therefore insufficient to determine whether its use remains lawful in a particular environment.

## 27 — Adaptive and Learning Systems

Adaptive systems raise a distinctive control problem because behaviour may evolve after initial authorization or validation. If a system changes how it identifies or responds to targets during operation, assumptions underlying the original authorization may no longer hold.

The ICRC has raised concerns about systems that learn during use and questioned how users can remain reasonably certain that attacks remain within legal limits when the model changes. Adaptation must therefore be incorporated into the control and reassessment framework.

## 28 — Multiple-System Interaction and Swarming

Autonomous systems can interact in ways more difficult to anticipate than the behaviour of a single system. The ICRC has identified increased interaction between systems, including swarms, as a factor that may reduce predictability.

This creates a scale problem: an operator who can supervise one platform may not be able to meaningfully supervise hundreds operating simultaneously. Human control must consider aggregate workload and interaction effects, not only individual system performance.

## 29 — Failure Modes and Unexpected Behaviour

Testing cannot eliminate every unexpected circumstance. The relevant control question is what happens when a system encounters an input or condition outside its validated operational envelope. A robust framework should identify failure modes, define safe responses and establish when autonomous operation must stop or be reconsidered.

IHL obligations do not disappear when technology behaves unexpectedly. Human decision-makers remain responsible for deciding whether continued employment is permissible within circumstances known to them.

## 30 — Predictability of Effects, Not Merely Accuracy

Accuracy is an incomplete metric for autonomous weapons. A system might accurately classify a target according to its model while the underlying target category is legally insufficient, the environment changes, or anticipated military advantage and incidental civilian harm evolve.

Technical accuracy must therefore be assessed in the context of the legal and operational purpose for which force is employed. Predictability concerns the effects and circumstances of use, not simply whether a classifier usually labels an input correctly.

## 31 — The Operating Environment as a Legal Variable

The same autonomous system can create different control problems in different environments. A constrained system operating against a narrowly defined category of military object in a controlled environment presents different circumstances from a system operating for long periods in a densely populated and dynamic area.

The ICRC identifies operating environment, target type, geographic space and time as relevant to human control. Brazil's contextual approach likewise rejects determining legality solely from technical identity.

## 32 — Urban Warfare and Civilian Density

Urban environments can involve dense civilian presence, rapidly changing circumstances, complex structures and ambiguous objects. These characteristics can increase the demand for context-sensitive judgment and make broad autonomous target profiles more difficult to constrain.

Brazil should examine whether validated performance, target restrictions, duration and intervention mechanisms remain adequate in the particular urban environment. A control regime acceptable in an unpopulated area cannot automatically be transplanted into a densely populated city.

## 33 — Dynamic Battlefields

Battlefields change. People move, objects change status, units reposition, communications conditions fluctuate and previously lawful targets can become unlawful to attack. Meaningful control must therefore accommodate temporal change rather than treating authorization as permanently valid.

If circumstances underlying a human decision materially change, the human connection to the force decision may need to be renewed rather than presumed to continue indefinitely.

## 34 — Geographic Restrictions

Geographic boundaries can constrain the circumstances in which autonomous functions operate. Limiting a system to a defined area reduces the range of environmental and target conditions that the authorizing human must anticipate.

The ICRC includes geographical scope among practical restrictions that can contribute to human control. Such restrictions are particularly important where civilian presence, infrastructure and target characteristics vary significantly across locations.

## 35 — Temporal Restrictions

Duration matters because a system operating autonomously for seconds under tightly defined conditions presents a different control problem from a system permitted to operate for hours or days while circumstances evolve. Longer operation increases opportunities for the environment to diverge from conditions considered during authorization.

The ICRC explicitly identifies duration as a relevant operational constraint. Brazil can therefore treat time limits as one component of the human-control architecture.

## 36 — Target-Type Restrictions

Target restrictions narrow the range of objects the system is permitted to engage. They can reduce uncertainty and make human authorization more meaningful by establishing a constrained target envelope.

The ICRC recommends limits on target types for non-prohibited autonomous weapons, including restrictions to objects that are military objectives by nature in appropriate circumstances. Brazil can use this to distinguish tightly bounded autonomy from generalized target-selection authority.

## 37 — Scale of Autonomous Employment

Scale affects supervision. The relevant question is not simply whether an operator can technically monitor a system, but whether the operator can meaningfully process and act upon information from the number of systems operating simultaneously.

A deployment involving many autonomous platforms can multiply decisions, alerts and possible interventions. Human control must therefore account for cognitive workload and operational saturation. The 2026 CCW discussion's attention to training, interfaces and automation bias is relevant to this problem.

## 38 — Communications Failure and Degraded Conditions

Autonomous systems may encounter degraded communications, sensor limitations or other disruptions. A framework based on continuous human intervention must confront what happens when the connection needed for intervention is unavailable.

Possible responses include predefined restrictions, fail-safe behaviour, limited autonomy, automatic termination or other controls appropriate to the system. Loss of communication should not silently transform supervised autonomy into unrestricted autonomy without an authorized legal and operational basis.

## 39 — Changing Mission Parameters

A mission can change after deployment. The target set may expand, the area may change, duration may be extended, or the operational objective may be altered. Such changes can invalidate assumptions underlying initial human judgment.

Brazil should distinguish minor changes within a validated envelope from material changes that alter the nature of deployment. The latter should trigger reassessment rather than allowing an old authorization to function as unlimited legal permission.

## 40 — Reassessment During Deployment

Reassessment is the logical consequence of contextual human control. If material circumstances change, the responsible human authority should reconsider whether the system remains predictable, constrained and appropriate for continued use.

This does not require a human to manually approve every trivial machine action. It requires a clear threshold for when factual circumstances have changed enough that the original judgment can no longer reasonably be treated as controlling.

```text
INITIAL AUTHORIZATION → VALIDATED CONDITIONS → AUTONOMOUS OPERATION
                                                   ↓
                                           MATERIAL CHANGE?
                                           ↙             ↘
                                         NO              YES
                                         ↓                ↓
                                      CONTINUE         REASSESS
                                                          ↓
                                              CONDITIONS STILL VALID?
                                                ↙               ↘
                                              YES               NO
                                               ↓                 ↓

```

## 41 — Article 36 Weapons Reviews and LAWS

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires States party to determine, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, whether its employment would in some or all circumstances be prohibited by applicable international law. Legal review is therefore an important mechanism for examining autonomous systems before operational adoption.

For autonomous weapons, review faces distinctive questions: how predictable is the system, under what circumstances will it be used, what constraints apply, and can the State establish compliance with IHL in anticipated circumstances? The ICRC has emphasized that national legal reviews complement international work on autonomous weapons.

## 42 — Distinction and Autonomous Targeting

Distinction requires parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants and between civilian objects and military objectives. Autonomous target selection creates a particular challenge where target status depends on contextual information that can change over time.

Brazil's 2024 submission places distinction at the centre of assessing autonomous weapons and states that absence of meaningful human control would render a system inherently incompatible with IHL. The question is therefore not simply whether a system can classify objects, but whether its use preserves the human judgment necessary for lawful targeting in the circumstances concerned.

## 43 — Proportionality and Contextual Judgment

Proportionality involves a contextual assessment of anticipated incidental civilian harm in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. This is not reducible to a static machine accuracy score. The information available, expected harm, military objective and surrounding circumstances all matter.

Brazil should therefore be cautious about claims that an autonomous system can independently replace human proportionality judgment merely because it has been trained on prior examples. Human control must remain connected to the contextual legal judgment required at the time of attack.

## 44 — Precautions in Attack

The obligation to take feasible precautions is relevant to autonomous weapon employment because precautions can be built into planning, target restrictions, timing, geographic limits, verification processes and intervention mechanisms. Brazil's 2024 submission emphasizes constant care to minimize civilian harm.

This reinforces that meaningful control is not confined to the instant of engagement. Precautionary measures can operate throughout the deployment chain, from selection and planning through authorization and operation.

## 45 — Unlawful or Unpredictable Effects

Where effects of an autonomous weapon cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained, the ability of humans to exercise meaningful judgment becomes substantially impaired. The ICRC has advocated prohibiting autonomous weapons whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained.

Brazil can converge with this principle while retaining its own emphasis on meaningful human control and context. The central issue is whether the human decision-maker has a sufficient basis to anticipate consequences of the intended use of force and constrain the system accordingly.

## 46 — Existing IHL and New International Rules

Existing IHL applies to autonomous weapon systems, but the existence of general legal rules does not necessarily answer every autonomy-specific operational question. States continue to debate how to translate distinction, proportionality, precautions, accountability and weapons review into practical limits for systems with autonomous critical functions.

Brazil can therefore argue that new international rules should complement existing IHL rather than replace it. The purpose is to clarify how meaningful human control and operational limits can preserve compliance where autonomy creates distinctive risks.

## 47 — Brazil's Two-Tier Approach

Brazil has advocated a two-tier structure in the CCW debate: systems that cannot satisfy applicable international law and meaningful human control should be prohibited, while other permissible systems should be subject to regulation and appropriate limits. This avoids treating all autonomy as identical while establishing a clear floor for unacceptable systems.

The ICRC similarly advocates prohibition of certain unpredictable systems and strict regulation of others, although the precise scope differs. Brazil's framework should therefore distinguish autonomy that cannot be reconciled with legal and humanitarian requirements from autonomy that can operate only within rigorous constraints.

## 48 — A Brazil Test for Lawful Meaningful Control

Brazil's framework can be synthesized through five questions. **Understand:** does the responsible human know relevant capabilities and limitations? **Anticipate:** can system behaviour and effects be reasonably predicted in intended circumstances? **Constrain:** are targets, geography, duration and other parameters sufficiently bounded? **Intervene:** can a human act in time when circumstances require it? **Reassess:** is continued authorization reconsidered when material circumstances change?

These questions are cumulative. A system can be technically reliable yet operationally inappropriate if the environment changes; a human can have a theoretical veto yet lack meaningful time to use it; and a weapon can be carefully authorized yet become unsuitable after a material change.

## 49 — Counterarguments and Rebuttals

One common argument is that existing IHL is sufficient because humans remain responsible for weapons. Brazil can respond that the question is not whether IHL applies, but whether practical conditions of autonomous employment preserve the human judgment needed to comply with it. Another argument is that technology will improve. Brazil can acknowledge technological development while insisting that future capability cannot substitute for present legal safeguards.

A further argument is that national militaries should retain discretion to determine appropriate human control. Brazil can recognize operational diversity while asking whether an international framework can function if core thresholds are so divergent that the same level of autonomy is treated as controlled by one State and uncontrolled by another. The 2026 CCW discussion reflects this tension between common measures and flexibility for national practice.

## 50 — Brazil's Proposed International Framework

Brazil's framework begins with a simple premise: autonomy may assist military operations, but it must not displace the human judgment required for lawful application of force. The international framework should address meaningful human control across the lifecycle rather than treating deployment as a single moment. Design, testing, legal review, authorization, operational restrictions, supervision, intervention, reassessment and post-operation traceability should form a coherent chain.

At the substantive level, Brazil supports prohibiting systems whose operation cannot be reconciled with applicable international law and meaningful human control, while regulating permissible systems through operational constraints appropriate to their functions and context. Such a framework should preserve distinction, proportionality, precautions and accountability while preventing autonomy from becoming an excuse for uncertainty about who made the critical decision.

```text
DESIGN → TEST → REVIEW → AUTHORIZE → DEPLOY
                                      ↓
                         TARGET / ENVIRONMENT / TIME
                                      ↓
                             HUMAN SUPERVISION
                                      ↓
                         INTERVENTION / REASSESSMENT
                                      ↓
                              USE OF FORCE
                                      ↓
                       TRACEABILITY / INVESTIGATION
```

## Source discipline

This dossier distinguishes **documented State positions, established legal rules, technical evidence, institutional analysis and Brazil's advocacy position**. Contested interpretations should not be presented as settled law. Technical claims should be tied to the particular system, environment and evidence available. The central Brazilian argument is that meaningful human control must be substantive, contextual and maintained across the deployment chain rather than reduced to a formal authorization step.
