# SUBSTANTIVE II — PROSE RESEARCH EDITION
## Meaningful Human Control: From Authorization to Operational Reality

**Committee:** CCW — LAWS  
**Country:** Federative Republic of Brazil  
**Agenda:** Deliberation on human control and accountability across the LAWS deployment chain

> **Core proposition:** Meaningful human control cannot be reduced to the existence of a human somewhere in a command structure. It concerns whether human judgment remains substantively connected to the circumstances, parameters and consequences of the use of force.

---

# HOW TO USE THIS DOSSIER

This edition is intentionally prose-heavy. It is meant to sit beside the diagrams and POI material rather than replace them. Each section develops an argument that can be lifted into a GSL, moderated caucus speech, substantive chit, POI, drafting session or rebuttal. The sections deliberately avoid treating “autonomy” as one single technological category. The relevant question throughout is what function has been delegated, under what circumstances, with what information, within what limits, and with what capacity for human intervention.

The current CCW discussions are particularly useful because the 2026 working paper records broad support for the proposition that responsibility and accountability cannot be transferred to machines, while also discussing a responsible human chain of command and control, understandable interfaces, guidance, training, traceability and investigation. The 2025 work further examines control mechanisms, target, duration and geographical limits, human roles and lifecycle information. These materials provide a strong factual foundation for Brazil's argument while leaving room for genuine debate over how demanding the international standard should be.

# PART I — CONCEPTUAL ARCHITECTURE

## 01 — Meaningful Human Control Is a Functional Standard

Meaningful human control should be assessed by what a human can actually understand, decide and change, rather than by whether a formal human role exists. A commander's name on an authorization document is not itself evidence that the commander retained meaningful control over a later autonomous engagement. Likewise, a person sitting at a console is not necessarily exercising meaningful judgment if the system acts faster than the person can assess, reject or interrupt its outputs.

Brazil should therefore treat meaningful control as a functional standard. The assessment should examine the system's capabilities, the human operator's knowledge, the operational environment, the authorized target set, the duration and geographical scope of operation, and the practical ability to intervene. This approach is compatible with the CCW discussion of human judgment and control as dependent on circumstances rather than labels. It also avoids an artificial binary between “human” and “machine”: the question is precisely where decision-making is distributed and whether that distribution remains compatible with IHL.

**Brazilian takeaway:** a human presence is a fact; meaningful human control is a condition that must be demonstrated.

## 02 — Human Agency and the Application of Force

IHL regulates the conduct of hostilities through rules applied by human actors. The difficult question raised by LAWS is therefore not whether machines can assist with navigation, sensing or other functions, but how far decision-making about the application of force can be delegated while the human connection to the legal decision remains meaningful. The ICRC has emphasized that obligations governing attacks cannot simply be transferred to a machine.

Brazil should use this carefully. It does not mean that every automated function is impermissible. Rather, it means that autonomy cannot be treated as a legal vacuum. Where an autonomous function materially determines which object is attacked, when it is attacked or under what circumstances force is applied, the State must be able to identify the human judgment that remains decisive and the safeguards connecting that judgment to the result.

**Pressure point:** if a machine makes the final choice between several potential targets, ask the other delegation to identify the human judgment that remained legally decisive.

## 03 — Authorization Is Only the Beginning

Authorization is an important human decision, but it is not automatically equivalent to continuing control. Authorization normally occurs before deployment. Autonomous operation may continue after the human decision has been made, potentially while the environment changes. If the original authorization depended on assumptions about the target, geography, duration, civilian presence or system behaviour, those assumptions may cease to hold.

Brazil can therefore distinguish between **initial authorization** and **continuing control**. This does not mean that a human must manually approve every machine movement. It means that authorization should be bounded by operational parameters and accompanied by mechanisms for monitoring and reassessment. A framework that treats one signature as sufficient regardless of subsequent changes risks converting human judgment into a ceremonial event rather than a continuing constraint on lethal autonomy.

**POI:** “What material change in the operating circumstances would require your human decision-maker to reassess the original authorization?”

## 04 — The Decision-Making Nexus

A useful Brazilian concept is the **decision-making nexus**: the substantive connection between human judgment, operational circumstances and the application of force. The nexus is strong where a human understands the system, knows the environment, defines the mission and limits, can monitor relevant behaviour and can intervene or terminate employment when required. It becomes weaker when a person authorizes a broad mission but the system independently determines the targets and circumstances of engagement.

This concept also avoids the trap of treating all autonomy as equivalent. A system that automatically follows a human-selected object is not performing the same function as one that independently searches for and selects targets from a generalized profile. Brazil should therefore insist on function-specific analysis. The closer autonomous decision-making comes to the critical application of force, the more demanding the assessment of human judgment and control should become.

## 05 — Identification, Selection and Engagement

Identification, selection and engagement provide a useful analytical sequence. Identification concerns what the system believes it has detected; selection concerns which object or person it chooses for engagement; engagement concerns the actual application of force. These stages are not interchangeable. A human may identify a target while a system tracks it automatically, or a system may independently select among multiple objects.

The legal significance depends on what has been delegated. If the system's autonomous function determines a fact that directly governs the use of force, the human-control analysis must examine that function specifically. Brazil can therefore ask not simply “Is there a human in the loop?” but “At which critical function does the human exercise judgment?” This makes the debate considerably more precise.

## 06 — Human-in-the-Loop Is Not a Magic Phrase

“Human-in-the-loop” is an architectural description, not a guarantee of meaningful control. A person can technically occupy the loop while lacking sufficient time, information or practical authority to influence the engagement. Conversely, a system without continuous manual handling may still be subject to substantial human control through carefully bounded mission parameters, appropriate operational restrictions and effective intervention mechanisms.

Brazil should therefore avoid making terminology the centre of the debate. The relevant evidence is practical: what information does the operator receive, how quickly does the system act, how many engagements are occurring, what can the operator change, and what happens when the operator disagrees with the system? The 2025 CCW discussion explicitly recognized that direct continuous manual oversight is not the only possible form of human control, which makes a functional approach especially important.

## 07 — Nominal Oversight Versus Effective Oversight

Nominal oversight exists when formal authority exists but cannot realistically be exercised. Effective oversight requires the human to possess relevant information, sufficient time, operational authority and technical means to influence the system. This distinction becomes more important as the number and speed of autonomous engagements increase.

The 2026 CCW working paper highlights understandable human-machine interfaces, guidance, training and risks such as automation bias. These are not peripheral administrative matters. If a human is overloaded, receives confusing information or systematically defers to machine recommendations, the formal presence of the human may not correspond to meaningful judgment. Brazil should therefore ask delegations to demonstrate operational effectiveness rather than merely describe organizational charts.

## 08 — Human Judgment Under IHL

Distinction, proportionality and precautions require application of legal rules to the circumstances of an attack. A sensor can identify a shape or pattern, but legal judgment may require consideration of the target's status, surrounding civilians, available alternatives and expected effects. Machines may assist these processes, but Brazil should be cautious about treating technical classification as identical to legal judgment.

This does not establish that machines are incapable of assisting lawful warfare. It establishes a more precise proposition: where autonomous functions materially affect the application of force, the human-control architecture must preserve the ability of responsible human actors to apply the relevant legal standards. The ICRC has repeatedly emphasized the importance of human understanding, supervision, intervention and operational constraints in connecting human decisions to the outcome of an attack.

## 09 — Meaningful Control as a Continuing Condition

Meaningful control should be understood across the weapon's relevant lifecycle. The 2026 CCW working paper explicitly states that responsibility and accountability should be considered across the entire lifecycle and discusses roles in development, deployment and use. This is important because a system's legal and operational characteristics are not fixed at the moment of procurement.

Software may be updated. Mission parameters may change. Operators may change. Training may become outdated. The operational environment may differ from the one used for validation. Consequently, the question is not simply whether a system was controllable when first approved. It is whether the relevant human-control conditions continue to exist when force is actually employed.

## 10 — Brazil's Five-Capacity Test

Brazil can organize the concept into five capacities: **understand, anticipate, constrain, intervene and reassess**. Understanding concerns capabilities and limitations. Anticipation concerns expected behaviour within the intended operational envelope. Constraint concerns mission, target, geographical and temporal limits. Intervention concerns the realistic ability to alter, suspend or terminate employment. Reassessment concerns responding to material changes.

This should be presented as an analytical framework, not as a replacement for IHL or a universal numerical test. Its value is that it turns an abstract phrase into questions that can be answered with evidence. If a delegation claims meaningful control, Brazil can ask what evidence demonstrates each capacity. If a State can only point to initial authorization, the gap between formal oversight and substantive control becomes apparent.

# PART II — THE DEPLOYMENT CHAIN

## 11 — Research, Development and Design

Human control is influenced by design choices made before deployment. Design determines what the system can perceive, what parameters can be imposed, what information is available to the operator and whether intervention mechanisms actually function. A restriction that cannot be technically enforced may exist in doctrine without functioning as an operational control.

Brazil should therefore resist the idea that legal safeguards begin only when a weapon reaches a commander. Development decisions can determine whether later safeguards are credible. Questions about sensor limitations, failure modes, intervention architecture, logging and system updates are relevant because they affect the human ability to understand and control the weapon during later stages.

## 12 — Training Data and System Behaviour

Machine-learning systems may depend on data and models whose limitations affect performance. Brazil should avoid the unsupported claim that a particular dataset automatically makes a weapon unlawful. The stronger argument is that data and model limitations can affect predictability, robustness and the operator's ability to understand where performance may degrade.

The practical issue is whether the system was tested against representative conditions and whether operators were informed of relevant limitations. If a system is validated on a narrow range of circumstances but deployed into a materially different environment, the human decision-maker may have less basis for anticipating its behaviour. That is a control problem even before it becomes a question of legal responsibility.

## 13 — Testing and Validation

Testing should establish more than ideal-case performance. Validation asks whether the tested conditions meaningfully correspond to the intended circumstances of use. The ICRC has identified predictability and reliability as practical challenges for legal review of autonomous weapons, including the difficulty of assessing how a system will perform in intended or expected circumstances.

Brazil should distinguish three questions: **Did the system perform under the test conditions? Are the test conditions representative of the intended operation? Does the operator know when the system is outside that validated envelope?** A high laboratory success rate does not by itself answer the third question. Operational assurance therefore depends on both technical evidence and human understanding.

## 14 — Operational Authorization

A meaningful authorization should specify enough about the mission for the human decision to remain connected to later machine behaviour. Relevant parameters can include the task, target category, geographical area, duration, environmental conditions, scale and intervention mechanisms. The CCW discussion has specifically examined limits on targets, duration and geographical scope.

The point is not bureaucratic complexity for its own sake. Specific parameters narrow the circumstances in which autonomous force can be applied and make later assessment more intelligible. If an authorization is so broad that almost any machine behaviour falls within it, the human decision may cease to function as a meaningful constraint.

## 15 — Rules of Engagement as a Control Architecture

Rules of engagement can help translate legal and operational judgments into constraints, but their effectiveness depends on how they interact with the system. A restriction written in an order is not necessarily equivalent to a restriction the system can technically enforce. Brazil should therefore ask where a stated limitation is implemented and how compliance with it can be verified.

The 2026 CCW material discusses guidance, instructions and training for personnel, while the 2025 material examines control mechanisms and operational parameters. This supports a layered approach: doctrine provides the rule, mission planning specifies the parameters, technical architecture helps enforce them, and human supervision provides a further safeguard. No single layer should be assumed sufficient in isolation.

## 16 — Target Selection

Target selection is one of the clearest points at which human control can become legally significant. If a human identifies the specific military objective and an autonomous system merely executes within a narrowly defined task, the human decision may remain comparatively direct. If the system independently determines which objects satisfy a generalized target profile, the distribution of judgment is different.

Brazil should therefore ask what information defines the target and who decides that the conditions for engagement have been met. This question is more useful than simply asking whether the system is autonomous. It directs the debate to the precise function through which lethal force becomes possible.

## 17 — Engagement Decisions

The engagement stage is where earlier judgments culminate in the application of force. Human control at this stage may involve direct approval, meaningful supervision, pre-established constraints, intervention mechanisms or combinations of these measures. The adequacy of any mechanism depends on the speed and nature of the system.

A theoretical ability to intervene is insufficient if the engagement occurs faster than the human can reasonably respond. Conversely, an autonomous system operating slowly within a narrow and predictable environment may allow more meaningful supervisory control. Brazil should therefore connect intervention to time, information and operational context rather than treating it as a checkbox.

## 18 — Human Supervision During Operation

Supervision is meaningful only if the human can observe the relevant aspects of operation and understand what the system is doing. The quality of the interface matters because excessive information can overwhelm an operator while insufficient information can conceal important changes.

The 2026 CCW discussions refer to understandable human-machine interfaces and training. Brazil can use this to move the debate beyond the simplistic question of whether a person is “watching.” The real question is whether the person is capable of recognizing a situation that requires intervention and acting before force is applied.

## 19 — Intervention and Deactivation

Intervention is one of the most practical elements of human control. The ability to interrupt, modify or terminate an autonomous operation can provide a meaningful safeguard, but only if the mechanism is reliable, timely and accessible under expected conditions.

The ICRC has identified human supervision and the ability to intervene or deactivate as central elements of control. Brazil should ask three questions: **Can the human intervene? Can the human intervene in time? Can the human know when intervention is necessary?** A “kill switch” that exists technically but cannot be used quickly or intelligently in the operational environment may offer less protection than its existence suggests.

## 20 — Post-Strike Review and Feedback

Post-operation review cannot replace control during an attack, but it remains important for learning, investigation and future deployment decisions. Logs, records and system outputs can help reconstruct what occurred and whether human instructions were followed.

The 2026 CCW paper calls for effective internal mechanisms for investigation, reporting and appropriate action following incidents that may involve IHL violations. Brazil can therefore distinguish **ex ante control**, **operational control** and **ex post investigation**. The latter cannot retroactively make an uncontrolled decision controlled, but it can prevent failures from disappearing into an evidentiary void.

# PART III — PREDICTABILITY, RELIABILITY AND UNDERSTANDING

## 21 — Predictability as a Condition of Control

Control presupposes some ability to anticipate what the system will do. If the user cannot reasonably predict the system's behaviour within the circumstances of intended use, the human decision may become disconnected from the resulting use of force. The ICRC has repeatedly linked control to predictability and reliability.

Brazil should avoid demanding mathematical certainty. The stronger formulation is contextual: what degree of predictability is necessary for the task, target, environment and duration in question? A system may be sufficiently predictable for a constrained function in a controlled environment while being unsuitable for a dynamic setting involving civilians and rapidly changing conditions.

## 22 — Reliability Versus Predictability

Reliability and predictability are related but distinct. Reliability asks whether the system performs consistently according to its intended function. Predictability asks whether users can reasonably anticipate how it will behave in a given situation. A system could be highly reliable in repeated laboratory conditions yet difficult to predict when exposed to unfamiliar inputs.

This distinction gives Brazil a sophisticated answer to the argument that “our system is 99% accurate.” Accuracy alone does not identify the circumstances in which the remaining errors occur, how severe their consequences are, or whether operators can recognize the system's uncertainty. The legally relevant question is not merely performance on average but whether the human decision-maker can rely on the system within the authorized circumstances.

## 23 — Explainability and Legal Judgment

Explainability matters because human decision-makers may need to understand why a system reached a particular output, especially when the output contributes to a lethal decision. The degree of explanation required will vary by system and function, but an opaque output can make it harder to detect malfunction, unexpected behaviour or a mismatch between the system's assumptions and the operational environment.

Brazil should not claim that every algorithm must be mathematically transparent. Instead, ask what level of information the human needs to exercise lawful judgment. If the operator cannot understand relevant capabilities, limitations and triggers, the ability to supervise may be diminished even if the system's average performance appears strong.

## 24 — The Black-Box Problem

Some machine-learning systems can be difficult for humans to predict, understand, explain and test. The ICRC has specifically discussed this concern in relation to machine-learning controlled autonomous weapons. The problem is not simply philosophical opacity. It can become operational when an unexpected classification leads to an unexpected engagement.

Brazil should therefore connect explainability to practical control. If the human cannot determine why the system is behaving differently from expected, can the human recognize that a restriction has become necessary? If the answer is no, intervention becomes more difficult. The issue is consequently not whether every internal computation is visible, but whether the human possesses enough information to exercise meaningful judgment.

## 25 — Uncertainty and Confidence Thresholds

Autonomous systems often operate through classifications, probabilities or confidence measures. These technical quantities should not be casually equated with legal thresholds. A high model confidence score does not itself establish that an object is a lawful target, nor does a low score automatically establish that an attack is prohibited.

The human decision-maker must understand what the system's confidence means, what its limitations are and how uncertainty interacts with the operational environment. Brazil can use this distinction to challenge proposals that rely on a single numerical threshold as though lawfulness could be reduced to a percentage. The relevant question is how technical uncertainty is incorporated into human judgment and precautions.

## 26 — Distribution Shift and Changing Environments

A system validated under one distribution of inputs may behave differently when the environment changes. In warfare, this can occur through differences in geography, weather, lighting, civilian patterns, camouflage, infrastructure, target behaviour or conflict intensity.

Brazil should therefore treat environmental change as a potential trigger for reassessment. The 2025 CCW discussion explicitly notes that variables such as environment and targets may change over time and that the operational environment and intensity of conflict can influence authorization. This provides a concrete basis for arguing that human control must remain sensitive to circumstances rather than fixed permanently at procurement.

## 27 — Adaptive and Learning Systems

Systems that adapt during operation raise a particular control question: if the system's behaviour changes, does the original human understanding remain adequate? Not every autonomous system learns during use, and Brazil should not conflate all autonomy with online learning. Where adaptive behaviour does occur, however, it may complicate validation and prediction.

The key issue is governance of change. What changes are permitted without renewed testing? Which changes trigger reassessment? What records are retained? Who is informed? These questions connect technical adaptation to human control without assuming that adaptation is inherently unlawful.

## 28 — Multiple-System Interaction and Swarming

Interaction among multiple autonomous systems can make behaviour more difficult to anticipate. Even if each system is individually predictable, the combined system may produce emergent interactions that increase complexity. The ICRC has identified increased interaction among multiple systems, including swarms, as a factor that may affect predictability.

Brazil should therefore ask whether the validation unit is the individual weapon or the operational network. If several systems share information or influence one another's behaviour, human supervision must account for the combined architecture. A human who understands each component separately may still lack sufficient understanding of the collective system.

## 29 — Failure Modes and Unexpected Behaviour

Every system has limitations. The relevant question is whether those limitations are understood and whether safeguards exist for circumstances outside expected conditions. A meaningful control framework should identify foreseeable failure modes and specify what happens when they occur.

Brazil can distinguish between a known failure mode that triggers suspension and an unanticipated behaviour that leaves operators without a clear response. Testing, training, logging and operational constraints can reduce the risk of both, but none should be treated as infallible. The objective is to prevent unexpected behaviour from becoming an excuse for abandoning human responsibility.

## 30 — Predictability of Effects, Not Merely Accuracy

A system can be accurate at classification while its overall effects remain difficult to predict. For example, identifying an object correctly does not necessarily establish what secondary effects will follow an engagement, particularly in complex environments.

Brazil should therefore widen the inquiry from model accuracy to **predictability of effects**. What will happen if the target moves? What if civilians enter the area? What if the system encounters a new object? What if the mission continues longer than expected? These are operational questions that determine whether the human authorization remains connected to the eventual consequences.

# PART IV — THE OPERATIONAL ENVIRONMENT

## 31 — The Operating Environment as a Legal Variable

The same autonomous system can present different control problems in different environments. A narrowly constrained system operating against a predictable object in an unpopulated area may pose different issues from a system operating in a dense urban environment with civilians, protected objects and rapidly changing information.

The 2025 CCW material identifies environmental conditions, civilian presence, protected persons and objects, information quality and available means and methods as relevant factors. Brazil should therefore reject claims that a weapon is simply “lawful” or “unlawful” without considering the circumstances of employment where the legal analysis depends on use.

## 32 — Urban Warfare and Civilian Density

Urban environments can compress the time and space available for distinction, precautions and intervention. Civilians and military objectives may be physically close, information can change rapidly and the consequences of an incorrect classification can be severe.

Brazil does not need to claim that autonomous systems can never be used in cities. The more defensible position is that the density and dynamism of the environment can increase the demands placed on human judgment and operational constraints. The relevant question is whether the system and its human-control architecture remain capable of supporting lawful decisions under those circumstances.

## 33 — Dynamic Battlefields

A dynamic battlefield can alter the factual basis on which an authorization was made. A civilian object may become occupied by civilians, a military objective may move, a protected object may enter the area, or the tactical situation may change the expected military advantage.

This is why Brazil emphasizes reassessment. A system that remains within a technical mission envelope may nevertheless move outside the factual assumptions that supported the human decision. Human control must therefore include mechanisms for recognizing material changes and reconsidering continued operation.

## 34 — Geographic Restrictions

Geographical limits can reduce the range of circumstances in which an autonomous system may operate. Restricting a system to a defined area can narrow the possible target set and make validation more meaningful. The ICRC has repeatedly identified geographical scope as a practical element of human control.

Brazil should not present geographical restriction as sufficient by itself. A small area can still contain civilians, protected objects and changing conditions. The stronger argument is that geographic limits can function as one layer in a broader control architecture that includes target, time, environment and intervention constraints.

## 35 — Temporal Restrictions

Duration matters because longer autonomous operation creates more opportunities for circumstances to change. A system that is predictable over a short window may become less predictable as the environment evolves.

Temporal restrictions can therefore help preserve the connection between the original human decision and the later use of force. Brazil should ask whether the authorization specifies how long autonomous operation may continue and what events require renewal. The question is not whether every operation must be short, but whether duration is considered as part of control rather than ignored.

## 36 — Target-Type Restrictions

Target restrictions can narrow the system's discretion and reduce the range of possible engagements. The ICRC has identified restrictions on target types as one practical means of limiting autonomy. Such restrictions are particularly relevant where certain objects are easier to distinguish reliably than others.

Brazil should emphasize that target restrictions must correspond to actual system capabilities. If a system cannot reliably distinguish the permitted target category in the intended environment, a formal restriction may not solve the underlying problem. The human-control question therefore connects the target rule with technical validation and operational context.

## 37 — Scale of Autonomous Employment

Scale can affect the practical meaning of human supervision. One operator supervising a small number of systems may have a different capacity from a command structure overseeing a large number of simultaneous engagements. More systems can mean more alerts, more information and more potential interventions.

Brazil should therefore ask whether the human-control architecture scales with the number of autonomous functions being used. A supervision model that is credible at small scale may become nominal at large scale if the operator cannot meaningfully monitor or intervene across the entire operation.

## 38 — Communications Failure and Degraded Conditions

Autonomous systems may be designed to continue operating when communications are degraded or unavailable. This can provide military resilience, but it raises a human-control question: what happens when the human loses the ability to communicate new instructions or terminate the mission?

Brazil should not claim that loss of communications automatically makes autonomy unlawful. The relevant question is whether the system's autonomous behaviour under degraded conditions remains within the parameters that were anticipated, validated and authorized. If not, the system may need a safe behaviour, automatic termination condition or other safeguard.

## 39 — Changing Mission Parameters

A mission can change after deployment. The target set can expand, the operating area can shift, the duration can be extended or the intensity of the conflict can change. Each change can affect the assumptions behind the original authorization.

Brazil should therefore distinguish between changes that are already contemplated within the validated mission and **material changes** that alter the circumstances in which the system was assessed. The latter should trigger renewed human consideration. This is a practical way to prevent an authorization from becoming a blank cheque.

## 40 — Reassessment During Deployment

Reassessment is the bridge between static authorization and dynamic warfare. A meaningful control framework should identify the circumstances that trigger it. Examples may include material changes in civilian presence, target characteristics, geographical scope, duration, system performance or operating conditions.

Brazil can argue that reassessment does not necessarily mean stopping an operation every time something changes. It means establishing thresholds for determining when the original human judgment no longer adequately describes the operation. This preserves operational flexibility while preventing human control from becoming permanently frozen at the moment of launch.

# PART V — IHL, LEGAL REVIEW AND BRAZIL'S FRAMEWORK

## 41 — Article 36 Weapons Reviews

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a State Party, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would be prohibited by applicable international law in some or all circumstances. Brazil should treat weapons review as a foundational mechanism rather than a complete answer to operational control.

A review conducted at development or acquisition cannot automatically resolve every question arising from a later mission. Autonomous systems create particular challenges because the legality of use can depend on system behaviour and circumstances. The ICRC has highlighted the practical difficulty of assessing predictability and reliability in legal review. Brazil can therefore argue for strong review processes coupled with operational safeguards and reassessment.

## 42 — Distinction and Autonomous Targeting

The principle of distinction requires parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants and between civilian objects and military objectives, and to direct operations accordingly. Where autonomous targeting is involved, the central question is whether the system can perform its assigned function with sufficient reliability and within the relevant circumstances.

Brazil should avoid claiming that a machine can never assist with distinction. Instead, the argument should focus on the circumstances in which autonomy is deployed. The more complex the environment and the more consequential the autonomous targeting function, the more demanding the requirements for understanding, predictability and human control become.

## 43 — Proportionality and Contextual Judgment

Proportionality requires an assessment of expected incidental civilian harm against the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage. This is contextual. The factual circumstances surrounding an attack can change, and the availability of alternative means can matter.

Brazil should therefore ask how a system's autonomous function interacts with the human proportionality assessment. If the human makes the assessment before deployment but the circumstances materially change before engagement, the original judgment may no longer be adequate. This reinforces the case for operational parameters, supervision and reassessment.

## 44 — Precautions in Attack

The obligation to take feasible precautions requires attention to the circumstances known at the relevant time. Autonomous systems may assist in collecting and processing information, but their use does not eliminate the human obligation to take feasible precautions.

Brazil can therefore frame meaningful control as part of precaution rather than as an entirely separate concept. If a commander knows that a system is unreliable in a particular environment, continuing to employ it without appropriate restrictions may raise questions that cannot be answered simply by saying that the system was authorized. The control architecture should help the human decision-maker implement precautions in practice.

## 45 — Unlawful or Unpredictable Effects

Unpredictability is particularly important when it prevents the user from understanding or anticipating the effects of an autonomous system. The ICRC has argued that autonomous weapons whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained should be expressly ruled out.

Brazil can draw on this reasoning without pretending that the ICRC's position is identical to every State's negotiating position. The useful point for debate is that predictability has a legal function: it helps determine whether human authorization can remain connected to the consequences of use. Where effects are inherently outside meaningful anticipation, the case for permitting autonomy becomes substantially harder to defend.

## 46 — Existing IHL and the Need for Additional Rules

Some States emphasize that existing IHL already applies to new weapons and that responsibility remains with humans. This is an important proposition, but it does not by itself answer every question about how meaningful human control should operate in practice.

Brazil can acknowledge that existing IHL remains applicable while arguing that the CCW can develop additional rules, standards or practical requirements that clarify how compliance should be secured for autonomous systems. The 2026 GGE process itself reflects continuing work toward common understandings and practical recommendations. Brazil should therefore avoid presenting new rules as a rejection of existing IHL; the stronger argument is that new rules can operationalize and reinforce it.

## 47 — Brazil's Two-Tier Approach

Brazil's two-tier approach should distinguish systems whose operation cannot satisfy applicable international law and meaningful human judgment from systems that can be used only under stringent safeguards. The first category requires prohibition; the second requires binding regulation and operational limits.

The purpose of the framework is not to prohibit autonomy merely because technology is new. It is to prevent systems from operating beyond the conditions in which human judgment, IHL compliance and accountability can be maintained. The framework should therefore be linked to concrete factors such as predictability, target restrictions, operational environment, duration, scale and intervention.

## 48 — A Brazil Test for Lawful Meaningful Control

Brazil's analytical test can be expressed through five questions. **Understand:** does the responsible human understand relevant capabilities and limitations? **Anticipate:** can behaviour reasonably be anticipated within the intended circumstances? **Constrain:** can the human impose enforceable limits on task, target, geography, time and scope? **Intervene:** can the human intervene or terminate operation in time? **Reassess:** are there clear triggers for reconsideration when material circumstances change?

This framework should not be presented as a mechanical formula that replaces legal judgment. Its value is evidentiary. It tells a delegation what evidence to produce and what questions an international instrument should address. It also gives Brazil a coherent basis for challenging vague claims that a weapon remains “under human control.”

## 49 — Counterarguments and Rebuttals

**“Existing IHL is enough.”** Brazil can answer that existing law remains applicable, but practical standards can strengthen implementation and clarify control requirements. **“Humans remain responsible.”** Brazil can agree while asking whether the human retained meaningful control over the critical decision. **“A human can deactivate the system.”** Brazil can ask whether intervention is timely, informed and realistic. **“Technology will improve.”** Brazil can respond that future capability does not justify present uncertainty and that standards should be capable of adapting to technological change.

**“Manual control is unnecessary.”** Brazil need not demand constant manual handling. The issue is meaningful judgment and control appropriate to the circumstances. **“National practice should determine the standard.”** Brazil can acknowledge operational diversity while questioning whether an international legal obligation can remain meaningful if its core threshold is entirely variable. These rebuttals keep Brazil rigorous rather than absolutist.

## 50 — Brazil's Proposed International Framework

Brazil should close the dossier by connecting the entire deployment chain. International rules should preserve a responsible human chain of command and control; require sufficient understanding of autonomous capabilities and limitations; establish operational constraints appropriate to the task and environment; provide effective supervision and intervention; require reassessment when material circumstances change; and preserve records sufficient to investigate incidents and establish responsibility.

The final Brazilian proposition is therefore simple but demanding: **autonomy must remain bounded by human judgment that is informed, operationally effective and legally connected to the use of force.** A human signature cannot substitute for control. A machine's autonomy cannot substitute for legal responsibility. And a technical capability cannot by itself establish lawful employment. The CCW's task is to translate these principles into rules that remain credible across the full deployment chain.

## Research method

For conference use, prioritize the original CCW documents and treaty text when making a factual claim about a State's position. Use ICRC material to explain legal and operational concepts, while distinguishing ICRC recommendations from binding treaty law and from positions adopted by States. Avoid turning a working paper or an individual submission into a claim that “the CCW has decided” an issue unless the document actually records agreed language.

# BRAZIL'S RESEARCH CHECKLIST

Before using a claim in committee, ask:

- Is this a treaty rule, State position, ICRC recommendation, expert analysis, or Brazil's own proposal?
- Is the source current enough for the 2026 CCW context?
- Am I accurately representing the delegation I am targeting?
- Can I identify the exact document if challenged?
- Am I confusing technical accuracy with legal compliance?
- Am I confusing formal authorization with continuing control?
- Am I treating “human-in-the-loop” as proof rather than examining what the human can actually do?
- Have I considered task, target, environment, duration, geography and scale?
- Have I considered whether material changes require reassessment?
- Can I explain who remains responsible without claiming that machines themselves bear legal responsibility?
