# 02 — SUBSTANTIVE RESEARCH: MEANINGFUL HUMAN CONTROL & LAWS

**Committee:** CCW — Emerging Technologies in the Area of LAWS  
**Country:** Federative Republic of Brazil  
**Agenda:** Deliberation on human control and accountability across the LAWS deployment chain

> **Purpose of this substantive:** concentrated research ammunition — documented State positions, UN/CCW developments, ICRC findings, legal provisions, operational evidence and Brazil-specific analytical conclusions. This is deliberately distinct from Substantive I on Accountability & Attribution.

---

## 01 — Brazil's 2024 CCW Position

Brazil's own 2024 response to the CCW GGE is the foundation for this substantive. Brazil stated that humans must retain ultimate control and responsibility regarding the use of force in armed conflict. It connected human control with the ability to make ultimate decisions concerning lethal force, sufficient control over the weapons system, and accountability for decisions over life and death. Brazil also made an important qualification: it did not consider it possible or desirable to define one universal technical standard for the amount or form of human control. Instead, the required control depends on the characteristics of the system, the circumstances of deployment and the operational environment.

That position is strategically useful because Brazil can simultaneously reject both extremes: it does not need to claim that every autonomous function is inherently unlawful, but it can insist that the human element must be sufficient to ensure operation consistent with international law. Brazil's 2024 response also discusses the black-box problem and whether commanders can make the contextual assessments required by IHL when the system's algorithmic predictions are not transparent. It identifies weapons reviews as an essential tool and states that a State must be able to answer affirmatively, before deployment, whether the system can be used consistently with IHL.

**Research point:** Brazil already has a documented position tying human control to lawful use, contextual circumstances, predictability and accountability. This is not a newly invented conference argument.  
**Primary source:** CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1, Brazil response. citeturn0search9

## 02 — 2026 CCW: Responsibility Cannot Be Transferred to Machines

The 2026 Chair's summary records broad support for the proposition that States, parties to armed conflict and individuals remain responsible and accountable under applicable international law for decisions involving LAWS, because responsibility and accountability cannot be transferred to machines. It further records that this should be considered across the entire life cycle of a weapon system.

The same 2026 discussion goes beyond a generic statement of responsibility. It refers to a responsible human chain of command and control and records that several delegations emphasized the need for that chain to be **traceable**. One suggestion was the use of data logs and audit trails documenting who issued instructions to the system. The discussion also covers understandable human-machine interfaces, guidance and instructions, training on capabilities and limitations, and automation bias.

This is valuable for Brazil because it demonstrates that the contemporary CCW discussion is increasingly concerned with the practical architecture of human responsibility. If responsibility cannot be transferred to a machine, then States need mechanisms that identify the human decisions, instructions and control points through which responsibility was exercised.

**POI ammunition:** “If the delegate agrees that responsibility cannot be transferred to a machine, what traceable mechanism does their delegation propose for identifying the human decision-maker at each critical stage of deployment?”  
**Primary source:** CCW/GGE.1/2026/WP.2. citeturn0search8

## 03 — The 2025 UNGA Vote: 164 States in Favour

UN General Assembly Resolution 80/57 on lethal autonomous weapons systems was adopted on 1 December 2025 by **164 votes in favour, 6 against and 7 abstentions**. Brazil voted in favour. The resolution affirms that international law, including the UN Charter, international humanitarian law and international human rights law, applies in relation to autonomous weapons systems. It also reaffirms that any weapon, including an autonomous weapon system, that cannot be used in compliance with IHL must not be used.

The resolution additionally recognizes humanitarian, legal, security, technological and ethical challenges and expresses concern about miscalculation, escalation, proliferation and the possible lowering of thresholds for conflict. It is important not to overstate the legal effect of a General Assembly resolution: it is not itself a treaty creating a universally binding LAWS regime. Its importance here is political and evidentiary. It demonstrates substantial international support for continued consideration of the issue and for the proposition that existing international law applies.

**Brazilian use:** cite the vote as evidence of broad international political engagement, but do not present it as proof that 164 States have accepted one identical treaty standard for MHC.  
**Primary source:** A/RES/80/57. citeturn0search0turn0search10

## 04 — The Two-Tier Approach in CCW Negotiations

The 2024 CCW discussions recorded broad support for a two-tier approach: systems inherently incompatible with IHL would be prohibited, while systems capable of being used consistently with IHL would remain subject to regulation. The same discussions show disagreement over the exact formulation of human control and over how to define the criteria for each tier.

The rolling-text discussion also identifies specific possible restrictions: types of targets, duration, geographical scope, scale of operation and number of engagements. Delegations discussed predictability, reliability, traceability and explainability as possible tools for addressing the challenges posed by high degrees of autonomy. Rigorous testing and evaluation were proposed to give human operators a reliable expectation of system performance in anticipated circumstances.

This is significant for Brazil because its two-tier approach has substantive negotiating precedent. The argument should not be “Brazil invented two tiers.” The stronger argument is that the CCW itself has been discussing a distinction between systems that cannot be used consistently with IHL and systems that may be permissible but require restrictions.

**Brazilian research conclusion:** the regulatory tier must contain real operational constraints; otherwise “regulation” becomes a label rather than a safeguard.  
**Primary source:** CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.11. citeturn1search12

## 05 — ICRC: Human Control Is Closely Connected to Predictability

The ICRC identifies several practical elements of effective control over autonomous weapons: predictability and reliability; human supervision and the ability to intervene; and operational restrictions concerning the task, targets, operating environment, geographical space and time of operation. It also identifies human ability to deactivate a system if necessary as relevant.

The importance of this research is that it turns “meaningful human control” into observable variables. A delegate can argue about whether the word “meaningful” is vague, but it is harder to dismiss a concrete question such as: What targets? What geographical area? For how long? Under what environmental conditions? How much supervision? What intervention mechanism?

The ICRC's more recent 2026 position paper warns that trends toward broader target sets, wider operating environments, swarms and AI integration into targeting can erode existing IHL limitations and increase the risk of losing human control and judgment over decisions of life and death.

**Brazilian use:** frame MHC as a relationship between human judgment and operational constraints, not as a slogan about keeping a human somewhere in the command chain.  
**Sources:** ICRC position paper 2026; ICRC statement on autonomous weapons. citeturn0search11turn0search1

## 06 — Accuracy Is Not the Same as Predictability

One of the most important technical distinctions for the committee is between **accuracy, reliability and predictability**. A system can perform with high accuracy on a defined test set without being sufficiently predictable in a dynamic operational environment. Reliability concerns whether a system consistently performs a function; predictability concerns whether the relevant human can reasonably anticipate how the system will behave and what effects it may produce under the circumstances of use.

The 2024 CCW discussions specifically considered predictability and reliability as possible concepts for addressing LAWS risks. The ICRC likewise links effective control to both. This makes it dangerous to accept a statement such as “the system is 99% accurate” as if it answers the legal question. The relevant questions are: accurate for which task, against what target set, under what conditions, over what period, and with what consequences for the remaining cases?

**POI:** “Does the distinguished delegate equate technical accuracy with legal predictability, and if so, what evidence establishes predictable performance in the actual circumstances of deployment?”

## 07 — Operational Restrictions: Target, Time, Geography and Scale

The CCW discussions and ICRC research repeatedly identify operational restrictions as important control mechanisms. These include limiting the type of target, geographical area, duration of operation and scale of autonomous employment. The logic is straightforward: autonomy becomes easier to constrain when the human-defined operational envelope is narrow and clearly specified.

The same principle appears in Russia's 2024 CCW working paper, which lists limitations on target types, duration, geographical scope and scale among possible ways of exercising control. This provides useful common ground even with States that disagree with Brazil on the need for a new legally binding instrument.

For Brazil, the important point is that restrictions are not arbitrary technical preferences. They can be the conditions under which a system's behaviour remains sufficiently predictable and human-controlled for the relevant circumstances. A system operating against a narrowly defined military object in a controlled environment is materially different from a system permitted to search a broad area for hours and autonomously select targets from a generalized profile.

**POI:** “If the delegate accepts operational limits as a form of human control, what prevents those limits from becoming so broad that the human-defined envelope no longer meaningfully constrains the system?”  
**Sources:** CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.11; ICRC; Russian Federation CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.2. citeturn1search12turn1search4

## 08 — Russia: Human Control, But State Discretion

Russia's 2024 CCW working paper is useful precisely because it contains more nuance than a simple “Russia opposes regulation” characterization. Russia states that human control over LAWS is an important constraint and says a control system should allow human intervention or upper-level control to change the mode of operation, including partial or complete deactivation.

Russia also lists increased reliability and fault tolerance, limitations on targets, duration, geography and scale, timely intervention, real-environment testing and operator training as possible control measures. At the same time, Russia argues that the specific forms and methods of human control should remain at State discretion and can be ensured by means other than direct control. Russia also maintains that existing IHL is sufficient and that Article 36 review should not be replaced by a universal mandatory LAWS-specific mechanism.

This creates a precise Brazilian pressure point: Russia's own document identifies several common control variables, but its framework leaves the form of control primarily to States. Brazil can ask how the international community prevents a common legal requirement from becoming radically different minimum standards across States.

**POI:** “Russia identifies intervention, target limits, duration, geography and scale as control mechanisms. What common minimum standard prevents each State from defining sufficient control differently?”  
**Source:** Russian Federation, CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.2. citeturn1search13turn1search4

## 09 — India: Human Judgment and Oversight Are Essential

India's 2025 CCW working paper states that IHL applies fully to LAWS and that human judgment and oversight in their use are essential. India says these may take different forms and occur at different life-cycle stages depending on operational context and the characteristics of the system. India also describes autonomy as a continuum and connects autonomy to human oversight, control, responsibility and accountability.

India supports prohibition of fully autonomous LAWS that cannot be used in compliance with IHL and regulation of other LAWS to ensure compliance with IHL. This creates considerable overlap with Brazil's two-tier logic.

The difference Brazil can explore is not whether India recognizes human control—it clearly does—but how minimum conditions for sufficient oversight should be established. If the standard is entirely contextual, States still need a method of assessing whether the human role is enough for the circumstances.

**POI:** “India agrees that human judgment and oversight are essential. What minimum factors should every State assess before declaring that its oversight is sufficient for a particular LAWS deployment?”  
**Source:** India, CCW/GGE.1/2025/WP.3. citeturn1search10

## 10 — China: Human-Machine Interaction Throughout the Life Cycle

China's 2024 statement supports necessary human-machine interaction throughout the life cycle of weapon systems so that they remain under human control. China also advocates forward-looking risk assessment and tiered and categorized management. It recognizes the applicability of IHL while stating that questions remain about whether LAWS can discern and act consistently with distinction and proportionality and how responsibility and accountability should be determined.

For Brazil, this is valuable common ground. China itself accepts that control must be considered across the life cycle and that IHL compliance raises specific operational questions. Brazil can therefore focus on the evidence needed to demonstrate that human-machine interaction is sufficient in practice.

The most useful distinction is between **having an interface** and **having meaningful interaction**. An operator can technically interact with a system without understanding its limitations or being able to intervene effectively. Brazil can ask what information, constraints, testing evidence and intervention capabilities make the interaction substantively meaningful.

**POI:** “China supports human-machine interaction throughout the life cycle; what minimum operational evidence should demonstrate that this interaction remains meaningful at the point of lethal engagement?”  
**Source:** China Mission in Geneva, statement of 27 August 2024. citeturn1search0

## 11 — United States: “Appropriate” Human Judgment

The United States provides an important example of a flexible human-control model. U.S. policy requires systems to allow commanders and operators to exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force. U.S. explanations have emphasized that “appropriate” is context-dependent rather than a single fixed amount of human judgment applicable to every system and operational environment.

For the CCW debate, this creates a genuine conceptual difference with approaches seeking more internationally standardized minimum requirements. Brazil does not need to claim that the United States has no human-control policy. It clearly does. The stronger question is how context-dependence can coexist with international consistency.

**POI:** “If the level of human judgment is context-dependent, what internationally shared criteria prevent ‘appropriate’ from becoming whatever level of control an individual State chooses to regard as appropriate?”

This is more precise than accusing the United States of abandoning human control. It targets the standard-setting problem: flexibility can accommodate different systems, but an international instrument still needs enough common substance to prevent safeguards from becoming purely self-defined.

**Sources:** U.S. DoD Directive 3000.09; U.S. response in CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1; Congressional Research Service, updated 26 March 2026. citeturn1search11turn1search1

## 12 — The Black-Box Problem: What Brazil Should Actually Argue

Brazil's 2024 CCW response specifically recognizes the black-box concern: even if sufficient data is available to an autonomous system, the algorithm's prediction of civilian casualties or damage may remain non-transparent. Brazil also connects sufficient understanding, prediction and explanation to the ability to assess whether an autonomous system can operate consistently with IHL.

Brazil should be precise here. “Black box” does not automatically mean unlawful. The stronger proposition is that opacity can become legally significant where it prevents the responsible human from understanding relevant capabilities, limitations or likely behaviour sufficiently to make the judgments required by IHL.

The standard should therefore be **operational explainability**, not a demand that every commander understand source code. The human needs to know what the system is designed to do, under what conditions it was tested, what its known limitations are, how uncertainty is communicated and what circumstances should trigger withdrawal or intervention.

**POI:** “If the operator cannot meaningfully understand why the system is likely to behave a certain way in the intended environment, on what basis can the operator exercise the judgment that IHL requires?”  
**Source:** Brazil response, CCW/GGE.1/2024/CRP.1. citeturn0search9

## 13 — Article 36: Essential, But Not a Permanent Certificate

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a State Party, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would, in some or all circumstances, be prohibited by applicable international law. The ICRC regards weapons reviews as an important tool for assessing new weapons.

Russia states that it complies with Article 36 and regards it as customary international law, but argues against creating a universal mandatory LAWS-specific review mechanism. This shows that disagreement exists not only over whether review matters, but over whether LAWS justify additional common procedures and transparency.

Brazil can use a careful position: Article 36 review is indispensable, but a pre-deployment legal review should not be treated as a permanent authorization for every future use. Autonomous systems may be deployed in different environments, against different target categories, for different durations and at different scales. If those circumstances materially change, the factual basis for the original assessment can change.

**POI:** “Does the delegate regard an Article 36 review as sufficient for every later deployment even when the system, mission or operational environment materially changes?”

**Source:** Additional Protocol I, Article 36; Russian Federation CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.2; ICRC legal-review guidance. citeturn1search13turn0search1

## 14 — Testing and Validation Must Match the Circumstances of Use

The 2024 CCW discussions proposed rigorous testing and evaluation so that a human operator can have a reliable expectation of how a weapon system will perform in anticipated circumstances. Russia separately supports testing in the real operational environment and comprehensive testing throughout the life cycle.

This provides Brazil with a concrete evidence standard. Testing should not simply establish that a system works in a controlled demonstration. It should establish that its relevant behaviour is sufficiently understood under the circumstances in which it is intended to operate. Relevant variables can include terrain, weather, civilian presence, target characteristics, duration, sensor degradation, communications and interaction with other systems.

Brazil should not demand absolute certainty. The point is that the human decision-maker needs a defensible basis for expecting the system to behave within the conditions of lawful employment. Where the testing envelope does not correspond to the operational envelope, the evidentiary basis for relying on autonomy becomes weaker.

**POI:** “What evidence does the delegate require before concluding that a system tested in one environment will remain sufficiently predictable in the environment where it is actually deployed?”

**Sources:** CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.11; Russian Federation CCW/GGE.1/2024/WP.2. citeturn1search12turn1search4

## 15 — Automation Bias and the Human-Machine Interface

The 2026 CCW Chair's summary records discussion of automation bias and supports understandable human-machine interfaces, guidance and instructions, and training on system capabilities and limitations. This is important because formal human involvement can coexist with practical over-reliance on machine outputs.

The problem is not that humans always obey machines. The more precise issue is whether the interface, workload, information design and training allow the human to exercise independent judgment. If an operator receives a machine recommendation but lacks the information or time to evaluate it, the human can become a procedural approver rather than a substantive decision-maker.

Brazil can therefore move beyond the simplistic phrase “human in the loop.” A human approval step is not automatically meaningful control. The relevant evidence includes what the operator can see, how uncertainty is communicated, how many systems are supervised simultaneously, how quickly intervention is possible, and whether the operator has been trained to recognize abnormal system behaviour.

**POI:** “If the safeguard is merely a human approval step, how does the delegate prevent automation bias from turning that approval into procedural confirmation rather than independent judgment?”  
**Source:** CCW/GGE.1/2026/WP.2. citeturn0search8

## 16 — Training and Operator Competence

The 2026 CCW discussion specifically links responsibility and accountability to training personnel so they understand guidance, instructions, and the capabilities and limitations of autonomous functions in anticipated circumstances. This provides an important operational dimension to human control.

An operator cannot meaningfully supervise a system without understanding the relevant limitations of that system. Similarly, a commander cannot make a defensible decision about deployment without knowing the conditions in which the system was validated and the circumstances in which its performance may degrade.

Brazil can therefore treat training as one part of a chain: **information → competence → authority → intervention**. The human needs sufficient information about the system; sufficient competence to interpret that information; sufficient authority to constrain or terminate its use; and practical means to act when necessary.

Training also matters for accountability because it helps establish whether personnel understood the instructions and limitations governing their use of the system. A robust framework should therefore treat operator qualification as part of the conditions for deployment rather than as an afterthought.

## 17 — Real-World Autonomous Weapons: Use Cases Must Be Described Precisely

Brazil should be extremely careful when discussing alleged battlefield use of “fully autonomous” weapons. The label can cover very different technical arrangements. A system may use AI for image recognition while a human selects the target; another may autonomously track a human-selected object; another may independently identify, select and engage targets within a programmed target profile. These are not interchangeable facts.

The ICRC's 2026 position paper states that autonomous weapon systems are already used against military objectives by nature, including systems such as missiles, radars and warships, generally in environments where civilians are absent or excluded and often under human supervision. It also warns about newer developments involving broader target selection, swarms and AI integration into targeting processes.

Brazil should therefore use real-world examples as evidence of a trend or control problem only when the underlying source establishes the relevant mode of operation. Do not convert a media allegation into a statement that a weapon autonomously selected and killed a person unless the evidence actually establishes that fact.

**Research rule:** identify the system, source, autonomous function, target-selection process and degree of human intervention before making a battlefield claim.

**Source:** ICRC Position Paper 2026. citeturn0search11

## 18 — What the Evidence Actually Shows About “Meaningful” Control

Across the primary material, there is substantial agreement that humans retain responsibility and that IHL applies to LAWS. The harder disagreement concerns how to make that responsibility operational. The CCW record discusses predictability, reliability, traceability, explainability, testing, target restrictions, duration, geography, scale, supervision, intervention, training and interfaces. Different States disagree over how prescriptive those safeguards should be and whether existing IHL is sufficient or should be supplemented by a new legally binding instrument.

Brazil's strongest research-based position is therefore not that every autonomous function is automatically unlawful. It is that the human-control requirement must be capable of being demonstrated through evidence. A State should be able to show what the system can do, what it cannot do, where it was tested, what circumstances it is authorized to operate in, what restrictions apply, who supervises it, how intervention works, and what happens when the factual basis for the original authorization changes.

This gives Brazil a disciplined framework: **understand the system; anticipate its behaviour in the intended circumstances; constrain the operational envelope; retain realistic intervention; reassess when circumstances materially change.** This framework complements Substantive I rather than repeating it: the first substantive addresses responsibility and attribution; this one supplies the operational research for determining whether human control is genuinely present.
