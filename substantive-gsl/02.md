# 02 — Meaningful Human Control: From Authorization to Operational Reality

> **Committee:** CCW — LAWS  
> **Country:** Federative Republic of Brazil  
> **Core thesis:** Meaningful human control is not a single authorization event. It is a continuing, context-dependent condition linking human judgment to the critical functions, operational circumstances and effects of force throughout the weapon system's life cycle.

---

## 01 — Defining Meaningful Human Control

Meaningful human control should not be reduced to the mere presence of a human somewhere in a weapons system's chain of command. The substantive question is whether the human retains sufficient judgment and control over the circumstances in which force is applied to ensure compliance with international law, particularly IHL. The 2025 CCW work describes “context-appropriate human judgement and control” as a context-dependent threshold of timely, appropriate or sufficient human involvement. It also links control to sufficient assurance that a system will act foreseeably enough for its effects to be assessed against IHL, rules of engagement and commanders' intentions. Brazil should therefore frame MHC as a functional legal standard rather than a technological label. The relevant inquiry is not **“Was a human involved?”**, but **“What did the human actually understand, decide, constrain and retain control over?”**

## 02 — Human Agency and the Decision to Employ Force

Human agency is central because the legal regulation of attacks presupposes human decision-making. A system can automate navigation, detection or other functions without necessarily displacing the human decision to employ force. The concern becomes acute when autonomy reaches identification, selection and engagement. Brazil should distinguish between delegating a supporting function and delegating the critical decision through which force is ultimately applied. Human agency therefore has to be substantive: the responsible person should have enough information and authority to make the relevant judgment and enough control to act when circumstances change. The ICRC emphasizes that IHL obligations concerning attacks cannot be transferred to a machine. For Brazil, this means autonomy can assist human decision-making, but it cannot become a mechanism for removing human judgment from the use of lethal force.

## 03 — Authorization Is Not Control

Authorization is an important stage of the deployment chain, but it is not synonymous with continuing control. A commander may approve a mission based on specified targets, geography, duration and expected system behaviour. If the system later encounters circumstances materially different from those assessed, the original authorization may no longer provide an adequate basis for continued autonomous action. Brazil should therefore distinguish **authorization before activation** from **control during employment**. The distinction becomes especially important where the system independently identifies and selects targets. A human may have authorized a category of operation without deciding which particular object or person the system will engage. Meaningful control consequently requires more than a signature or activation command; it requires a continuing relationship between human judgment and the system's operational conduct.

## 04 — The Human Decision-Making Nexus

A useful way to analyse MHC is through a “decision-making nexus”: the connection between human judgment, operational circumstances and the resulting use of force. If a human understands the relevant circumstances, establishes lawful parameters, retains the ability to constrain the system and can intervene when necessary, the nexus is stronger. If the human merely authorizes a system whose subsequent behaviour is opaque, unpredictable or unconstrained, the nexus weakens. Brazil should use this concept to avoid simplistic debates over whether a weapon is “autonomous.” The legal significance of autonomy depends on where autonomous functions sit within the chain leading to force. The closer the autonomous function comes to identifying, selecting and engaging a target, the more demanding the requirements for human understanding, predictability, control and intervention become.

## 05 — Critical Functions of LAWS

The CCW's recent work has increasingly focused on critical functions such as **identification, selection and engagement**. These functions matter because they sit directly within the chain through which force is applied. A system that autonomously navigates to a predetermined human-selected target presents a different control question from one that identifies a target category and independently decides which object satisfies that category. The distinction should not be hidden by broad labels such as “autonomous weapon.” Brazil should therefore analyse the exact function being delegated. Where autonomy performs a critical lethal function, the human role must be sufficiently substantive to preserve lawful judgment. This functional approach also makes Brazil's argument more technically precise: the issue is not autonomy in the abstract, but the delegation of particular decisions that have legal consequences.

## 06 — Human-in-the-Loop, Human-on-the-Loop and Human-out-of-the-Loop

The labels “human-in-the-loop,” “on-the-loop” and “out-of-the-loop” can be useful descriptive shorthand, but they are not themselves legal conclusions. A human-in-the-loop may still have inadequate information or time to make a meaningful decision. A human-on-the-loop may have effective supervision and intervention in one operational context but not another. A system with no human intervention after activation raises more demanding questions about parameters, predictability and lawful effects. Brazil should therefore resist a purely terminological debate. The proper test is functional and contextual: what does the human know, what can the human decide, what can the human constrain, and how quickly can the human intervene? The 2025 CCW discussions similarly emphasize that different forms of human judgment and control may be appropriate at different stages of a system's life cycle.

## 07 — The Problem of Ceremonial Human Oversight

Formal oversight can become ceremonial when the human retains nominal authority but lacks the practical ability to exercise independent judgment. Examples include excessive system speed, inadequate information, overwhelming numbers of simultaneous engagements, or an intervention mechanism that cannot realistically operate before the effect occurs. Brazil should therefore distinguish **formal authority** from **effective control**. The question is not whether an operator theoretically possesses a veto, but whether that veto can be exercised meaningfully under the actual circumstances. This distinction also matters for accountability: a paper trail showing that a human “approved” an operation does not by itself demonstrate that the human understood the relevant risks or could control the critical functions. Meaningful control must be operationally real, not merely administratively documented.

## 08 — Human Judgment Under IHL

Human judgment is particularly important because IHL contains context-sensitive obligations. Distinction requires assessment of the nature and status of persons and objects. Proportionality requires a judgment concerning expected incidental civilian harm in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage. Precautions require feasible measures to avoid or minimize civilian harm. These are not simply technical classification problems. Their application depends upon the circumstances of the attack. Brazil should therefore ask whether the human decision-maker has enough information, time, authority and control to perform the required judgment in the actual environment of use. The existence of a machine-generated classification does not itself discharge the human's legal obligations. A system may assist a human, but meaningful control requires that the human remain capable of exercising the judgment that IHL demands.

## 09 — Meaningful Control as a Continuing Condition

Meaningful control should continue throughout the relevant life cycle and operation of a weapon system. The CCW's 2025 discussions considered context-appropriate human judgment and control across the entire life cycle. This is significant because a system's legal and operational characteristics do not end with development or acquisition. Testing, modification, deployment, activation, operation and reassessment can all affect whether the conditions for lawful use remain satisfied. Brazil should therefore reject the assumption that one pre-deployment review permanently establishes control. If material circumstances change, the factual basis for the original judgment may change as well. Continuing control does not necessarily mean continuous direct human intervention; rather, it means that the level and form of human judgment remain sufficient for the circumstances and that critical changes do not occur beyond meaningful human authority.

## 10 — The Brazil Standard: Understand, Anticipate, Constrain, Intervene, Reassess

Brazil can organize MHC around five practical capacities: **understand, anticipate, constrain, intervene and reassess**. The responsible humans should understand the system's capabilities and limitations; anticipate its behaviour in the expected operational environment; constrain its mission, targets, geography, duration and other critical parameters; intervene or deactivate when necessary; and reassess when material circumstances change. This is not intended as a mechanical checklist that replaces IHL. It is a way to translate the broader concept of meaningful human control into operational questions. The standard should remain context-dependent, because different systems and missions require different levels of human involvement. What matters is that the human-machine relationship remains sufficiently robust to support compliance with IHL and preserve human responsibility throughout the relevant chain.

## 11 — Research, Development and Design

Meaningful control begins before a weapon reaches the battlefield. Design choices determine what the system can perceive, how it processes inputs, what parameters constrain it and whether humans can intervene. A State cannot sensibly assess operational control without understanding the characteristics that have been built into the system. Brazil should therefore treat design as part of the control architecture rather than as a purely technical stage. Critical questions include whether the system can be bounded geographically and temporally, whether mission parameters can be modified without human validation, whether operators can understand relevant limitations, and whether the system's behaviour remains within the conditions for which it was assessed. The 2025 CCW discussions explicitly contemplate human judgment and control throughout the weapon system's life cycle, reinforcing the importance of considering control from the earliest stages.

## 12 — Training Data and System Behaviour

Where machine-learning techniques are involved, the characteristics of training and validation data can affect system performance. Brazil should avoid simplistic claims that data quality automatically determines legality; the legal issue is whether the resulting system can be used consistently with IHL in the expected circumstances. Nevertheless, inadequate, unrepresentative or biased data can contribute to uncertainty, misclassification and unpredictable performance. Relevant questions include what populations, environments and target categories were represented during development; what known limitations exist; and whether operators understand those limitations. The ICRC has identified AI and machine-learning systems as raising particular concerns regarding unpredictability, explainability and bias. For Brazil, these are not merely software-engineering concerns: where system behaviour affects the lawful application of force, technical limitations become part of the human-control assessment.

## 13 — Testing and Validation

Testing should establish more than whether a system can perform a task under ideal conditions. The relevant question is whether its performance is sufficiently understood and predictable in the circumstances in which it will actually be employed. Validation should therefore consider environmental conditions, target characteristics, civilian presence, mission duration, geographic scope, expected system interactions and foreseeable failure modes. Brazil should also distinguish laboratory performance from operational validation. A system that performs reliably against a fixed test set may behave differently in a dynamic environment. Testing must consequently be tied to the intended conditions of use. The ICRC has identified predictability and reliability as practical challenges for legal review of autonomous weapons. Brazil can use this distinction to argue that technical performance claims must be connected to the actual legal and operational context rather than presented as universal guarantees.

## 14 — Operational Authorization

Operational authorization should rest upon an informed human assessment of the system and the mission. Relevant information includes capabilities, limitations, expected effects, target categories, operating environment, geographic boundaries, duration and available intervention mechanisms. A broad authorization such as “engage qualifying targets in an area” can raise more demanding questions than a narrowly bounded mission with tightly specified parameters. Brazil should therefore emphasize that authorization must be sufficiently informed and specific to the circumstances. This does not require commanders to manually approve every machine action in every context. It requires the authorization to remain connected to the foreseeable conduct of the system. Where that connection is absent, the authorization becomes increasingly formal rather than substantive.

## 15 — Rules of Engagement and Autonomous Systems

Rules of engagement can translate legal obligations into operational instructions. With autonomous systems, however, the relationship between rules and machine parameters becomes particularly important. If an operator authorizes a system subject to geographic, temporal or target restrictions, those restrictions must meaningfully constrain its operation. Human-machine interfaces should communicate the relevant parameters clearly enough for personnel to understand what the system is authorized to do. The 2026 CCW work highlights understandable interfaces, guidance and instructions, training, and responsible human command and control as measures supporting responsibility. Brazil should therefore treat rules of engagement as part of the operational control architecture, not as documents detached from the technical system. The machine's programmed constraints and the human commander's legal instructions should not contradict one another.

## 16 — Target Selection

Target selection is among the most consequential points in the deployment chain. There is a fundamental difference between a human identifying a specific lawful target and a human establishing a generalized target profile from which a system independently selects particular objects or persons. The latter raises questions about whether the system can reliably determine whether the conditions for engagement are satisfied in the actual circumstances. The 2025 CCW material explicitly discusses systems that can identify and select targets under preset conditions and asks whether they can satisfactorily comply with IHL. Brazil should therefore insist that target-selection autonomy requires particularly careful assessment of predictability, context, discrimination and human control. The more discretion the system has over which particular object becomes the target, the more important it becomes to establish that the human retains meaningful control over the resulting use of force.

## 17 — Engagement Decisions

Engagement is the point at which a targeting process culminates in the application of force. A human may have selected a mission and established parameters, but if the system itself determines the moment of engagement, the relationship between prior human judgment and the actual attack becomes crucial. Brazil should ask whether the human could anticipate the relevant circumstances, whether the system was constrained to lawful conditions, and whether intervention remained possible. The 2025 CCW work distinguishes systems where a human initiates engagement from systems that can engage autonomously. It also identifies increasing concerns where systems lose human safeguards such as intervention or preset conditions. Brazil should therefore avoid the simplistic proposition that every autonomous engagement is automatically unlawful. Instead, it should examine whether the system's level of autonomy remains compatible with the required human judgment, control and IHL obligations in the circumstances.

## 18 — Human Supervision During Operation

Supervision has substance only if the supervisor can understand what is happening and act when necessary. Relevant factors include the information presented, the number of systems being monitored, the speed of operations, the complexity of the environment and the availability of intervention mechanisms. An operator who receives thousands of alerts simultaneously may possess a formal monitoring role without possessing realistic control. Similarly, a supervisor who cannot distinguish between normal and anomalous system behaviour may not be able to intervene effectively. Brazil should therefore analyse supervision as a capacity rather than a job title. The required level of supervision is context-dependent, consistent with the CCW concept of context-appropriate human judgment and control. The key question is whether supervision preserves a meaningful ability to influence the use of force before unlawful consequences occur.

## 19 — Intervention and Deactivation

An intervention mechanism is meaningful only when it can operate in practice. The ICRC identifies human supervision, the ability to intervene and timely deactivation as key control elements. Brazil should therefore consider response time, system speed, communications, interface design, operator awareness and the consequences of intervention. A “kill switch” can be valuable, but its existence alone does not resolve every control question. If the system acts faster than a human can reasonably respond, or if the operator cannot identify the need to intervene, the safeguard may be largely theoretical. The appropriate question is whether intervention can occur **in time, with sufficient information, under the expected conditions of use**. This reinforces Brazil's broader principle: meaningful human control must be judged according to operational reality rather than formal architecture.

## 20 — Post-Strike Review and Feedback

Post-strike review cannot substitute for control during the use of force, but it remains important. Investigations can reveal system failures, unexpected behaviour, inadequate parameters, operator misunderstandings or weaknesses in training. Brazil should support mechanisms that preserve relevant records and enable authorities to reconstruct what occurred. However, retrospective analysis has a distinct function: it supports accountability, learning and corrective action rather than replacing the human judgment that should have existed before and during the attack. A sound framework therefore combines ex ante assessment, operational monitoring and ex post investigation. The 2026 CCW discussions specifically considered effective internal mechanisms for investigation, reporting and appropriate action following incidents involving possible IHL violations. This provides a useful bridge between operational control and institutional learning.

## 21 — Predictability as a Condition of Control

Predictability is not simply a technical performance metric. It is connected to whether a human can reasonably anticipate how a weapon will behave in the relevant circumstances. The ICRC has linked effective control to predictability and reliability, particularly where autonomous systems operate across changing environments or interact with other systems. Brazil should therefore ask: predictable **for whom, under what conditions, and for which function?** A system may be predictable in a controlled test environment yet insufficiently predictable in a dynamic battlefield. A high average success rate does not answer whether rare but foreseeable failures can produce unlawful effects. Predictability should consequently be assessed in relation to the specific task, target, environment, duration and operational scope for which the system is authorized.

## 22 — Reliability Versus Predictability

Reliability and predictability are related but not identical. Reliability can concern whether a system consistently performs a specified function. Predictability concerns whether humans can reasonably anticipate its behaviour and effects under relevant circumstances. A system can be highly reliable at following a rule and still produce problematic results if the rule is inappropriate for a changing environment. Conversely, a system can have occasional technical failures that are detectable and safely contained. Brazil should therefore resist numerical arguments that collapse the two concepts into a single “accuracy” figure. The legal inquiry should ask whether the system is sufficiently reliable and predictable for the task and circumstances in which it will be used, and whether humans retain enough control to respond to uncertainty or deviation.

## 23 — Explainability and Legal Judgment

Explainability has practical relevance because human decision-makers must understand enough about a system to assess its lawful use. The objective is not necessarily to require every operator to understand source code. Rather, the responsible humans should understand relevant capabilities, limitations, operational conditions and expected effects well enough to exercise legal judgment. Explainability also matters after an incident, when authorities may need to reconstruct why the system behaved as it did. Brazil's 2024 position links sufficient understanding, prediction and explanation to the compatibility of autonomous weapons with IHL. The CCW's 2025 discussions also identified explainability as an element some delegations considered relevant to LAWS-specific safeguards. Brazil can therefore present explainability as a practical support for both informed authorization and credible accountability.

## 24 — The Black-Box Problem

A black-box system does not necessarily become unlawful merely because its internal computation is complex. The more precise concern is whether its opacity prevents the responsible humans from exercising the judgment and control required in the relevant circumstances. If an operator cannot understand the system's relevant limitations, cannot anticipate its likely behaviour, or cannot reconstruct critical events after an incident, meaningful control becomes more difficult to demonstrate. Brazil should therefore avoid demanding impossible technical transparency. The proper standard is functional transparency: enough information for lawful decision-making, effective supervision and investigation. This distinction makes Brazil's argument more defensible and avoids confusing algorithmic explainability in a mathematical sense with the practical information required by commanders and legal reviewers.

## 25 — Uncertainty and Confidence Thresholds

Autonomous systems frequently operate with uncertainty. The legal problem is not solved by declaring a numerical confidence threshold without explaining what that threshold means. A confidence score may describe a classification output, but IHL requires context-specific judgments about persons, objects, military advantage and expected civilian harm. Brazil should therefore ask what information the confidence measure actually represents, how it was validated, and how uncertainty is communicated to the human decision-maker. A system that produces a high-confidence classification in a context outside its validated conditions may still present serious risks. The correct framework should combine technical uncertainty with operational constraints and human judgment rather than treating a single numerical threshold as a universal legal answer.

## 26 — Distribution Shift and Changing Environments

System performance can change when operational conditions differ from development or testing conditions. Different terrain, weather, lighting, populations, objects, camouflage, sensor conditions or adversarial behaviour can affect performance. Brazil should therefore emphasize context-specific validation. The fact that a system performed well in one environment does not automatically establish that it will behave predictably in another. This is particularly important for adaptive systems and machine-learning applications. The CCW concept of “context-appropriate” human judgment and control supports an approach in which the level of control is linked to the circumstances of use. Brazil can use distribution shift as a technical illustration of a broader legal principle: **control must be assessed against the circumstances actually authorized, not merely those in which the system was originally tested.**

## 27 — Adaptive and Learning Systems

Adaptive systems raise a special question: what exactly has the human authorized if the system can alter relevant behaviour during operation? Not every adaptive function necessarily changes the legal assessment, but material changes to mission parameters or system behaviour can. The 2025 CCW discussions specifically considered systems capable of modifying preset parameters such as engagement area, time of operation or mission objectives without external intervention. Such systems raise heightened questions about predictability, precautions and control. Brazil should therefore distinguish benign adaptation from changes that materially affect lethal decision-making. Where a system can modify critical parameters autonomously, human validation may be required to preserve the connection between authorization and actual employment.

## 28 — Multiple-System Interaction and Swarming

Interaction among multiple autonomous systems can make control more difficult because behaviour may emerge from the interaction rather than from a single system's isolated programming. The ICRC has noted that increased mobility, adaptability and interaction among multiple systems can increase unpredictability. Brazil should therefore treat swarm operations as a distinct operational-control problem. Questions include how many systems one human can supervise, whether individual system behaviour remains understandable, whether interactions can create unexpected effects, and whether a human can intervene effectively across the group. A swarm should not be considered adequately controlled merely because each individual platform passed a separate test. The relevant assessment must consider the behaviour of the integrated system under the circumstances of intended use.

## 29 — Failure Modes and Unexpected Behaviour

No complex system should be assessed solely on successful operation. Human control requires an understanding of relevant failure modes and safeguards against them. Brazil should distinguish between failures that are detectable and containable and failures that can produce uncontrolled lethal effects. Testing should examine foreseeable abnormal conditions, not only nominal performance. Operators should receive guidance on indicators of degraded performance and circumstances requiring suspension or deactivation. The objective is not to demand zero technical failure, which would be unrealistic. It is to ensure that failures do not undermine the ability to apply IHL or leave humans without meaningful control. A system's safety architecture therefore forms part of the overall assessment of whether its autonomous functions can be lawfully employed.

## 30 — Accuracy Is Not Lawfulness

“Accuracy” is an incomplete metric for evaluating autonomous force. A system could accurately recognize the object it was trained to recognize while the legal question remains whether that object is a lawful target in the circumstances. Similarly, a system could accurately follow a target while circumstances change so that engagement is no longer proportionate or precautions require modification. Brazil should therefore insist upon a distinction between technical accuracy and legal judgment. Accuracy can support lawful use, but it cannot substitute for the contextual assessments required by IHL. The critical question is whether the system's capabilities, parameters and human oversight allow the responsible humans to apply the law to the actual circumstances in which force is used.

## 31 — The Operating Environment as a Legal Variable

The same autonomous function may present different legal and operational implications in different environments. An air-defence system operating in a tightly controlled environment with no civilians may present a different control problem from a system selecting targets in a densely populated urban area. Brazil should therefore reject universal claims that a system is simply “safe” or “unsafe” independent of context. Context matters to the level and form of human involvement required. The 2025 CCW work expressly describes context-appropriate control in relation to the operational environment, conditions, weapon type and planned mission. This allows Brazil to argue for rigorous limits without claiming that every autonomous function is identical. The central standard remains whether the system can be used in the particular circumstances consistently with IHL and meaningful human judgment and control.

## 32 — Urban Warfare and Civilian Density

Urban environments intensify the demand for contextual human judgment because civilians, civilian objects and military objectives can be closely intermingled and circumstances can change rapidly. Autonomous targeting based on generalized profiles may face greater difficulty where the legal status of an object or person depends upon changing circumstances. Brazil should therefore examine whether the system's operational constraints are sufficiently narrow for the environment. The ICRC has repeatedly highlighted concerns about autonomous systems being used in populated areas and has proposed restrictions on situations of use for certain systems. Brazil need not claim that every autonomous system is automatically unlawful in every urban setting. Instead, it should emphasize that civilian density, target ambiguity and changing circumstances can materially alter what level of human judgment and control is required.

## 33 — Dynamic Battlefields

A dynamic battlefield can change faster than a human authorization remains valid. Targets move, civilians enter an area, objects change status, missions evolve and communications may degrade. Brazil should therefore focus on whether the system and its human operators can respond to these changes while preserving IHL compliance. A system that is lawful under one set of assumptions may require reassessment when those assumptions cease to hold. The key concept is not continuous manual operation but **continuing legal relevance of human judgment**. If the human has no practical means to recognize or respond to a material change, then the original authorization may become detached from the actual attack circumstances. This is why temporal, spatial and target restrictions can be important control mechanisms.

## 34 — Geographic Restrictions

Geographic limits can preserve a clear relationship between human authorization and autonomous operation. A commander can assess a defined area, its expected characteristics and the mission to be conducted there. If the system moves beyond that area, the assumptions underlying the original judgment may no longer hold. Brazil should therefore support meaningful geographic constraints where necessary to preserve control. The ICRC has identified the geographical scope of operation as one of the practical factors relevant to human control. Geographic limits can also assist traceability and investigation because they define the authorized operational envelope. The important point is that geography should not be treated as a mere technical fence; it can be a substantive mechanism for keeping autonomous force within the circumstances that human decision-makers actually assessed.

## 35 — Temporal Restrictions

Time can be as important as geography. An autonomous system authorized for a short period may operate under assumptions that cease to be valid after a prolonged mission. Civilian presence, target status, weather, communications and tactical circumstances can all change. Brazil should therefore consider whether duration limits are appropriate to the system and mission. A temporal limit can function as a trigger for renewed human assessment rather than merely as a technical timer. The ICRC identifies the time frame of operation as an important operational restriction. The broader principle is that the longer a system operates without meaningful reassessment, the greater the risk that its behaviour will outlive the factual assumptions that supported the original authorization.

## 36 — Target-Type Restrictions

Target restrictions can narrow the circumstances in which autonomous functions are employed. The ICRC has discussed limits on target types as a practical control measure, including distinctions between objects and persons. Brazil should analyse whether the system can reliably determine the relevant target category and whether the category remains stable in the intended environment. Restricting autonomy to clearly defined military objectives by nature can reduce some contextual uncertainty, although the legal status of objects can still change. The critical point is that target restrictions must be technically enforceable and operationally meaningful. A prohibition written in a document is insufficient if the system cannot reliably implement it. Human control therefore involves both legal parameters and the technical ability to make those parameters effective.

## 37 — Scale of Autonomous Employment

Scale can affect whether human supervision remains meaningful. One operator supervising a small number of systems may have a different capacity from one operator supervising hundreds or thousands of simultaneous engagements. Brazil should therefore treat scale as a control variable. The question is not merely whether the human has a screen showing all systems, but whether the human can meaningfully understand, monitor and intervene across the relevant operations. The 2025 CCW discussions included operational scale among the factors that can affect human judgment and control. A system that is individually predictable may still create an aggregate control problem if the number or speed of engagements exceeds the realistic capacity of human supervision. Human control must therefore be assessed at the level of the actual mission, not only the individual platform.

## 38 — Communications Failure and Degraded Conditions

Intervention mechanisms depend upon functioning communications and interfaces. If communications are disrupted, the system may continue operating without the human oversight originally contemplated. Brazil should therefore consider whether autonomous operation has defined fail-safe conditions for communications loss and other degraded circumstances. Relevant questions include whether the system terminates, returns, enters a restricted mode, continues under tightly bounded parameters, or otherwise changes behaviour. There is no single technical answer for every system. The legal issue is whether the chosen behaviour preserves the conditions required for lawful use. Communications resilience and degradation scenarios should consequently form part of testing, operational planning and legal review. A human cannot meaningfully supervise a system that has become operationally disconnected without safeguards appropriate to the circumstances.

## 39 — Changing Mission Parameters

Mission parameters define the operational envelope within which autonomous behaviour is authorized. They can include target types, geographic boundaries, duration, engagement conditions and objectives. The 2025 CCW Chair's summary specifically included a proposal that LAWS' mission parameters should not be modified by the system without context-appropriate human control and judgment. Brazil should therefore treat autonomous modification of critical parameters as a major control concern. If a system changes its own engagement area or mission objective, the human may no longer be controlling the same operation that was originally authorized. Human validation of material changes preserves the link between the original legal judgment and the subsequent use of force.

## 40 — Reassessment During Deployment

Reassessment is the bridge between pre-deployment judgment and operational reality. It does not require constant manual approval of every machine action. Rather, it requires human review when circumstances materially change or when the system behaves outside its validated assumptions. Triggering events could include major changes in civilian presence, mission objective, geographic area, target set, system performance or communications. Brazil should therefore advocate clear material-change criteria. Reassessment can be understood as a safeguard against stale authorization: the fact that an operation was lawful to initiate does not automatically establish that every later phase remains lawful. This concept is especially important for adaptive systems and prolonged autonomous operations.

## 41 — Article 36 Weapons Reviews

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a High Contracting Party, when studying, developing, acquiring or adopting a new weapon, means or method of warfare, to determine whether its employment would be prohibited in some or all circumstances by applicable international law. Brazil should treat legal review as a foundation, not the endpoint, of meaningful control. A review can examine intended functions, expected environments, limitations and foreseeable modes of use. For autonomous systems, practical challenges arise because future behaviour may depend on data, software, operational context and modifications. The ICRC has emphasized both the importance and practical difficulty of legal reviews for autonomous weapons. Brazil should therefore support rigorous review processes while recognizing that review at one stage does not eliminate the need for reassessment when a system is materially modified or employed in materially different circumstances.

## 42 — Distinction and Autonomous Targeting

Distinction requires parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants and between civilian objects and military objectives. Autonomous systems that identify or select targets must therefore be assessed against the actual conditions in which they will operate. Brazil should not reduce distinction to object-recognition accuracy. Legal status can depend on context, conduct, and changing circumstances. A system may recognize an object correctly while failing to determine whether the object is a lawful military objective at that moment. The human-control question is therefore whether the responsible humans have sufficient understanding, parameters and intervention capacity to ensure that the system's use remains consistent with distinction. Where the system's autonomous target selection exceeds those capabilities, the legal basis for employment becomes increasingly difficult to sustain.

## 43 — Proportionality and Contextual Judgment

Proportionality requires assessing whether expected incidental civilian harm would be excessive in relation to the anticipated concrete and direct military advantage. This is inherently contextual. A machine can calculate inputs, but the legal question depends upon the information, assumptions and judgments available at the time of attack. Brazil should therefore examine whether humans retain sufficient control to update or terminate an engagement when circumstances change. The 2025 CCW material illustrates this issue by considering whether a human can abort an engagement if the situation changes or whether parameters can require the system to abort under specified conditions. The relevant point is not that autonomous systems can never support proportionality assessments, but that meaningful human judgment must remain connected to the circumstances in which the attack actually occurs.

## 44 — Precautions in Attack

The obligation to take feasible precautions is particularly relevant to autonomous systems because precautions may need to be adapted as circumstances evolve. A human commander may select a time, method or target based on available information. If the system then operates autonomously, safeguards must ensure that the operation remains within the conditions assessed. Brazil should therefore consider advance precautions, operational constraints, human supervision, intervention and termination mechanisms together. A precaution that exists only in a planning document but cannot be implemented by the system or operator is weak as an operational safeguard. Meaningful human control should consequently be understood as part of the practical architecture through which precautions are maintained during autonomous employment.

## 45 — Unlawful or Unpredictable Effects

Where a system's effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or controlled, the legal and humanitarian concerns become particularly acute. Brazil's own CCW approach identifies systems whose functions and effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained, or whose use may prevent attribution, as serious grounds for incompatibility with IHL. The ICRC has likewise recommended prohibiting autonomous systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted and explained. Brazil should therefore distinguish between ordinary uncertainty that can be bounded through operational restrictions and fundamental unpredictability that defeats meaningful control. The latter is not merely a performance inconvenience. If humans cannot reasonably anticipate the consequences of employing the weapon in the relevant circumstances, the connection between human judgment and force becomes substantially weakened.

## 46 — Existing IHL and New International Rules

Brazil should recognize that existing IHL applies to new weapons and means of warfare while also examining whether autonomy creates risks requiring more specific international rules. The two propositions are not contradictory. Existing rules establish obligations such as distinction, proportionality and precautions; new rules can clarify how those obligations should be implemented where autonomy changes the relationship between human judgment and force. The CCW's current work reflects this discussion: delegations have debated whether proposed LAWS-specific elements should be framed as direct IHL obligations or as additional regulatory safeguards. Brazil should therefore avoid claiming that existing IHL is irrelevant. Instead, it can argue that preserving IHL's applicability does not eliminate the need for specific, operationally meaningful rules addressing autonomy.

## 47 — Brazil's Two-Tier Approach

Brazil has supported a two-tier approach distinguishing systems that must be prohibited from systems that may be permitted only under strict regulation. For Substantive II, the important point is how this framework relates to meaningful control. Systems whose functions and effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted or controlled, or whose use would prevent compliance with IHL, cannot be made acceptable merely by assigning a human supervisor in name. Other systems may require context-specific restrictions on targets, geography, duration, scale, mission parameters and intervention. Brazil's approach should therefore be presented as a spectrum of legal treatment rather than a blanket rejection of every autonomous function. The decisive question is whether the system can operate within conditions that preserve human judgment and compliance with international law.

## 48 — A Brazil Test for Lawful Meaningful Control

Brazil can operationalize its approach through five questions. **Understand:** do responsible humans understand relevant capabilities, limitations and expected effects? **Anticipate:** can they reasonably anticipate system behaviour in the authorized context? **Constrain:** can they impose and maintain meaningful limits on targets, geography, duration, scale and mission? **Intervene:** can they effectively interrupt or deactivate when required? **Reassess:** can they reconsider employment when material circumstances change? These questions should not replace IHL; they operationalize the conditions under which IHL can be meaningfully applied to autonomous systems. A system need not satisfy a single universal technical architecture. What matters is whether its particular human-machine relationship provides sufficient control for the particular task and circumstances.

## 49 — Counterarguments and Rebuttals

**“Existing IHL is sufficient.”** Brazil can respond that existing IHL remains applicable, but autonomy creates implementation questions that may require specific rules. **“The commander remains responsible.”** Brazil can ask whether the commander retained sufficient judgment and control over the critical engagement. **“Technology is becoming more accurate.”** Accuracy does not itself establish predictability, proportionality or accountability. **“A kill switch solves the problem.”** A theoretical intervention mechanism is insufficient if it cannot operate in time or under actual conditions. **“National militaries should define their own standards.”** Brazil can recognize contextual differences while insisting that international obligations cannot become so indeterminate that meaningful control has no common substance. **“Autonomous systems reduce human error.”** Even if a system can improve certain technical tasks, the legal question remains whether humans retain the judgment and control required by IHL in the circumstances of use.

## 50 — Brazil's Proposed International Framework

Brazil's objective is to ensure that meaningful human control remains a **substantive, continuous and context-appropriate condition of lawful autonomous weapons employment**. The framework should preserve human judgment over critical functions; require understanding of capabilities, limitations and expected effects; maintain operational constraints appropriate to the mission; prevent autonomous modification of critical mission parameters without human validation; preserve effective intervention and deactivation; require reassessment when material circumstances change; and strengthen legal review, training, traceability and investigation. The central principle is deliberately simple: **autonomy may alter how force is delivered, but it cannot sever the relationship between human judgment and lawful use of force.** Brazil therefore supports an international framework in which the question is never merely whether a human was somewhere in the chain, but whether meaningful human judgment and control remained connected to the critical use of force throughout the deployment chain.

## CORE BRAZILIAN FORMULATION

> **Meaningful human control is not a moment of authorization. It is the continuing capacity of human decision-makers to understand, anticipate, constrain, intervene in and reassess the critical use of force.**

This is the central distinction separating Substantive II from Substantive I: **Substantive I asks where responsibility resides; Substantive II asks what must remain within human judgment and control for that responsibility to remain meaningful in the first place.**
